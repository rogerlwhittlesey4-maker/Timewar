⚜ The Name and Its Origin

The surname Whittlesey derives from the small market-town of Whittlesey in Cambridgeshire, England, on the southern edge of the Isle of Ely. Whittlesey itself takes its name from Old English Witel's eg — "Witel's island" — referring to a slight rise of dry land in the great fenland marsh that surrounded it. As a hereditary surname, "Whittlesey" (with many spelling variants — Whittelsey, Whittelse, Whittlesea, Wittelsea, Witsley) appears in Cambridgeshire records from the 13th century onward.

The Whittlesey family was established in Cambridgeshire for generations before written records clearly document individual members. By the 14th century the name was prominent enough to produce the most distinguished bearer in the family's English history — William de Whittlesey, Archbishop of Canterbury. ✠ William de Whittlesey — Archbishop of Canterbury (d. 1374)

Documented Facts at a Glance

William de Whittlesey (also spelled Whittlesea, Wittlesey, Wytlesey, and in the Latin records of his own day variously as Wittleseye or Wytleseye) was the fifty-seventh Archbishop of Canterbury, holding the primacy of all England from 11 October 1368 until his death on the night of 5/6 June 1374 at the archbishop's manor of Lambeth (Wikipedia; Dictionary of National Biography 1885-1900; 1898 Whittelsey Genealogy). The contemporary record permits a relatively secure outline of his career, though the personal materials are thin and the date of his birth remains undocumented.

The principal documented facts, drawn from the Dictionary of National Biography article by R. L. Poole (DNB, vol. 61, 1900), the Wikipedia article that summarises it, the catalog entries of the Catholic-Hierarchy database compiled from papal records, the 1898 Genealogy of the Whittelsey-Whittlesey Family by Charles Barney Whittelsey, the Fasti Ecclesiae Anglicanae 1300-1541 on British History Online, and the Worcester episcopal register known as Reg. Wittlesey, are these:

  • Born, almost certainly in the first decade of the fourteenth century, probably in the Cambridgeshire fenland town of Whittlesey from which he took his surname (Wikipedia; 1898 Whittelsey Genealogy). No baptismal record survives. He was old enough to be made archdeacon by 1337, which by the canon law of the day required him to be at least twenty-five years old, placing his birth not later than 1312 and probably several years earlier.
  • Maternal nephew of Simon Islip (c. 1300-1366), Archbishop of Canterbury 1349-1366 (DNB, Islip, Simon; Wikipedia). The 1898 Genealogy and the DNB agree that the kinship was a maternal one and that "to him Whittlesey owed his education and much ecclesiastical promotion."
  • Educated at the University of Oxford, where he took the doctorate of both canon and civil law - the utriusque iuris doctor qualification typical of the senior English ecclesiastical bureaucracy of the period (DNB; Wikipedia).
  • Collated Archdeacon of Huntingdon in June 1337, reappointed by royal letters patent on 20 June 1343 (DNB). He held this archdeaconry, in the diocese of Lincoln, for over twenty years.
  • Made custos (Master) of Peterhouse, Cambridge, on 10 September 1349, in the immediate aftermath of his uncle's elevation to the archbishopric of Canterbury; he resigned this office in 1351 (DNB; Peterhouse College history; Wikipedia List of masters of Peterhouse, Cambridge).
  • Prebendary of Lichfield from 1350; prebendary of Chichester and of Lincoln from 1356; held livings (parish benefices) at Ivychurch in Kent, at Croydon in Surrey, and at Cliffe in Kent (DNB; Wikipedia).
  • The 1898 Whittelsey Genealogy also assigns to him the office of Master of the Order of Sempringham (the English Gilbertine order), though this claim is not corroborated in the DNB or in the modern lists of Gilbertine masters and should be treated with caution.
  • Elected to the see of Rochester on 23 October 1360, his uncle Islip having secured the agreement of the cathedral priory (DNB). The papal provision was issued by Pope Innocent VI on 31 July 1361 (Catholic-Hierarchy). Consecrated as Bishop of Rochester on 6 February 1362 in the chapel of the archbishop's manor-house at Otford, Kent, by his uncle Simon Islip - privately, in the presence of no other diocesan bishop, owing to Islip's growing infirmity (DNB).
  • Translated to the see of Worcester on 6 March 1364 by papal bull of Urban V; enthroned at Worcester on 11 May 1364 (Reg. Wittlesey fol. 9; Fasti Ecclesiae Anglicanae, vol. IV; Catholic-Hierarchy).
  • Translated to the archbishopric of Canterbury on 11 October 1368 in succession to Simon Langham, who had resigned to take a cardinal's hat at the papal curia in Avignon (DNB; Wikipedia; Catholic Encyclopedia).
  • Died at Lambeth Palace on the night of 5/6 June 1374. Buried in the nave of Canterbury Cathedral, between two pillars on the south side of the nave, near the tomb of his uncle Simon Islip (DNB; Burial-Places of the Archbishops of Canterbury in Archaeologia Cantiana; British History Online - Survey of Kent, vol. 11; vol. 12 The archbishops: Walter Reynolds to William Whittesley).
  • A sapphire ring associated with him survives in the Victoria and Albert Museum, London (V&A inv. M.191-1975; V&A William Wytlesey's Ring).
  • Succeeded as Archbishop of Canterbury by Simon Sudbury (Bishop of London), papally provided in 1375 (Wikipedia Simon Sudbury).

These constitute the documentary skeleton on which all serious modern accounts of William de Whittlesey are built. Everything else, including the precise year and month of his birth, the names of his parents, the place and details of his early schooling before Oxford, and the texture of his personality, is either undocumented or known only from sources later than his lifetime.

The Whittlesey of His Birth

Whittlesey lies in the Cambridgeshire fenland, in what was historically the North Witchford hundred of the county and which formed part of the great ecclesiastical liberty known as the Isle of Ely. The Isle, although now thoroughly drained farmland, was in the fourteenth century quite literally an island: a low ridge of dry gravel and clay surrounded by the marshes of the Great Fen. The medieval traveller approached Whittlesey by causeway from Peterborough to the west, by boat across the meres from Ely to the east, or by a similar route from Crowland in Lincolnshire to the north (British History Online - VCH Cambridgeshire, vol. 4, "Whittlesey"). Before the seventeenth-century drainage works of Cornelius Vermuyden, the town was effectively a fen-island bordered by Whittlesey Mere - until its destruction one of the largest sheets of freshwater in lowland England.

The town's name appears in the Cartularium Saxonicum of 973 as Witlesig; in Domesday Book of 1086 as Witesie; and in the twelfth-century Inquisitio Eliensis in similar form (Wikipedia Whittlesey). The element "-ey" or "-ig" is Old English for "island", carrying the same sense as in nearby Ely, Manea, and Thorney. The patronymic "Witel" was a personal name. By the fourteenth century the township was substantial enough to support two ancient parishes, St Mary's and St Andrew's, each with a parish church of its own, and the manors of Whittlesey were divided between the Abbey of Thorney and the Bishop of Ely - divided lordship which, the Victoria County History suggests, may explain why so prosperous a market town never attained corporate borough status (VCH Cambridgeshire, vol. 4).

In the period of William's youth, the parish church of St Mary's was being extended and the nave lengthened by one bay, with the addition of a wide south aisle, a south chapel, and the south porch - work usually dated stylistically to the first half of the fourteenth century (VCH Cambridgeshire; British-towns.net "Whittlesey"). The other parish church, St Andrew's, was likewise prospering. The fenland economy that paid for these works was a peculiar one: peat-cutting for fuel, reed-cutting for thatch, fowling in the meres, eel-fishing on a massive scale (the abbots of Ely's eel-rents were paid in tens of thousands of eels per year), summer pasture on the marsh fringe, and arable cropping on the better-drained gravels. The medieval Whittlesey villager was at once farmer, fisherman, and fowler, and the town's prosperity through the fourteenth century is a documented fact (VCH Cambridgeshire).

It is conventional in older biographical writing to assume that the future archbishop took his surname from the place of his birth, and this is overwhelmingly the most likely explanation. The form "de Whittlesey" is precisely that of a fourteenth-century clerical toponymic byname - the standard medieval way of distinguishing one William from another by reference to his birthplace. The 1898 Genealogy of the Whittelsey-Whittlesey Family compiled by Charles Barney Whittelsey is explicit on the point: William "took his name from his native town of Whittlesey in Cambridgeshire" (1898 Genealogy, p. 18). It is, however, worth conceding that no document of his lifetime explicitly names the place of his birth; the inference rests on his surname and on later antiquarian tradition.

Family and Patronage

The single most important fact of William de Whittlesey's career, and the one fact about his family that is firmly documented, is that he was the nephew of Simon Islip, Archbishop of Canterbury from 1349 to 1366. The DNB entry on Islip is explicit that "Whittlesey was a sister's son of Islip"; the same DNB entry on William Whittlesey, written by R. L. Poole, says without qualification that "to him Whittlesey owed his education and much ecclesiastical promotion." The 1898 Whittelsey Genealogy, drawing on antiquarian sources of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, says the same thing in nearly the same words: "his maternal uncle Simon Islip, Archbishop of Canterbury, was the source of his education and of all his early advancement in the Church."

Simon Islip himself was a man of obscure birth. The DNB and Wikipedia agree that he took his surname from his native village of Islip in Oxfordshire, the birthplace of Edward the Confessor and a settlement which sat at the junction of the Cherwell and the Ray, north of Oxford. Islip was a cousin of Walter de Islip, Chief Baron of the Irish Exchequer under Edward II (DNB Islip, Simon); the family was therefore minor administrative gentry of the sort that supplied the clerks and judges of the fourteenth-century English crown. Islip was an Oxford-trained doctor of both laws, served as Vicar-General to the Bishop of Lincoln, then as Dean of the Arches and clerk in the household of Edward III, was Lord Privy Seal in 1347-1350, and was elected Archbishop of Canterbury by papal provision in September 1349, in succession to John de Ufford and Thomas Bradwardine, both of whom had died of the Black Death within months of taking office.

What this means in practical terms for William de Whittlesey is that he came of age in a moment when his maternal uncle was the rising star of the royal administration and was about to become the senior churchman of England. The collation of William to the Archdeaconry of Huntingdon in June 1337 - when Islip was still only a clerk-of-the-household and Vicar-General of Lincoln - is the earliest indication of family patronage at work, since the archdeaconry of Huntingdon lay within the diocese of Lincoln, where Islip's connections were strongest. The subsequent royal confirmation of the post by letters patent in 1343 indicates that the king's own administration recognised William as a young man of family connection worth protecting. By 1349, when Islip became archbishop, the chain of preferments - Peterhouse, Lichfield, Chichester, Lincoln, Ivychurch, Croydon, Cliffe - followed promptly.

Of William's parents nothing certain is recorded. The 1898 Whittelsey Genealogy, written by an American descendant working backwards through the seventeenth-century English line, speculates about his parents but does not name them with documentary support. Older antiquarians were content simply to record his Whittlesey origin and his Islip kinship and let the matter rest. The records of the Islip family are scarcely better. We do not know with certainty the name of Simon Islip's sister who was William's mother, and the absence is significant: it means that all attempts to attach William de Whittlesey to a continuous documented Whittlesey lineage of subsequent centuries are speculative.

The pattern of his promotion is, however, a classic fourteenth-century one. Nepotism in the proper Latin sense - the elevation of nepotes, nephews - was not yet stigmatised. It was the recognised mode of building an ecclesiastical career. A great prelate or curial cardinal was expected to provide for his family. The papal court at Avignon was openly so structured, and the English episcopate of the mid-fourteenth century was honeycombed with relatives of earlier prelates. The DNB observes drily that Whittlesey's promotions were "owed principally to the fact that he was a nephew of Simon Islip." This is the verdict of every modern historian who has touched the subject.

Early Ecclesiastical Career

The first office in William de Whittlesey's career which is firmly dated is the Archdeaconry of Huntingdon, to which he was collated in June 1337. An archdeacon in the fourteenth-century English Church was a senior dignitary subordinate to the diocesan bishop, with disciplinary and visitatorial jurisdiction over the clergy and laity of his archdeaconry. The diocese of Lincoln, then the largest in England, was divided into eight archdeaconries (Lincoln, Stow, Leicester, Northampton, Oxford, Buckingham, Bedford, and Huntingdon), and Huntingdon was a substantial one, taking in the modern counties of Huntingdonshire, Hertfordshire, and parts of southern Cambridgeshire. It was an income-producing dignity (with court fees, procurations, and prebendal rents) and one routinely held by absentees and by young men of family while they did other things at court or at university.

The 20 June 1343 royal letters patent confirming the archdeaconry, recorded in the Calendar of Patent Rolls, indicate that William's position was contested or at least required royal reinforcement - possibly a sign that he was non-resident and had been the object of a complaint, possibly merely the routine confirmation that often followed a regnal change or a vacancy in the see. By the 1340s, William was certainly an Oxford man - the DNB and all secondary sources agree that he took his doctorate in canon and civil law at Oxford, although the year is not preserved. The doctorate would have required several years' study in the schools, very probably between about 1330 and the early 1340s, with all the residence and disputation requirements of the medieval university.

In September 1349 his uncle Simon Islip was provided to Canterbury, and the immediate effect on William's career was electric. On 10 September 1349 - within days, probably, of his uncle's first acts as archbishop - William was made custos of Peterhouse at Cambridge. Peterhouse, founded in 1284 by Hugh de Balsham, Bishop of Ely, was the oldest of the Cambridge colleges and the leading college of the Isle of Ely diocese. The post of custos (later more commonly called Master) carried with it both academic and administrative authority within the small foundation. William held it until 1351. Whether he was ever resident there is unclear; the position may have been a sinecure intended to give him a base in the diocese of Ely closest to his birthplace. Modern Peterhouse history (Peterhouse College, History of the Peterhouse Libraries) records that he later donated or bequeathed books to the college library, and he is one of the figures memorialised in the college's later heraldic record (The Heraldry Society, Peterhouse Cambridge).

Following his Peterhouse appointment, the prebends began to accumulate. A prebend in fourteenth-century English usage was a share of the endowed income of a cathedral chapter, attaching to a stall in the cathedral choir but not requiring residence; it was the standard form of clerical revenue. William held the prebend of Lichfield from 1350, and prebends of both Chichester and Lincoln from 1356 (DNB). Each carried an annual stipend without imposing duties incompatible with his concurrent service. To these were added the parish livings of Ivychurch (a marshland parish in Romney Marsh, Kent, which was lucrative because of its sheep-pasture tithes), Croydon (the archbishop's own seat in Surrey, then a parish under direct archiepiscopal patronage), and Cliffe-at-Hoo in north Kent. The cumulation of livings - what later canonists would condemn as pluralism - was at the time accepted on dispensation, and was conventional for a kinsman of a primate.

The 1898 Whittelsey Genealogy adds that William also held the office of Master of the Order of Sempringham (the English Gilbertine order, with its mother-house at Sempringham in Lincolnshire). No corroboration of this office is provided in the standard biographical reference works - the DNB, the Fasti, or the Catholic-Hierarchy database - and the named Masters of the Gilbertine order at the appropriate dates do not include him. The claim should be treated as uncertain and may reflect either a confusion with another office or an antiquarian tradition not now verifiable. (It is possible that William held some non-master office in connection with a Gilbertine house - patron-protector, for instance, or visitatorial commissary on behalf of the archbishop - and that this evolved in family memory into the claim that he was "Master".)

A further notice in the Peterhouse manuscript records describes William of Whittlesey, "archdeacon of Ely", as donor of a manuscript shortly before 1369 (University of Cambridge exhibitions, The Moving Word - Peterhouse MS 11). If this is the same man - and the dating and the Ely connection make it overwhelmingly probable that it is - then he must briefly have held the archdeaconry of Ely as well. (The archdeaconry of Ely was conventionally a single office covering the whole diocese of Ely, which was small.) This would have been after he ceased to be archdeacon of Huntingdon, but before he became Bishop of Rochester. It fits the chronology and is the sort of additional dignity an active clerk of his standing would have held.

By 1360, then, William de Whittlesey was a well-established senior pluralist clerk of the English Church, in his fifties (assuming a birth date around 1305), bearing a doctorate in both laws, an archdeaconry, two or three prebends, three parish livings, and his uncle's confidence. He was positioned for the bench of bishops.

Bishop of Rochester (1362-1364)

Rochester was the smallest, oldest, and poorest of the English dioceses. Founded by St Justus in 604 as the second see established by St Augustine's mission to the English (Canterbury being the first), it consisted essentially of north-west Kent - the country between London and Canterbury - and its modest cathedral was geographically and ecclesiastically tied to Canterbury, the bishop of Rochester being a suffragan of the archbishop in a peculiarly close way. As the see of Rochester was the senior suffragan of the Canterbury province, its bishop traditionally consecrated newly elected archbishops of Canterbury and crowned the kings and queens of England by virtue of the archbishop's commission. By the mid-fourteenth century, Rochester was conventionally a stepping-stone diocese: a younger or junior bishop's first see, very poor in temporalities, frequently held briefly before translation to a richer bishopric.

William de Whittlesey's election to Rochester is recorded as taking place on 23 October 1360, with the active intervention of his uncle Archbishop Islip negotiating with the monks of the cathedral priory of St Andrew at Rochester (DNB; Catholic-Hierarchy). The election was confirmed by papal provision of Pope Innocent VI on 31 July 1361 - the gap between election and provision being normal for the period, when business between Canterbury and the curia at Avignon was conducted by slow courier, and elections were customarily ratified rather than initiated at Rome.

His consecration as Bishop of Rochester took place on 6 February 1362. The DNB records the unusual circumstance that the ceremony was held privately in the chapel of the archbishop's manor at Otford in Kent - Otford being the most favoured of the country residences of the Canterbury archbishops, a substantial moated palace whose ruins still stand - and that the consecration was performed by Archbishop Islip alone, with no other diocesan bishop present. The reason for this irregularity, the DNB explains, was the deteriorating health of Islip himself, who had suffered the stroke that would eventually leave him speechless and from which he never fully recovered. Ordinarily an episcopal consecration required at least three consecrating bishops; Islip, conscious of his own incapacity and unwilling to delay, performed the rite on his own authority as metropolitan. The ceremony seems to have been brief and private, almost domestic, in keeping with the personal nature of the patronage that had elevated William to his see.

The tenure was short and seems to have been administratively quiet. The diocese of Rochester was in good order, and the bishop's principal duties were the cycle of ordinations, parish visitations, and confirmations characteristic of a fourteenth-century English bishop. The recurrence of plague in 1361-62 - the second great mortality, less severe than the catastrophe of 1348-49 but still very damaging, with the death-rate higher among children and the young - would have given him the immediate task of refilling vacancies in his clergy as best he could. Surviving references to Reg. Whittlesey (the Rochester portion of his register, where preserved) record routine institutions and the standard work of a working bishop. Nothing in the surviving record suggests that William played any conspicuous national role during his Rochester years; his uncle the archbishop, although crippled by illness, was still notionally exercising the primacy, and William's role was that of dutiful suffragan.

Bishop of Worcester (1364-1368)

On 6 March 1364 William was translated by papal bull of Urban V from Rochester to the much wealthier and more important see of Worcester. The translation was secured, again, through the intervention of Archbishop Islip, who though ailing remained metropolitan. Worcester was one of the larger and richer of the English dioceses, covering the modern counties of Worcestershire, the greater part of Warwickshire, and parts of Gloucestershire (including the western half of the Cotswolds). Its cathedral, refounded as a Benedictine monastic church in the late tenth century by St Oswald and rebuilt in the years after the Conquest, was one of the great Romanesque cathedrals of England, with the body of St Wulfstan (canonised in 1203) one of the major pilgrimage attractions of the West Midlands. The temporalities of the see were substantial, including the bishop's town of Worcester itself, the manors of Hartlebury, Kempsey, Hanbury, Henbury, and others, and a great deal of land in the upper Severn valley.

William was enthroned at Worcester on 11 May 1364. The reference in Reg. Wittlesey fol. 9 - "Reg. Wittlesey" being the Worcester register kept in his name and surviving in the diocesan archives - is the principal contemporary documentation of the act (cited in the Fasti Ecclesiae Anglicanae 1300-1541, vol. IV, Monastic Cathedrals: Worcester, available on British History Online). The register is the standard administrative record of his episcopate at Worcester: ordinations, institutions of clergy to benefices, dispensations granted, visitations of religious houses, sentences in consistory court, licences for non-residence, and the like.

His four years at Worcester were the most administratively active phase of his career. He was now a vigorous middle-aged bishop in a wealthy see, no longer overshadowed by his uncle (who died on 26 April 1366 at Mayfield in Sussex, paralysed and speechless from his stroke), and was beginning to act in his own right at the level of national church politics. Convocation, the assembly of the English clergy of the southern province, met in his time; the king and council called on him for advice on ecclesiastical matters. The Worcester register documents his routine pastoral work, including ordinations of priests, deacons, and subdeacons in numbers consistent with a diocese still recovering its clerical numbers after the staggering mortality of 1348-49 and 1361-62.

The death of his uncle Islip in April 1366 produced a temporary vacancy at Canterbury filled by the election of Simon Langham (Abbot of Westminster and Chancellor of England), provided by Pope Urban V on 24 July 1366. Langham's tenure at Canterbury was short and unsettled; in 1368 he accepted appointment as Cardinal Priest of San Sisto from Urban V without seeking the king's permission, an act which forced his resignation of the archbishopric. (The king, Edward III, then aging and increasingly suspicious of papal manoeuvres in the long aftermath of the Statute of Provisors of 1351 and the Statute of Praemunire of 1353, would not tolerate the loss of an archbishop to the curia at Avignon.) The vacancy at Canterbury thereby created in the autumn of 1368 was the occasion for William de Whittlesey's elevation.

Archbishop of Canterbury (1368-1374)

The papal bull translating William de Whittlesey from Worcester to Canterbury is dated 11 October 1368. The DNB explains the choice candidly: Langham's loss had been a political embarrassment, and "a more colourless and pliant primate being desired, the choice fell upon Whittlesey, who was accordingly translated to Canterbury by a papal bull." This is the verdict of the modern record on the politics of his appointment. He was now a man in his sixties, an experienced bishop and competent canonist, of well-attested family loyalty to the Plantagenet crown (his uncle had been Islip the king's clerk), and apparently disinclined to make trouble. He represented, in fourteenth-century terms, safe hands.

The years of his primacy - from late 1368 to mid-1374 - fall in the closing phase of the long reign of Edward III, who was now in his late fifties and increasingly enfeebled and dominated by his mistress Alice Perrers and by his ambitious third son John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster. The Hundred Years War, after the brilliant English successes of Crecy (1346), Poitiers (1356), and the Treaty of Bretigny (1360), had turned sour. From 1369 the war was resumed at French initiative; the French king Charles V and his constable Bertrand du Guesclin reconquered most of the English gains by means of patient siege and skirmish; the great chevauchee of John of Gaunt across France in 1373 achieved nothing; and the heir to the English throne, Edward of Woodstock the Black Prince, returned from Aquitaine in 1371 a sick man, ruined in body by the dysentery contracted in the Castilian campaign of 1367. The kingdom was sliding into the crisis that would explode in the Good Parliament of 1376 - but Whittlesey did not live to see that crisis, dying two years before it.

The DNB summarises the primacy as "very uneventful, a circumstance due partly, but not wholly, to his feeble health." This is a fair assessment. Whittlesey is mentioned in the chronicles of his time chiefly in passing; he held convocations as required, conducted the routine governance of the southern province, and presided over the cycle of ordinations, institutions, and visitations characteristic of a working metropolitan. He kept his register, preserved as Reg. Whittlesey at Lambeth Palace Library, which is the principal documentary witness to the administrative acts of his archbishopric.

One incident is well documented: the heavy taxation pressure on the English clergy in 1373, in support of John of Gaunt's expensive and unsuccessful military campaigning in France, brought Whittlesey to London - exhausted and ill - for the December 1373 convocation, where the clergy reluctantly granted a subsidy. The DNB observes that "due to heavy pressure of taxation upon the clergy, Whittlesey dragged himself up to London for the meeting of convocation in December 1373." This is the principal recorded political act of his archbishopric.

A more famous anecdote - that the Black Prince called Whittlesey "an ass" in a great council of prelates and lords called after Pentecost (20 May 1374), reacting to Whittlesey's refusal to support a papal demand for a subsidy against the Florentines - was reported by the chronicler Thomas Walsingham of St Albans. The DNB observes that this story "is disposed of by the fact that he was on his deathbed at Lambeth when the scene is supposed to have taken place at Westminster." Whittlesey was too ill to attend the council, and the rebuke, if it occurred, was directed at someone else or was a chronicler's later invention. The story is nevertheless useful in indicating how Whittlesey was perceived in the immediately following decades: as a passive, somewhat ineffectual figure whose role in great affairs was at best secondary.

The political context, in which Whittlesey's passivity must be read, was one in which the English church was caught between two enormous pressures. From above, the papacy at Avignon - under Urban V (1362-70) and Gregory XI (1370-78), both French and politically aligned to a France at war with England - continued the older practice of provisions, demanded large subsidies, and used English benefices to provide for cardinals and Italian curialists. From below, the English crown and parliament, deeply hostile to papal taxation of an enemy nation's clergy, were pressing the Statutes of Provisors (1351) and Praemunire (1353) ever more aggressively. The negotiations at Bruges in 1374-76, in which both papal nuncios and the future Lollard heresiarch John Wycliffe took part on the English side, were the diplomatic expression of this conflict. Whittlesey, as primate, would have been formally responsible for steering English ecclesiastical policy through these dangerous waters; in practice the policy was made by John of Gaunt and his clerical clients, including the rising William Courtenay (Bishop of Hereford, then of London), who would eventually succeed Sudbury at Canterbury in 1381 after the Peasants' Revolt.

It was during Whittlesey's primacy that John Wycliffe began to be a public figure in the English Church. Wycliffe had emerged from Merton College, Oxford, as a leading academic theologian and philosopher; in 1374 he was appointed by royal commission to the Bruges embassy. His full attack on the papacy, on the doctrine of transubstantiation, and on ecclesiastical wealth still lay in the future (the developed Wycliffite positions belong to 1377-84), but his earlier writings on dominion and on the relationship between the temporal and spiritual powers were already in circulation. Whittlesey is not recorded as having taken any decisive action on Wycliffe; the formal proceedings against him were begun by his successor Sudbury in 1377, after Whittlesey's death.

In Whittlesey's own diocese, the work of his archiepiscopate was conventional. His register at Lambeth records ordinations, the institution of incumbents to vacant benefices, the issue of dispensations, the granting of probates of wills (the prerogative court of Canterbury, which Whittlesey controlled, had jurisdiction over wills of testators with property in more than one diocese, and the volume of business was already substantial). He visited religious houses in the province. He probably resided much of the time at Lambeth (the archbishop's London residence on the south bank of the Thames opposite Westminster), at Otford and at Maidstone (his Kentish manors), and at Canterbury itself when business required. By 1372-73 his health was visibly failing, and the chronicles after that point speak of him as a sick man.

The construction of the new Perpendicular nave of Canterbury Cathedral, sometimes associated in popular accounts with Whittlesey, was in fact not begun until under his successor Simon Sudbury, who paid for the demolition of the old Lanfrancian nave in 1378 and under whom the new work of Henry Yevele was commenced. The project had been mooted before 1370 by Prior Hathbrand, who opened a subscription list, and Whittlesey would certainly have known of and encouraged the plan; but the visible reconstruction is Sudbury's, not Whittlesey's (Canterbury Cathedral architectural history; Canterbury Historical and Archaeological Society, History & Architecture of the Nave). The cathedral nave of Whittlesey's day was still the Romanesque structure raised by Archbishop Lanfranc in the 1070s on the model of the Abbey of Saint-Etienne at Caen.

The England of His Primacy

To understand the period of William de Whittlesey's primacy is to grasp the long shadow of the Black Death. The first and most devastating outbreak of the bubonic and pneumonic plague reached England in the summer of 1348 and ravaged the country through 1349, killing somewhere between forty and sixty percent of the entire population - perhaps three million people out of a pre-plague figure of around six million (Wikipedia Black Death in England; Brewminate). The clergy were among the hardest hit, with mortality rates as high as forty-eight percent in some archdeaconries and fifty-five percent in cities such as Bristol. Two successive archbishops of Canterbury (John de Ufford and Thomas Bradwardine) died of plague in 1349 before being consecrated or shortly after. The recurrence of 1361-62, less severe but still grievous (perhaps twenty percent of the population), came during William de Whittlesey's Rochester episcopate. Another recurrence in 1369 came in the first year of his archbishopric. By 1374 the English population was perhaps half of what it had been when William was born.

The social consequences were immense. The shortage of labour after 1349 produced both rising real wages for the surviving peasantry and an attempt by the crown and lords, embodied in the Ordinance of Labourers (1349) and the Statute of Labourers (1351), to hold wages and prices at pre-plague levels. The tension between this legislation and the new realities of the labour market would explode in the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, but the pressure was building throughout Whittlesey's primacy. The clergy, themselves heavily depleted, had to be replenished by ordinations of poorly trained men, and the educational standard of the ordinary parish priest declined visibly in the second half of the fourteenth century - a fact noted with disgust by Wycliffe and by the Lollards a generation later, but already a commonplace of episcopal visitation records by the 1360s.

Politically, the period was dominated by the long war with France. The first phase of the Hundred Years War, opened by Edward III in 1337 (the year of William de Whittlesey's first archdeaconry), had produced the great English victories of Sluys (1340), Crecy (1346), Calais (1347), and Poitiers (1356), and was concluded by the Treaty of Bretigny (1360), which gave Edward sovereignty over an enlarged Gascony and the surrender of his claim to the French crown. The truce did not last. From 1369 the war was renewed, with Edward reasserting his claim and Charles V of France conducting a much more patient and successful war of attrition under the constable Bertrand du Guesclin. By the time of Whittlesey's death in 1374, the English territorial position in France had been largely reduced again to the Calais Pale and a coastal strip in south-west Gascony. The financial cost of the war was enormous, and the demands made on the English Church - through papal subsidies, parliamentary subsidies, clerical convocational grants, and direct loans - were the constant background to ecclesiastical politics.

The reign of Edward III had begun in 1327 with the deposition of Edward II and the regency of Isabella and Mortimer; it concluded in 1377 with the king dying half-senile, his great son the Black Prince already dead (1376), and the throne passing to his young grandson Richard II, then a boy of ten. Whittlesey served the king through the closing decade of the reign, watching the decline of the once-glorious Plantagenet warrior into a feeble and morally compromised old man dominated by Alice Perrers. The Good Parliament of 1376 - the great political reckoning of the reign, in which the Commons impeached the king's ministers and Alice Perrers was banished - would not occur until two years after Whittlesey's death; but the political conditions that produced it were ripening throughout his primacy.

In religion, the most important development of the period was the slow drift of academic Oxford theology toward what would become the Wycliffite or Lollard movement. John Wycliffe (c. 1328-1384) was during Whittlesey's primacy a respected academic theologian at Merton, Balliol, and Queen's, Oxford; his more radical positions on dominion, ecclesiastical wealth, and papal authority were still being developed; his condemnation by Archbishop Courtenay at the Blackfriars Synod of 1382 was eight years in the future. But the intellectual ground was shifting, and the Statute of Provisors and the Statute of Praemunire - both enacted under Edward III in 1351 and 1353 respectively - were the political expression of an English ecclesiastical nationalism that fed directly into Wycliffe's later attacks on papal claims. Whittlesey, doctor of both laws and conventional canonist, lived through these developments as a senior bishop without contributing to or resisting them in any conspicuous way; his health and his temperament alike inclined him to keep his head down.

The cathedral chapter at Canterbury was, throughout Whittlesey's primacy, the Benedictine cathedral priory of Christ Church Canterbury, with its prior and convent of monks. The shrine of Thomas Becket, behind the high altar at the eastern end of the cathedral, remained the leading pilgrimage destination of England; the four-hundredth anniversary of Becket's translation had been celebrated in 1370, two years into Whittlesey's primacy. Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1340-1400), then a junior royal servant and customs official, was writing the first of his works during this period; he would not begin the Canterbury Tales until the 1380s, after Whittlesey's death, but the world of pilgrim Canterbury that Chaucer would describe was the world over which Whittlesey presided as archbishop.

Death and Burial

William de Whittlesey died at the archbishop's London residence of Lambeth Palace on the night of 5/6 June 1374. The DNB and the Worcester register agree on the date; the precise hour is uncertain and some authorities give 5 June, others 6 June, the difference being whether the death is reckoned in canonical time (with the new day beginning at sundown) or civil time (with the new day at midnight). The cause of death is not recorded in the chronicles, but his protracted illness over the preceding three years - first noted in 1371-72 - strongly suggests a chronic wasting disease rather than an acute illness. Several modern accounts speak of him as paralytic in his last months; whether this is documented or simply assumed from his uncle's example is not clear.

His body was taken from Lambeth to Canterbury - a journey of about sixty miles via Dartford, Rochester, and Faversham, which would have taken several days in the slow processional manner appropriate to an archiepiscopal funeral - and buried in the cathedral. The chosen site was in the nave, on the south side, between two pillars of the south arcade, near the burial place of his uncle Simon Islip, who had been buried "before the rood" (the great cross above the entrance to the choir) in 1366. The choice of burial near his uncle is biographically significant: William chose to be remembered in death as he had been formed in life, as Islip's nephew and beneficiary.

The tomb itself, as described by William Somner in The Antiquities of Canterbury (1640) and earlier by Francis Godwin (writing about 1586) and John Weever in his Antient Funerall Monuments (1631), was "a large handsome raised tomb of Petworth marble, richly inlaid with brasses." Petworth marble - sometimes also called Sussex marble or Bethersden marble after similar quarries in Kent - is a dark fossiliferous limestone polished to a deep grey-green sheen, much used in fourteenth-century tomb work. The brass inlay would have included a figure of the archbishop in pontifical vestments, his crozier and mitre, and a marginal inscription in Latin Gothic letters. The brasses were already much wasted by Weever's time and were entirely gone by the eighteenth century; the tomb itself was destroyed or removed during the eighteenth- or nineteenth-century alterations to the cathedral nave, though some fragmentary indications of its former position survive (British History Online - Survey of Kent, vol. 11, Monuments in the Cathedral; Kent Archaeological Society, Burial-Places of the Archbishops of Canterbury).

The epitaph, partly preserved in Godwin's reading of 1586, was conventional, naming William as archbishop and giving the date of his death. Weever in 1631 was able to record only a fragment.

A sapphire ring, said by tradition to have been taken from his tomb when it was opened at some point in the seventeenth or eighteenth century, survives in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London under accession number M.191-1975, catalogued as "William Wytlesey's ring." The ring is set with a pale blue sapphire of medieval cut, in a gold hoop with the modest workmanship characteristic of bishops' rings of the period. Sapphire was the conventional stone for an episcopal ring - its blue colour was held to symbolise heavenly contemplation - and the ring was presented at consecration as a sign of the bishop's mystical marriage to his diocese. Whether the V&A ring is in fact Whittlesey's is impossible to verify with certainty, but the provenance is plausible.

He was succeeded as Archbishop of Canterbury by Simon Sudbury, Bishop of London, who was provided by Pope Gregory XI in 1375 and translated to Canterbury formally on 4 May 1375. Sudbury - a forceful and politically engaged primate of a wholly different character - would be murdered by the rebels of the Peasants' Revolt on Tower Hill on 14 June 1381, less than seven years after Whittlesey's death.

Whittlesey left no recorded close family. The 1898 Whittelsey Genealogy attempts to attach later Whittlesey lines to him, but as a senior celibate clerk he had no acknowledged descendants. The Whittlesey line of Cambridgeshire which continued through the fifteenth, sixteenth, and seventeenth centuries - and which produced the seventeenth-century emigrant John Whittlesey of Saybrook, Connecticut - descended from collateral kinsmen of the archbishop, not from the archbishop himself. The genealogical link between William de Whittlesey and the documented Whittlesey lines of the Tudor and Stuart periods is asserted in family tradition but not securely established in surviving records. It is, however, very probable on geographical and onomastic grounds that the later Whittleseys of Cambridgeshire were of the same broader fenland family as the archbishop.

⚜ Ralph Whittlesey · 1508-1540

Documented Facts at a Glance

  • Baptized: March 11, 1508, in Cambridgeshire, England, as "Ralph Whittelse" — the spelling preserved in the parochial record extracted by Charles B. Whittelsey for his 1898 Genealogy of the Whittelsey-Whittlesey Family.
  • Approximate lifespan: c. 1508 to c. 1540 (about thirty-two years), spanning the final years of Henry VII and the first two-thirds of the reign of Henry VIII.
  • Locale: Cambridgeshire, almost certainly within the parishes of Whittlesey St. Mary or Whittlesey St. Andrew on the southern edge of the Isle of Ely. The town was the ancestral seat from which the surname is derived, and the 1898 genealogy treats this cluster of early sixteenth-century Cambridgeshire baptisms as the immediate ancestors of the later, better-documented English Whittleseys.
  • Surname spelling: Recorded as "Whittelse." The spelling was wholly unstable in this era; contemporary documents elsewhere yield Whittlesea, Whittelsey, Whyttlese, Witsley, and Wittelsea. Most scribes wrote phonetically.
  • Documentation NOT surviving: No parish-register entry of his marriage, occupation, children, will, inventory, manorial admission, lay-subsidy payment, or burial has been positively identified in any publicly accessible record. The 1898 genealogy lists him as one of the earliest baptismal entries it could recover; everything else about his life must be reconstructed from the historical setting of his parish, county, and class.

What follows is therefore a biography of a place and a time: the Cambridgeshire fenland community into which Ralph Whittelse was born and in which, on every reasonable inference, he lived out his short adult life.

The Cambridgeshire of His Time

Ralph was baptized into a county that had not changed much since the Domesday survey of 1086. Cambridgeshire in 1508 was, outside the university town of Cambridge itself, a long landscape of clay uplands in the south and an immense waterlogged plain — the Fens — running north and west from Ely to the Wash. The population of the whole county at the start of the sixteenth century is estimated at well under 50,000 souls. Wisbech, Ely, and Cambridge were the only places that could properly be called towns. The rest of the shire was a thin stitching of nucleated villages and isolated farmsteads, separated by miles of plough-land, fen meadow, sedge, and standing water.

In the year Ralph was christened, Henry VII had just over a year to live; the king's death in April 1509 brought to the throne his seventeen-year-old son, Henry VIII. The transition was peaceful, and in Cambridgeshire the immediate change was barely felt. Tax assessments, manorial routines, parish observances, and the rhythm of agricultural work proceeded unchanged. The great upheavals that would mark Ralph's adult lifetime — the break with Rome (1534), the dissolution of the monasteries (1536-1540), the destruction of shrines and image-worship — all lay in the future and could not have been predicted by anyone in the household into which he was born.

The Cambridgeshire of Ralph's childhood was a Catholic landscape. Every parish church had its rood screen, its painted saints, its altar candles, its perpetual lamp; the cycle of the liturgical year structured time itself. Boys of Ralph's age would have been taken to Sunday Mass, attended the parish gild feast on the patronal day, watched the Easter sepulchre lit on Good Friday, and joined Rogationtide processions around the parish bounds. They learned the Pater Noster, Ave Maria, and Credo in Latin from their parents or the parish priest before they could read English.

The wider political world was distant and intermittent. Letters from Westminster reached Cambridgeshire by horsed messenger; royal proclamations were read aloud after Sunday service. Henry VIII's victorious French campaign of 1513, his sumptuous Field of the Cloth of Gold meeting with Francis I in 1520, his rising minister Cardinal Wolsey, his courtly diplomacy with Charles V — these were rumours in a Whittlesey alehouse, no more. Yet within Ralph's adult lifetime the royal will would reach the smallest fen-parish with sudden and irreversible force.

Whittlesey Parish and the Fens

The town of Whittlesey, from which the surname derives, sits about five miles east of Peterborough at the north-western edge of the Isle of Ely. In the early sixteenth century it was an island in the fullest sense: a low rise of gault clay and gravel surrounded on every side by undrained fen. To the north lay the brimming Whittlesey Mere — at 1,570 acres the second-largest natural lake in England before its drainage in 1851 — and beyond it the marshes of Borough Fen. To the south and east lay the great washes that flooded each winter into a sheet of brown water broken only by reed beds. Travel was by causeway, punt, or wherry. The Whittlesey Dyke, an old engineered watercourse, was the chief artery linking the town to Ramsey and the Nene.

The parish was divided ecclesiastically between two churches with two manors. Whittlesey St. Mary, the larger, was the manor of the Benedictine abbey of Thorney; Whittlesey St. Andrew, smaller and lying to the west, was the manor of the prior and convent of Ely. The townspeople were tenants of one or the other (occasionally both), with copyhold and customary holdings that descended by inheritance, with the lord's consent, from father to son. In 1563 — only twenty-odd years after Ralph's likely death — the ecclesiastical census recorded 355 householders in Whittlesey town, 266 of them in St. Mary's parish and 89 in St. Andrew's. Whittlesey was already the second-largest place in the Isle of Ely, after Ely itself. If we project the demography backwards (Fenland population is known to have grown across the sixteenth century, even before drainage), Ralph's Whittlesey of 1520-1540 probably held something like 250-300 households, or about 1,200-1,500 souls.

A man of Ralph's name and station would have known the great spire of St. Mary's church, even then probably the chief landmark of the western fens. The fifteenth-century tower of St. Mary's, rising on its perpendicular columns above the low brick and reed-thatched town, was admired by travellers for centuries afterwards. (Cole, visiting in 1745, thought it the finest parish-church spire in the Isle.) Inside, before the Reformation, statues stood in niches on the tower's exterior columns and the rood screen filled the chancel arch with painted figures of Christ, the Virgin, and St. John. St. Andrew's was smaller, plainer, and served the western "Ely end" of the town. Either church might have been Ralph's parish; the surviving parish registers of St. Mary's do not begin until 1560, two decades after his presumed death, and the St. Andrew's registers begin still later. His baptismal record, which the 1898 genealogist transcribed from "other Cambridgeshire records," must therefore have come from a bishop's transcript, an early manorial roll, or a now-vanished churchwarden's memorandum rather than from a parish register proper.

Economically, Whittlesey lived from the wet. Like all the fen settlements, it combined a fringe of cultivated dryland with extensive common rights in the surrounding marsh. The townspeople grew wheat, barley, oats, and beans on the high ground; they ran cattle and sheep on the summer fens when the floods withdrew; they cut peat (turbary) for fuel; they cut sedge and reed for thatch, fodder, and litter; they fished for eels, pike, perch, bream, and tench; they trapped wildfowl — duck, geese, swan, heron, even bittern and crane — in the great wash-meres. Eels were so abundant that they served as a unit of customary rent. The Thorney and Ely abbeys both drew substantial revenues from eel-fisheries and from the licensing of turbary rights. A typical Whittlesey householder of Ralph's day would have owned a punt, fowling-snares and nets (and likely a longbow), eel-glaives, a peat-spade, and a sickle, and would have moved through a seasonal round that began with spring sowing on the high ground, ran into summer hay-making in the fen meadows, peat-cutting and reed-gathering in late summer, harvest in September, fowling and fishing through autumn, and the winter floods.

Religious Context: The Last Catholic Generation

Ralph Whittelse was, in the proper meaning of the term, the last full generation of Cambridgeshire Catholics. He was baptized in 1508 according to the Sarum rite, anointed with chrism, given a saint's name (Ralph, anglicized from Radulphus, after the abbot-saint and the various Norman bishops who had carried it), and signed with the cross on his forehead. The household into which he was born would have observed the full Catholic calendar — Lent, Easter, Whitsun, Corpus Christi, Lammas, Michaelmas, All Saints — and observed it without thinking. The break with Rome that lay ahead was not yet conceivable.

The Isle of Ely's diocese was governed at his birth by Bishop James Stanley (1506-1515), then by Nicholas West (1515-1533), and from 1534 by Thomas Goodrich, the first Reformation bishop. The huge Norman cathedral at Ely, only ten miles south-east of Whittlesey across the fen, was still a Benedictine priory with the full medieval cult of St. Etheldreda. The shrine of St. Etheldreda — gold-plated, gem-encrusted, the object of long pilgrimage — drew offerings from across eastern England. Whittlesey villagers walked or punted the ten miles to Ely on the saint's feast (23 June), trading at the great fair that grew up around the shrine. Closer to home, the abbey of Thorney, six miles west, held a major relic-collection; the abbey of Ramsey, ten miles south-east, held the relics of St. Ivo. Ralph's mental geography was a network of saints, shrines, holy days, and habits of devotion that pre-dated the Norman Conquest.

Within his lifetime, almost all of this was destroyed.

The first signs came in the late 1520s, when Henry VIII began his pursuit of a divorce from Catherine of Aragon. To a Whittlesey ploughman the controversy seemed remote and abstract — court business, lawyers' work — but its consequences arrived with shocking speed. The Act of Supremacy in November 1534 declared the king supreme head of the Church of England. The Valor Ecclesiasticus of 1535 surveyed every monastic income in the kingdom. The First Suppression Act of 1536 dissolved the lesser houses; the Pilgrimage of Grace that autumn was put down with hangings. The Second Suppression Act of 1539 swept away the great abbeys. Thorney Abbey, the lord of Whittlesey St. Mary, surrendered to the king's commissioners on 1 December 1539. Ely Priory, the lord of Whittlesey St. Andrew, was dissolved on 18 November 1539. Within a single calendar year, both of the institutions that had ruled, taxed, judged, and prayed for the people of Whittlesey for five centuries were extinguished.

The Thorney monastery's books, plate, vestments, and lead were seized for the crown. The abbey church was partly demolished and its building stone hauled away for other works. (The site eventually passed in 1549/50 to John Russell, 1st Earl of Bedford, whose family would lead the great fen-drainage scheme of the next century.) Ely Priory was reconstituted as a secular cathedral chapter, retaining most of its physical fabric — the only fortunate survival in the region. The great shrine of St. Etheldreda, however, was dismantled in 1541 on the orders of Bishop Thomas Goodrich, who carried out the Royal Injunctions of that year "with great speed and decision" throughout the diocese. Images were taken down, lights extinguished, painted screens whitewashed.

At Whittlesey St. Mary, the statues that had stood in the niches of the perpendicular tower were thrown down; the rood-screen, with its painted figures, was demolished, though the heavy dado base survived (and survives still). The pre-Reformation devotional gilds — and Whittlesey had ten of them in St. Mary's parish alone, plus two in St. Andrew's — were stripped of their property. One had been "Thomas Becket's gild-house" in St. Mary's parish; Becket's name was specifically attacked by royal proclamation in 1538.

Ralph Whittelse, dying around 1540 at thirty-two, would have witnessed the first wave of this destruction but not the full Edwardian iconoclasm of 1547-1553. He was buried — in all likelihood — in a Whittlesey churchyard whose church was still half-Catholic in furniture but already legally Protestant in headship, in a transitional moment that few of his neighbours would have understood as a permanent rupture. His parents, godparents, and the priest who buried him were all of them men and women whose ordinary mental world had just been demolished.

Daily Life, Economy, and the Wider World

The English economy of the 1520s and 1530s in which Ralph reached adulthood was beginning to feel a series of pressures that would mark the entire Tudor century. The population was rising; agricultural prices were rising; wages were lagging; and many landlords were turning over arable land to sheep pasture for the booming wool trade. The Fens, however, ran on a different economic logic. Sheep grazed in the summer fens but could not be wintered there; the wash-fens were too wet for the heavy ploughing that fed the wool-cloth boom of the West Country and East Anglian uplands; and the commons remained protected, in 1540, by manorial custom and the still-living abbeys.

This is not to say that Whittlesey was insulated. The town lay close to two great east-west arteries — the River Nene (running through Peterborough to Wisbech and the Wash) and the line that would later become the Old Bedford River. Wool from upland Cambridgeshire and Northamptonshire was barged down through Whittlesey and Wisbech to the North Sea and Flanders. Salt came up from the Wash. Coal had begun to arrive by sea from Newcastle and was carried inland. Whittlesey's brick-making industry, on the gault clay of Kings Dyke, was already established and would expand sharply in the seventeenth century.

The 1523 Lay Subsidy, granted by Parliament in May of that year to fund Henry VIII's wars in France, swept every English householder with goods or land worth £1 or more into the assessment. Ralph, fifteen at the first assessment and reaching legal majority at the second, would have lived in a household whose head paid (or evaded) this tax; the Cambridgeshire returns survive in patches in The National Archives' E179 series. The subsidy was deeply unpopular and was followed in 1525 by the Amicable Grant, which provoked open rural resistance in Suffolk and Essex and forced the government to back down. The fens were not riotous in those years, but the memory of the new fiscal reach of the Tudor crown stayed.

A man like Ralph, born to whatever tenement his family held, would have followed the customary path of any fen-side cottager or husbandman. He would have inherited (or, more often, contracted by service) a copyhold of fifteen to thirty acres of arable strips in the open fields, a meadow share in the fen, and rights of common — turbary, piscary, pannage, herbage. He would have married, very likely in his mid-twenties, a local woman whose family held similar rights. The 1898 genealogist found a Newton Whittlesea baptized 4 June 1560 in the same Cambridgeshire records, and a Simeon Whittelse baptized 23 June 1542 — two years after Ralph's presumed death. Whether either was Ralph's son cannot now be proved (documentation does not survive), but the chronology fits comfortably with Ralph being the father of Simeon and a near-kinsman to Newton. The 1898 compiler placed these names together in his Cambridgeshire roll precisely because they appeared to be members of the same parish lineage.

Health was the perpetual hazard. The fens were notoriously malarial — "fen ague," recurrent tertian malaria carried by Anopheles mosquitoes that bred in the standing water, was endemic; few fen-dwellers reached old age without it. Bubonic plague, which had returned in waves since 1348, struck East Anglia repeatedly in the early sixteenth century: London suffered serious outbreaks in 1509, 1517, 1521, and 1525, and these radiated into the surrounding shires. The sweating sickness — Sudor Anglicus, a strange and lethal disease unique to England — swept the country in 1485, 1508 (the very year of Ralph's baptism), 1517, 1528-29, and finally 1551. The 1517 outbreak was particularly severe in East Anglia, killing within hours of symptom onset and disproportionately taking healthy young adult men. In 1528-29 it returned more virulently still; Anne Boleyn herself nearly died of it. Either visitation could plausibly have carried off Ralph, who would have been nine years old in the first wave and twenty in the second. Some modern epidemiologists associate the disease with hantavirus or with anthrax acquired through contaminated wool; we will never know for certain.

If we ask what an ordinary day looked like in his life: he rose before dawn, said the Pater Noster, ate bread and small ale, walked out to his strip in the open field or to the fen-meadow, worked till noon, ate again, worked through the long English afternoon, returned at dusk, ate a third meal, mended tools or made nets by rush-light, and slept on a straw mattress in a one- or two-room cottage with a smoke-hole in the thatched roof. The cottage was probably built of clunch (Fenland chalk-marl) or rammed earth on a low brick footing, with reed thatch from his own cutting. His wife brewed ale, baked, churned butter, salted eel, span wool, and bore as many children as God sent her — most of whom would not reach fifteen.

He did not read. He could probably not sign his name. He spoke a broad fenland English, full of words now lost — gault, dyke, lode, leam, hythe, mere, washes, holm — that described the watery landscape with precise economy. He knew the local saints, the local boundaries (defined by Rogationtide processions every spring), the local cures, the local weather signs. His world ended at the horizon, perhaps thirty miles across.

Death and Burial

Ralph Whittelse died around 1540, at approximately thirty-two. The precise date is not recorded in any source accessible to modern researchers; the 1898 genealogist gave a death year only approximately, evidently inferred from the silence of later registers and from the appearance of the next generation. He was almost certainly buried in the churchyard of Whittlesey St. Mary or St. Andrew, in an unmarked or wooden-marked grave; stone gravestones for commoners did not become usual in English country churchyards until the later seventeenth century. No memorial brass, slab, or wall-monument records him. The Whittlesey parish registers of St. Mary's do not begin until 1560 and so cannot help. His burial probably followed the still half-Catholic rite then in use — the Sarum Office of the Dead, partly anglicized — and was conducted by a priest who, three years earlier, had been a monk of Thorney or Ely.

If the chronology of the 1898 genealogy is correct, Ralph's death roughly coincides with the execution of Thomas Cromwell (28 July 1540) — the architect of the dissolution that had transformed his parish. He did not live to see the Edwardian reforms (1547-1553), the Marian return to Catholicism (1553-1558), or the Elizabethan settlement (1559). His son Simeon, baptized two years after his death, would witness all of those reversals.

The Cambridgeshire of His Time

Simeon was baptized in the summer of 1542 into a county in shock. The dissolution of the monasteries had ended only two years earlier with the surrender of Waltham Abbey (March 1540); Thorney and Ely, the two great Benedictine houses on which Whittlesey had depended, had been suppressed in 1539. The pension lists, the property transfers, and the dispersal of monastic libraries were still being processed. The Cambridgeshire that Simeon entered was administratively the same — same parishes, same hundreds, same manorial structure — but ecclesiastically and culturally upended.

Cambridgeshire in the mid-Tudor decades was also a more populous and more economically pressured county than it had been a generation earlier. Estimates suggest the English population grew from roughly 2.3 million in 1525 to perhaps 3 million by 1571 — a 30% increase against an essentially fixed acreage of arable land, with predictable consequences: rising grain prices, falling real wages, more vagrancy, the great enclosure controversies that produced Robert Kett's rising in nearby Norfolk in 1549 and the Crown's anxious enclosure commissions. Inflation, fed both by population growth and by the silver flooding in from Spanish America, ran through the entire third quarter of the sixteenth century. A bushel of wheat that cost about 5d in 1500 cost about 15d in 1570 and would cost 30d by 1600.

The county's population sat between 60,000 and 70,000 by Simeon's adulthood. Cambridge, with its colleges, was the focal point of intellectual life; the university was now decisively Protestant, having produced or sheltered such figures as Hugh Latimer, Nicholas Ridley, Thomas Cranmer (Jesus College), Matthew Parker (Corpus Christi), and Richard Cox. The fenland north of the university — Ely, Wisbech, Whittlesey, March, Chatteris, Soham — was rural, agricultural, and much poorer than the upland south. Government from Westminster reached the fen towns through the Bishop of Ely (in his ecclesiastical capacity) and through justices of the peace drawn from the gentry families of the dryland fringes.

Simeon's young manhood unfolded during one of the most volatile periods in English history. The 1550s alone produced the Edwardian Reformation, the boy-king's early death, the brief and chaotic attempt to crown Lady Jane Grey, Mary I's Catholic restoration, the burning of nearly 300 Protestants at the stake (1555-1558), Mary's death, and Elizabeth's accession. By the time Simeon turned twenty in 1562 the Elizabethan settlement was three years old and the country had not yet fully tested whether it would hold.

Whittlesey Parish and the Fens

Simeon grew up in a Whittlesey already much changed from his father's childhood. The two manors — Whittlesey St. Mary (formerly the Thorney abbey manor) and Whittlesey St. Andrew (formerly the Ely priory manor) — had passed into different hands after the dissolution but the underlying tenancies and customs were largely unchanged. The man holding the St. Mary's manor when Simeon was born was a lessee named Lyname, who was reported as "not disturbed in his tenancy after the Dissolution"; no outright grant of the manor by the crown was made until 1603. The St. Andrew's manor likewise passed into long lessee tenure under the new ownership of the Dean and Chapter of Ely (the reconstituted cathedral body). For an ordinary customary tenant, the change of lord was mostly invisible: the manorial court still met, the same officers were elected, the same fees and reliefs were paid, the same boundaries were perambulated.

The 1563 ecclesiastical census — taken when Simeon was twenty-one — recorded 355 households in the two parishes (266 in St. Mary's, 89 in St. Andrew's), or a population of roughly 1,400-1,600 souls. Whittlesey was the second largest place in the Isle of Ely. It was a market town on Friday and a working town the other six days. Its livelihoods remained those of the wet fenland: mixed husbandry on the high ground, livestock on the summer fens, peat-cutting, sedge-cutting, reed-harvesting, fishing, fowling, and (increasingly) brick-making on the gault.

A Tudor traveller approaching Whittlesey from Peterborough across Borough Fen would have seen the great spire of St. Mary's rising from the dryland island, surrounded by a low town of timber, daub, brick, and reed thatch, with the gault-clay brickyards visible on the edge. The Whittlesey Mere lay to the north — a vast inland lake, full of pike and bream, with its own small fleet of fen-skaters in the winter and reed-cutters in the summer. To the south and east, the wash-fens flooded brown each November and slowly receded each spring. By Simeon's adulthood the first half-hearted drainage schemes were being mooted; the wholesale draining of the Bedford Level by Cornelius Vermuyden was still seventy years away, but ambitious local landowners and merchants were already petitioning the crown for permission to embank and drain individual fens, often against fierce opposition from the fen commoners.

In the parish of Whittlesey St. Mary itself, the physical fabric of the church was being radically altered through Simeon's lifetime. The statues that had stood in the niches of the tower were gone. The rood-loft, with its painted figures of Christ and the Virgin, was demolished — almost certainly in the Edwardian campaign of 1547-1548, when royal commissioners ordered the destruction of all "images, candlesticks, trindles or rolls of wax, pictures, paintings, and all other monuments of feigned miracles." The dado of the old rood screen survived, and survives still — heavy, painted, displaced into a side-aisle. The Latin missal was put away; in its place came the English Book of Common Prayer of 1549 (revised in 1552, then suspended under Mary, then reissued in 1559). The altar was replaced by a plain wooden communion table. The Mass became the Lord's Supper.

Religious Context: A Fenland Parish Through the English Reformation

No biographical detail about Simeon Whittlesea is more important than the chronology of religious upheaval through which he lived. Every year of his life corresponded to a different official orthodoxy. He was born Catholic by law (under the late-Henrician Six Articles of 1539, which preserved transubstantiation and clerical celibacy even after the break with Rome), became Protestant at five (Edward VI's accession, 1547), became radically Protestant at eleven (the 1552 Prayer Book), became Catholic again that same year at eleven (the failure of Lady Jane Grey, accession of Mary, summer 1553), and became Protestant once more at sixteen (Elizabeth's accession, November 1558, Act of Uniformity 1559). Then he lived the rest of his life under the Elizabethan settlement, which he probably never thought of as final, because no English settlement of religion within living memory had been final.

The late Henrician years (1542-1547)

When Simeon was baptized in June 1542, England's religious state was incoherent. The papal supremacy had been abolished; the monasteries were gone; English-language scripture (the Great Bible of 1539) was chained in every parish church. But the Six Articles of 1539 reasserted the Catholic doctrine of the Mass; clerical celibacy was reaffirmed; communion in both kinds was forbidden to the laity. Bishop Thomas Goodrich of Ely, a reformer at heart, governed his diocese with care during these years, removing obvious "abuses" while preserving the legal status quo. In Whittlesey, the priest still said Mass in Latin, the children still learned Pater Noster and Ave in Latin, but the relics, shrines, and pilgrimage cults of the previous decade were gone.

Simeon was christened during the war years that ended Henry VIII's reign — the rough wooing of Scotland (1543-1551) and Henry's last French campaign (1544-1546), which together drained the treasury and brought the great Tudor coinage debasements. Cambridgeshire farmers felt this through tax demands and through the falling silver content of the coins they took in for their grain and wool.

The Edwardian Reformation (1547-1553)

Henry died in January 1547. Edward VI, aged nine, ruled through the council of his Protector, Edward Seymour, Duke of Somerset (and from 1549, John Dudley, Duke of Northumberland). The Edwardian regime was decisively Protestant. The Royal Injunctions of July 1547 ordered the destruction of all images "abused" by pilgrimage, the whitewashing of painted walls, the removal of shrines, and the placing of an English Bible and a copy of Erasmus's Paraphrases in every church. The Chantries Act of December 1547 dissolved all chantry chapels and guilds in England. In Whittlesey, the ten gilds in St. Mary's parish and the two in St. Andrew's were swept up by this Act; their lights were extinguished, their lands were confiscated, their tiny endowed prayers for the dead were ended. A whole social system of small charitable association, centred on prayer for departed parishioners, was abolished in one statute.

Simeon was five years old when the first Edwardian Book of Common Prayer was imposed by the Act of Uniformity of 1549. It was a moderate transitional document, recognizably descended from the Latin liturgy but in English. He was ten when the much more thoroughly Protestant Second Book of Common Prayer of 1552 was imposed — communion no longer a sacrifice but a memorial, the priest no longer at an eastward-facing stone altar but at a north-facing wooden table, no Eucharistic vestments, no kneeling at the rail except for "reverent reception."

In the fenland, the Edwardian reforms produced visible destruction in every parish church. Wall-paintings of the Doom, of St. Christopher, of the Virgin and Child were whitewashed. Stained-glass figures were broken. Service-books in Latin were burned or sold. Brass memorial plates with prayer-for-the-dead inscriptions were prised up and melted. The Western Rising of 1549 (Cornish and Devon men resisting the new Prayer Book) and Kett's Rebellion in Norfolk that same summer showed how unpopular these changes were in some regions, but eastern England — generally more receptive to Protestantism — saw less resistance. Whittlesey, on the evidence of the surviving record, was quiet.

The Marian Restoration (1553-1558)

Edward died in July 1553. After the brief nine-day reign of Lady Jane Grey, Mary I — Henry's Catholic daughter by Catherine of Aragon — was proclaimed queen on 19 July. She was the first Catholic monarch in twenty years. By the end of 1553 the Edwardian church had been legally repudiated. By 1554 the papal supremacy was restored, Cardinal Pole returned from exile as Archbishop of Canterbury, married clergy were ejected from their livings, and the heresy laws of 1401 were re-enacted.

Between February 1555 and November 1558, nearly 300 Protestants were burned at the stake for refusing to recant. The victims were overwhelmingly East Anglian and southeastern, with a heavy concentration in Kent, Essex, and Sussex; Cambridgeshire saw fewer burnings than its southern neighbours but was not exempt. The great Cambridge martyrs Hugh Latimer (formerly Bishop of Worcester) and Nicholas Ridley (Bishop of London, formerly of Rochester) were burned at Oxford in October 1555; Thomas Cranmer, the architect of the English Prayer Book, was burned at Oxford in March 1556. In the Isle of Ely, Bishop Thomas Goodrich had died in 1554; his Catholic successor Thomas Thirlby (1554-1559) implemented the Marian restoration with thoroughness.

In Whittlesey, the parish church furnishings were restored. The wooden communion table was removed and a stone altar reinstated; the rood, or its replacement, was reconstructed on the screen; Latin missals were brought back; vestments were re-acquired (often from surviving private hoards in the parish — many parishes had quietly kept their plate and vestments through the Edwardian years, betting on a Catholic restoration). The parish priest, if he had married during Edward's reign, was deprived; if he had not, he was retrained in Catholic liturgy. Simeon, an eleven-year-old altar-boy in 1553, learned the Latin responses afresh. The Edwardian whitewash was scraped or painted over with images; the small wooden statues that had survived in farmhouses came back to the niches.

The Marian regime also implemented an active campaign of clerical enforcement. Visitation articles required parishioners to attend Mass, to confess, to receive Easter communion in one kind (i.e., bread only). Recusants — those who refused — were fined, presented to the church courts, and, if obdurate, prosecuted under the heresy laws.

The Elizabethan Settlement (1558-1571)

Mary died on 17 November 1558. Elizabeth, her Protestant half-sister, was proclaimed the same day. Simeon was sixteen. The Act of Supremacy of 1559 made Elizabeth Supreme Governor (not Head — a careful softening) of the Church of England; the Act of Uniformity of the same year imposed a slightly revised version of the 1552 Prayer Book. The settlement of 1559 was a deliberate compromise: Protestant in doctrine, retaining bishops, retaining surplices and a recognizable liturgy, removing the papacy, removing the Mass, but conducting itself in such a way that conscientious Catholics might (the queen hoped) attend services without too much offence.

In the diocese of Ely, the Marian bishop Thirlby was deprived in 1559 for refusing the Oath of Supremacy and replaced by Richard Cox (1559-1581), one of the Marian exiles returned from Frankfurt and Strasbourg. Cox was a moderate Protestant who would later resist Puritan pressure for further reform; under his governance the diocese of Ely was conformist, somewhat tolerant of conservative practice in remote parishes, but firm in its rejection of Mass.

The summer 1559 royal visitation of the eastern dioceses (including Ely) implemented the new settlement parish by parish. Visitors enquired whether the new Prayer Book was being used, whether stone altars had been replaced with wooden tables, whether the Bishops' Bible (in due course the Great Bible) was set up, whether the parish priest was conformable. The Whittlesey churches passed through this process in 1559 or 1560. The St. Mary's parish register, begun in 1560, dates from this moment — registers were among the practical instruments the Tudor church now insisted on, and many parishes began them afresh under the new dispensation.

Simeon spent the last thirteen years of his life (1558-1571) under this Elizabethan settlement. The crisis years for the regime — the Northern Rising of 1569, the papal bull Regnans in Excelsis of February 1570 (which excommunicated Elizabeth and absolved her subjects of their allegiance), and the Ridolfi Plot uncovered in 1571 — fell in his final years. The bull of 1570 in particular changed the character of English Catholic recusancy: what had been (in the queen's hope) a tolerable conservatism became, after Rome's declaration, a treason. The penal laws against recusants began to tighten just as Simeon was dying.

For an ordinary parishioner in Whittlesey, the lived reality of these doctrinal whiplashes was less abstract than it sounds. Simeon would have heard four substantially different forms of Sunday service before he turned twenty. He had learned three different sets of words for the Eucharist. The church furnishings of his parish had been broken, restored, and broken again. His parents, godparents, and elder kin — born under the old religion — had been required successively to repudiate Rome (under Henry), to embrace radical Protestantism (under Edward), to repudiate Protestantism and re-embrace Rome (under Mary), and to repudiate Rome once more (under Elizabeth). Conformism was a survival skill. Many families practised "church papistry" — outward conformity to the Elizabethan Prayer Book with private Catholic devotion at home — into the 1570s and beyond. We do not know on which side of this private divide the Whittlesey Whittleseas stood; the documentary evidence is silent.

Daily Life, Economy, and the Wider World

Economically, Simeon's lifetime was dominated by three trends: persistent inflation, growing population pressure, and the slow opening of the fenland economy to outside capital. He inherited (we may presume) a copyhold tenement in one of the Whittlesey manors and worked it with the same mixed husbandry — wheat, barley, beans on the dryland; cattle and sheep on the summer fens; eels, fish, fowl, peat, reed, and sedge from the wet — that had sustained his father. The price of his crops rose steadily through his adult life. So did the price of everything he bought.

The 1551 great debasement of the coinage was reversed by Elizabeth in 1560-61, restoring confidence in English silver currency; this stabilization was one of the early achievements of her reign and made the long Elizabethan economic recovery possible. But population pressure continued unabated. The 1563 ecclesiastical census recorded a Cambridgeshire that was substantially fuller than it had been at the dissolution.

In 1563 the so-called Statute of Artificers regulated apprenticeship, wages, and movement of labour throughout England; in the same decade the Elizabethan Poor Law began to take its early shape (the comprehensive Poor Law statutes of 1597 and 1601 lay in the future). Simeon would have known these legal frames as constraints on his ability to move, on the wages of his harvest workers, and on the obligation of his parish to relieve the indigent.

The wool-and-cloth trade dominated England's foreign commerce. The fenlands were not a centre of wool-cloth manufacture, but Whittlesey was a node in the inland water-transport system that moved upland wool from Northamptonshire and Lincolnshire down to King's Lynn, and from Lynn to Antwerp (until the disruption of the Antwerp market in the 1560s) and then to Hamburg and the Baltic. The disruption of Antwerp by the Dutch Revolt — which began in earnest in 1566 with the beeldenstorm (the Iconoclastic Fury) and the duke of Alba's repression of 1567 — sent shock-waves through English cloth-merchant capital. The English Merchant Adventurers moved their staple successively to Emden, Hamburg, and Middelburg; the queen's loan of armed support to the Dutch Sea Beggars (from 1572) was just over the horizon. None of this was abstract to a Whittlesey man whose livelihood depended in part on inland water-traffic.

Public health was no kinder to Simeon's generation than to his father's. Plague returned in serious form to East Anglia in 1563, when (in London) some 17,000 died — perhaps a quarter of the city's population. Outbreaks of "the sweat" had been ended by the strange disappearance of Sudor Anglicus after 1551, but plague continued in waves: 1563, 1567, 1574, 1582. The fens had their own particular endemic disease, the "fen ague," a quartan or tertian malaria carried by Anopheles mosquitoes from the standing water; this was so ubiquitous that fen-doctors specialized in it, prescribing opium-laced "fen-syrup" (poppy tea) as the standard palliative. Fen-dwellers tended to live shorter lives, on average, than upland villagers; the ague did not usually kill on its own but weakened the patient against fever, dysentery, and the recurring intestinal infections of a population drinking unfiltered surface water.

Simeon's death at around twenty-nine fits the pattern. Whether he died of the ague, of plague (in the 1567 wave), of a fall, of a fever, or of one of the recurring respiratory infections of a damp landscape, we cannot now know. He left no will recorded in the diocesan probate court (the surviving Ely consistory records do not contain his name), suggesting either that he died intestate with insufficient personal estate to require probate, or that he had transferred his customary holding inter vivos to a son or kinsman, or simply that the relevant probate document is lost.

A reasonable conjecture, which the 1898 genealogist seems to have made: Simeon was the father of one of the later sixteenth-century Whittlesey baptisms recorded in the same Cambridgeshire roll. The closest candidate by date is John Whittlesea, baptized 23 February 1570 (the year before Simeon's death), who in June 1592 married Elizabeth Wesley — a name that, with the Whittleseas, recurs in later New England genealogies as part of the kin network from which the immigrant ancestor John Whittlesey (b. 1623) emerged. Documentation does not survive to make this filiation certain, but the chronology is exactly consistent: Simeon born 1542, married in his early twenties, son born when Simeon was 28, Simeon dies a year later, the son grows up under the protection of an uncle or grandfather. This is a standard sixteenth-century life-pattern.

Death and Burial

Simeon Whittlesea died in 1571, age approximately twenty-nine. The exact date is not preserved in any source accessible to modern researchers; the 1898 genealogist, who had at least transcribed the baptismal entry from a now-vanished or unidentified Cambridgeshire register, gave only the year. He was almost certainly buried in the churchyard of his home parish — most likely Whittlesey St. Mary or Whittlesey St. Andrew — without a stone marker. (Stone gravestones for the commonalty did not appear in English country churchyards before the later seventeenth century.) The burial would have followed the Elizabethan order: a procession to the grave, the minister reading the prescribed text from the 1559 Book of Common Prayer ("I am the resurrection and the life"), the committal ("we therefore commit his body to the ground"), and the dispersal of the mourners. There would have been no requiem Mass, no votive lights, no prayers for the soul — the Catholic apparatus of intercession for the dead had been dismantled twenty years before, and even those parishioners who privately retained Catholic belief did so in silence.

His death in 1571 placed him in the first generation of English Whittleseys to live and die wholly outside the medieval Catholic apparatus that had shaped his grandparents' world. Twelve years after Elizabeth's accession, the parish life into which he was buried was settling into the long Elizabethan moderate Protestantism that would, in due course, give shape to the religion of the New England Puritans of whom his great-great-grandson would be one of the first generation.

The England of His Generation

John Whittlesea was born in 1570, twelve years into the reign of Elizabeth Tudor. The country he entered was, by the standards of his grandparents, almost unrecognisable. The break with Rome under Henry VIII (1534), the protestantising radicalism of Edward VI (1547-1553), the Marian Catholic restoration (1553-1558), and finally the Elizabethan religious settlement of 1559 had all rolled across the parishes of England in the span of a single human generation. The Whittlesea family of the Cambridgeshire fens had lived through every one of those convulsions; in 1570 the form of compromise we now call the Elizabethan Settlement was still being tested in every village.

Three large events shaped the political weather of John's childhood and youth. The first was the Northern Rising of 1569 - a Catholic revolt by the Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland the year before he was born - which had ended in some 600 executions and confirmed for Elizabeth that a Catholic restoration would come, if it came at all, by force. The second was Pope Pius V's bull Regnans in Excelsis (February 1570), which excommunicated Elizabeth and released English Catholics from any duty of obedience to her. From that moment onward, English Catholicism could be treated by the state as treason rather than simple recusancy. The infant John Whittlesea was thus born into a kingdom that was, for the first time in its history, formally and irrevocably at odds with Rome.

The third event was the Spanish Armada of 1588. John would have been eighteen years old that summer. The beacons that flared along the east coast - and on the high ground of the Cambridgeshire fens, where Whittlesey itself sat as an island above the surrounding marsh - were visible signals of an existential moment. In Cambridgeshire, the muster rolls of 1588 obliged every able-bodied man between sixteen and sixty to be enrolled and equipped. A young man of John's age would have been on those rolls, drilling with a billhook or, if his father could afford it, a caliver, expecting an invasion fleet that in the event never landed.

The Elizabethan economy that surrounded him was experiencing what historians call the "price revolution" - a long inflation of grain and land prices that ran from about 1540 to 1620 and that transformed rural society. The yeomen and husbandmen of the eastern counties who held secure leasehold or freehold land prospered; those who depended on wage labour or who held land by uncertain tenure found themselves squeezed. The decade of John's life saw bad harvests in 1586-1587 and a much worse run in 1594-1597, when grain prices in eastern England doubled and parish burial registers swell with the names of the poor. John died in 1594, at the leading edge of that crisis. Whether his death was connected to it - whether by famine fever, by simple typhus in a household weakened by want, by the plague which moved through East Anglia in waves throughout the 1590s - cannot be known, because the parish register entry, if it survives at all, has not been indexed under any of the spelling variants currently searchable online.

Cambridgeshire and the Fens

The Cambridgeshire into which John was born was a county defined by water. The Great Fen - the vast, half-drowned basin formed by the slow rivers Nene, Welland, Ouse, and Cam - covered more than two thousand square miles of what is now eastern England, stretching from the Wash down through the Isle of Ely and into Huntingdonshire. Within it, isolated patches of higher ground rose as "islands": Ely, March, Thorney, Chatteris, and Whittlesey itself. Whittlesey sat on a low gravel ridge, an inhabited island for as long as the Bronze Age, with the parishes of St Mary and St Andrew dividing the town between them.

Before the great Stuart drainage schemes of the 1630s and 1650s, the fenland economy was unlike that of any other English region. It was a wetland commonwealth, in which the men of villages like Whittlesey held customary rights to the surrounding marsh that were quite as valuable as any plough-share of arable. They cut peat for fuel, gathered reeds for thatching, kept geese by the hundred on the summer meres, fished for eels, pike, perch, and bream, and snared waterfowl in winter when the duck and teal massed on the flooded levels. The summer pastures, once the waters had retreated, supported huge herds of cattle and sheep that fattened on grasses no upland field could match. Daniel Defoe, writing a generation later, would describe the fenmen as living "as if in another country," and the seventeenth-century saying that a fenman could "fish in summer and skate in winter" was not a joke but a fair description of the seasonal rhythm.

If John Whittlesea spent his short life in or near Whittlesey itself, this was the economy he was bred into. The townsmen of Whittlesey were not gentry. They were yeomen, husbandmen, and craftsmen - bakers, smiths, fullers, weavers of fenland sedge - and a substantial number of them earned their living directly from the wet commons. Documentation does not survive that names John's specific trade, but the statistical likelihood, given his birth into a Cambridgeshire family of the period, is overwhelming that he worked the land or the water in some combination, perhaps holding a small copyhold of arable on the gravel island and a share of the fen-rights that came with his father Simeon's house.

The town itself was a recognisable Tudor market centre. Its two great parish churches - St Mary's, with its slender fourteenth-century spire visible for miles across the fens, and St Andrew's, the "low church" a few hundred yards to the west - bracketed the marketplace. A weekly market and a great fair drew traders from Peterborough, Wisbech, March, and Chatteris. The roads were causeways: raised banks of stone and earth, treacherous in winter, the only secure connection to the upland country. In summer, much of the traffic moved by boat, on the slow waterways that bound the fen towns to King's Lynn and the sea.

Religious and Political Context

John Whittlesea grew up in the second generation of the Elizabethan religious settlement, and his particular corner of England was one of the places where that settlement was being most actively reshaped from below. The diocese of Ely - which covered Cambridgeshire and the Isle - and the diocese of Peterborough, which bordered it to the north, were both regions of early and sustained Puritan strength. The University of Cambridge, only thirty-five miles south of Whittlesey, was producing year after year a stream of preachers who took up parish livings in the surrounding countryside and pressed for a "further reformation" of the English Church.

The 1570s and 1580s saw the first great wave of organised Puritan activity: the "Admonition" controversy of 1572, in which the Cambridge-trained Thomas Cartwright and his allies called for a presbyterian remodelling of the Church; the "Classical Movement" of the 1580s, in which Puritan ministers in many eastern counties met in secret synods to coordinate their preaching; the Marprelate Tracts of 1588-1589, satirical attacks on the bishops that scandalised the establishment. Cambridgeshire was implicated in all of it. By the time John reached adulthood in the late 1580s, "godly" preaching, lecture-day sermons, and the practice of weekly fast-day exercises were a familiar feature of life in the parish churches around the fens.

We do not know whether the Whittlesea family of this generation were committed Puritans, conforming churchmen, or something in between. The 1898 genealogy is silent on the question, and parish records from Whittlesey in this period, even where they exist, do not typically mark theological allegiance. What can be said with confidence is that John lived in a region where Puritan ideas were unusually accessible, where the parish church was a place of contention as well as worship, and where the children of yeoman families were beginning to learn to read - increasingly, to read the Geneva Bible, which by 1570 had become the household scripture of Protestant England. The seeds of the Puritan culture that would eventually drive his great-grandson to Connecticut were being sown in his lifetime, in the very parishes where he would have heard sermons preached.

Politically, John's youth saw the consolidation of the Elizabethan regime: the execution of Mary Queen of Scots (1587), the defeat of the Armada (1588), the wars in the Netherlands and France, and the long, exhausting Nine Years' War in Ireland that began in 1593. Subsidies and ship money were demanded with increasing frequency, and the burden fell heavily on the middling sort - the yeomen and husbandmen whose names appeared on the assessment rolls. By the time of John's death in 1594, England was a country at war on multiple fronts, suffering harvest failure, gripped by the long demographic squeeze of the late Elizabethan crisis.

Family and Lineage

John was the son of Simeon Whittlesey (1542-1571). His father died when John was only one year old - a fact that, while genealogically routine for the sixteenth century, has serious implications for any reconstruction of his life. Tudor children who lost their fathers in infancy ordinarily passed under the wardship of an uncle, an elder brother, a stepfather, or (in cases where the family held land of a manor) the manorial lord. Whatever the mechanism in John's case, the records that would identify it have not survived in any indexed and accessible form.

The 1898 Whittelsey-Whittlesey genealogy, working from a chain of family tradition rather than from contemporary documentation, places John in direct paternal descent from the medieval Whittlesey family of Cambridgeshire and ultimately - through a heraldic claim the modern researcher cannot verify - from Archbishop William de Whittlesey of Canterbury (d. 1374). Whether this descent is historical or constructive is impossible to say. What is certain is that families bearing the Whittlesey surname (in its multitudinous spellings) were clustered in the period 1500-1650 in a relatively compact zone around the eponymous town: Colne, Bluntisham, Woodhurst, Pidley, Godmanchester, Papworth St Agnes, Graveley, Chatteris, and Cottenham. The surname was, as one careful modern researcher has put it, "a distinctive regional name not found in other parts of England." Wherever John Whittlesea lived between his birth in 1570 and his death in 1594, he lived within a few days' ride of every other family that bore his name.

He married - the date and the woman's name are not preserved in any source accessible at present - and fathered at least one son, John E. Whittlesey, born in 1593. That son was therefore an infant of perhaps a year, possibly less, when his father died in 1594. The pattern of the previous generation, in which Simeon's death had left the one-year-old John fatherless, was thus repeated with painful exactness. Two consecutive Whittlesea fathers died young, leaving infant sons to be brought up by mothers, uncles, or guardians whose identities the surviving record does not preserve.

What Daily Life Likely Looked Like

In the absence of direct evidence, we can only describe the life that a young man of John Whittlesea's class, region, and generation almost certainly led. He was born into a yeoman or husbandman household - one of the literate, propertied middle ranks of rural Cambridgeshire. The house he grew up in would have been a single-storey or one-and-a-half-storey timber-framed building with wattle-and-daub infill and a thatched roof, two or three rooms on the ground floor with a smoke-hood (the brick chimney was still spreading into the lower social ranks during exactly this period) and a sleeping loft above. The hall would have been the centre of family life: a long table, a settle by the fire, a small array of pewter dishes that were the household's prestige objects, displayed on a shelf.

The agricultural year began in earnest in February, with the first ploughing of the gravel uplands for spring barley and oats. April and May brought lambing and the turning out of the cattle onto the summer commons - the great moment of the fenland year, when herds that had wintered on hay in the upland byres were driven down the causeways into the recovering pastures. Summer was hay-making, peat-cutting, and the long work of maintaining the small drainage dykes that protected each holding's lowest fields. August and September were harvest, the most labour-intensive weeks of the calendar, when even the children turned out to glean. Autumn brought the slaughter and salting of cattle that could not be wintered, and the cycle began again with Michaelmas (29 September), when many rents fell due and many tenancies turned over.

Religious life ran on a parallel calendar. Compulsory Sunday attendance at the parish church was the law; absence drew a one-shilling fine under the Act of Uniformity. The Book of Common Prayer, in its 1559 revision, dictated the form of worship. The litany, the catechism, the psalms in Sternhold and Hopkins' metrical version - these were the texts a child of John's generation would have known by heart before he could read them on a page. The great feast days - Christmas, Easter, Whitsun - and the great fast days were marked by the parish in a rhythm that, by the 1580s, was being reinforced by a generation of Puritan-leaning clergy who pressed for greater Sabbath observance and longer sermons.

Marriage typically came late in this society - mid-to-late twenties for men, slightly younger for women. If John fathered his son in 1593 at the age of 23, he had married rather young by the standards of his class. The brevity of his life - dead at perhaps 24 - is itself a reminder of the demographic reality of the period: roughly one English man in three did not survive to age forty, and the cluster of deaths in his immediate male line (Simeon at 29; John at 24; John E. at 30) is consistent with what we know of mortality among the middling sort in late Tudor and early Stuart England.

Death and Records

John Whittlesea died in 1594, in Cambridgeshire, at approximately twenty-four years of age. The cause of death is not documented. The mid-1590s were, however, exceptionally lethal years in eastern England. The catastrophic harvest failures of 1594, 1595, 1596, and 1597 - four bad years in a row - produced the worst sustained subsistence crisis of the Elizabethan period, with parish burial figures running at two or three times their normal levels in many counties. Bubonic plague was endemic in East Anglia in this decade; typhus, dysentery, and what contemporaries called "ague" (often malaria, common in the fen country) were constant background killers. Any of these, or a simple accident in a country where every cart, every horse, every flooded dyke posed real hazards, could have ended his life.

His burial, if recorded, was almost certainly entered into the register of St Mary's or St Andrew's Whittlesey - or, if he had moved out of the parent town, into one of the dozen or so parish registers of the surrounding villages where Whittleseys are known to have lived in this period. The destruction of records during the Civil War, the patchy state of pre-1600 fen-country registers, and the wild variability of spelling (Whittelse, Whittlesy, Wittelsea, Whitelseye, and at least a dozen other forms appear in surviving documents) all conspire to obscure his trace. The 1898 genealogy preserves the dates without preserving the source citation. It is best understood as a family tradition, written down in the late Victorian period, that may rest on a now-vanished family Bible, a surviving register entry seen by an earlier researcher, or - it must be admitted - on the reconstructive logic of generations counted backward from the immigrant.

What survived him was his son. The infant John E., born in 1593, was the unbroken link to the immigrant generation. Without that one fragile life, the Whittlesey line in America - and the long descent that runs from a one-year-old orphan in the Cambridgeshire fens in 1594 to the family historians of nineteenth-century Connecticut and beyond - would not exist.

The Wider World in His Lifetime

To finish the portrait, it is worth pulling back to the wider European and Atlantic context within which an obscure young man of the Cambridgeshire fens lived his twenty-four years. The 1570s, when John was a child, were the decade in which the Dutch Revolt against Spanish rule entered its sustained military phase: the Sack of Antwerp (1576) and the Union of Utrecht (1579) made the Low Countries the great theatre of European Protestant-Catholic conflict. English volunteers fought in the Netherlands from the late 1570s onward; East Anglian ports including King's Lynn, only a day's journey by water from Whittlesey, were the principal embarkation points and the principal landfall for Dutch refugees. Significant numbers of Dutch Protestants - the so-called "Strangers" - settled in East Anglian towns in this period, bringing with them weaving techniques, market gardening, and a culture of strict Calvinist worship that would influence East Anglian Puritanism for generations. The Cambridgeshire fens, with their natural waterway links to the North Sea, were on the route by which Dutch ideas, Dutch capital, and (eventually) Dutch drainage engineers would enter England.

The 1580s, his teenage years, saw the assassination of William the Silent in 1584, the execution of Mary Queen of Scots in 1587, and the great climax of the Armada in 1588. The English Catholic mission, organised by the seminary at Douai and reinforced by Jesuit priests trained on the continent, was at its peak of intensity in these years. Edmund Campion was executed in 1581; the Babington Plot was suppressed in 1586; the Throckmorton Plot in 1583. Anti-Catholic feeling in eastern England was running at a sustained high. For a boy in his teens, this was the political weather of the times - bell-ringings for victories, fast-days for deliverances, the constant low hum of treason trials in London and elsewhere.

The 1590s, his adult years, were the years of the long Spanish war, the Irish war, and the Elizabethan twilight. In intellectual life this was the great decade of Shakespeare's emergence: Romeo and Juliet (about 1594, the year of John's death), A Midsummer Night's Dream shortly after, the early history plays drawing the new playgoing public into a national mythology. In religious life, the 1590s saw the publication of Richard Hooker's Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity (1593-1597), the great Anglican defence of the Elizabethan settlement against Puritan critique; in the same years William Perkins of Cambridge was producing the systematic Calvinist theology that would become the household reading of godly New England a generation later. The first edition of the King James Bible was still seventeen years in the future; the Geneva Bible, with its Calvinist marginalia, was the scripture of the Whittleseys' parish.

There is no evidence that John himself was caught up in any of these grand currents. He was, on the available record, a young man of the Cambridgeshire fens, dead at twenty-four, who left behind one infant son and a name in a Victorian genealogy. But he lived in the great age, and the great age touched even the obscurest market town. The Armada beacons that flared on the high ground of his county in 1588 were his beacons. The grain prices that climbed remorselessly through the 1590s were the prices in his market. The sermons preached from the Cambridge-trained ministers in the fen-edge parishes were the sermons in his ear. To say his life is undocumented is not to say it was unimportant. He was the link without which the line did not continue.

John E. Whittlesey is, like his father, a figure whose dates and parentage are preserved by family tradition rather than by an unbroken chain of contemporary documentation. The 1898 Charles Barney Whittelsey genealogy is the principal source, and modern researchers have shown that some of its assertions about the immigrant generation rest on slender evidence. What can be said with confidence is that a man of this name and approximate dates produced a son who emerged in colonial Connecticut records as John Whittlesey of Saybrook, and that the family tradition consistently places him in Cambridgeshire. The chapters that follow describe the world of his thirty years - the late Tudor inheritance, the unsettled Jacobean church, the fenland disputes that broke into open violence in the very year of his death, and the religious and economic tensions that would, within a decade, send tens of thousands of his neighbours and contemporaries across the Atlantic.

The England of His Generation

John E. Whittlesey was born in 1593, in the last decade of Elizabeth's reign, into a country exhausted by war, harvest failure, and demographic crisis. He died in 1623, in the penultimate year of James I, with the great Caroline storm of civil war and Atlantic migration still over the horizon. The thirty years of his life were a hinge period in English history - the moment when the late-Tudor consensus on religion, monarchy, and the economy began to come apart, but had not yet visibly broken.

The 1590s into which he was born were brutal. The catastrophic harvest failures of 1594-1597 - exactly the years in which his father died and he himself was an infant - produced a four-year subsistence crisis worse than any England had seen since the early fourteenth century. Burial registers across East Anglia show death rates running at double or triple the normal level. The war with Spain dragged on; the long Nine Years' War in Ireland (1593-1603) drained men and money; the queen herself was visibly aged and the succession unresolved. To be a small child in eastern England in the late 1590s was to be born into hardship.

The accession of James VI of Scotland as James I of England in March 1603 brought, at first, an enormous sense of relief. The succession was peaceful. The Treaty of London (1604) ended the long Spanish war. The Irish settlement of 1603 brought, at least temporarily, an end to that long bleeding. James projected an image of himself as the king of peace, Rex Pacificus. For a child of ten - which John E. would have been in 1603 - the new reign looked like the beginning of better times.

But the optimism did not last. The Hampton Court Conference of January 1604 dashed Puritan hopes for a "further reformation" of the church; the new king's response to the Millenary Petition was to refuse almost all of its reforms and to threaten to "harry" nonconforming ministers "out of the land." The Gunpowder Plot of November 1605 traumatised English Protestantism and produced a hardening of anti-Catholic legislation that would shape political loyalties for a century. The Parliament of 1610 broke down over the Great Contract, beginning the long dispute between crown and Commons over taxation and prerogative that would culminate in 1642.

In John E.'s teens and twenties, the rhythm of crisis tightened. The Addled Parliament of 1614 dissolved in mutual recrimination after only two months. The disastrous Cockayne project of 1614-1617 wrecked the English cloth trade. The death of Prince Henry in 1612 had left the succession in the hands of the less politically gifted Prince Charles. The Thirty Years' War broke out in central Europe in 1618; James's son-in-law Frederick of the Palatinate was driven from his lands the following year; from 1620 onward, English Protestants watched the slow defeat of the German Protestant cause with mounting alarm. By 1621-1623, when the prince and the Duke of Buckingham travelled to Madrid in the abortive "Spanish Match," anti-Catholic feeling in eastern England was at a pitch not seen since the Armada.

This was the world in which John E. Whittlesey lived and died. He was a Jacobean man almost entirely - born in the last decade of Elizabeth, his memory and conscience formed under James. He died in 1623, in the late autumn of that reign, before the storms of Charles I had broken but at the precise moment when the conditions that would drive the Great Migration to New England were taking their decisive shape.

Cambridgeshire and the Fens

Cambridgeshire in the early seventeenth century was a county on the eve of revolutionary transformation. The fenland economy that had supported communities like Whittlesey for centuries was about to be challenged, more directly than ever before, by the Crown's interest in draining the Great Level. The fight over the fens would shape the politics of John E.'s generation and would, within his son's lifetime, redraw the map of eastern England.

The market town of Whittlesey itself - the toponymic origin of the surname - sat on its gravel ridge above the marsh, a parish of two churches and perhaps eight hundred to a thousand inhabitants in this period. Its two great churches, St Mary's with its prominent fourteenth-century spire and St Andrew's the "low church" beside it, between them served the agricultural and commercial population of a town whose economy still rested on the wet commons. Eels, fish, fowl, peat, reeds, sedge, summer grazing for cattle and geese - these were the fenland staples, and they were held under customary rights that descended in families across generations. The Whittlesea family, whose surname proclaimed the connection, were part of this commonweal whether or not they still lived in Whittlesey proper. Modern surname-cluster analysis of the period 1500-1650 shows that the Whittleseys had by John E.'s lifetime spread across the surrounding country: into the Colne-Bluntisham-Woodhurst-Pidley group of parishes east of Huntingdon, into Godmanchester and Huntingdon itself, into Papworth St Agnes, Graveley, Chatteris, and Cottenham. The family was numerous, regionally distinctive, and bound to the fen-edge landscape.

The threat to that world came from London. King James, like his Tudor predecessors, viewed the half-drained fens as a national disgrace and a national opportunity - millions of acres of land that could, if drained, be brought into cultivation and a substantial new revenue stream for the Crown. As early as 1600 the Crown had explored drainage schemes; in 1605 a major Act of Parliament gave drainage projectors broad powers to alter the watercourses of the fens; from 1620 onward the Dutch engineer Cornelius Vermuyden was being consulted on increasingly ambitious projects in the eastern counties. Vermuyden himself worked on dike repairs at Dagenham in 1622 and 1623 - the very years of John E. Whittlesey's last illness - and would, under Charles I, contract in 1630 to drain the Great Level itself.

The fen-dwellers' response was furious. They saw the schemes as the theft of their commons by Crown projectors and Dutch foreigners; they understood, correctly, that drained land would be enclosed and sold to the gentry, and that the customary economy of fish and fowl and summer pasture would be destroyed. Resistance ran from petitions and lawsuits in the early 1620s to organised sabotage of works and, by the 1630s, open riot. The famous "Fen Tigers" of the seventeenth century - the men of Whittlesey, Wisbech, Ely, Chatteris, and the surrounding villages who broke down dykes by night and pulled up sluices - were the heirs of a resistance that was already gathering in the years of John E.'s adulthood.

It is reasonable to suppose, though direct evidence has not survived, that the Whittlesey family of John E.'s generation followed these disputes with intense interest. Their economic stake in the fenland commons would have been substantial. The drainage controversies of the 1620s, like the religious controversies of the same decade, were among the formative political experiences of any thinking yeoman or husbandman in the Cambridgeshire fens.

Religious and Political Context

The single most important fact about the religious culture of John E. Whittlesey's Cambridgeshire was the proximity of the University of Cambridge. The university - thirty-five miles to the south of Whittlesey, less than two days' walk - had been since the 1560s the great engine of Puritan ministerial formation in England. Emmanuel College, founded in 1584 by Sir Walter Mildmay as an explicitly Puritan seminary, was producing in John E.'s lifetime a steady stream of preachers who took up livings across East Anglia. Sidney Sussex (founded 1596), Christ's, St John's, and Trinity all contained influential Puritan factions. Of the men who would lead the founding of New England in the 1630s, an astonishingly high proportion - John Cotton, Thomas Hooker, Thomas Shepard, John Harvard, John Winthrop himself - were Cambridge men.

The countryside around Cambridge and through the fens was thoroughly impregnated with this culture. Town lectureships paid for by the godly merchant elite, weekly fast-day sermons, market-day exercises in which neighbouring ministers came together to preach in turn, the careful study of the Geneva Bible in literate yeoman households - all of these were features of life in the diocese of Ely and the southern parts of Peterborough diocese throughout the early seventeenth century. The Whittleseys would have heard such preaching, even if they were not themselves of fully Puritan conviction; it was unavoidable.

The political religious crisis of John E.'s adult years was driven by James I's increasing attempts to enforce conformity. The 1604 Canons required strict use of the Book of Common Prayer and the wearing of the surplice; ministers who refused were deprived (around ninety, in the immediate aftermath). The Bancroft visitation of 1605-1606 enforced these requirements with energy. The 1611 publication of the Authorised (King James) Version of the Bible was, in part, a deliberate attempt to displace the Geneva Bible with its Puritan-leaning marginal notes; the project succeeded among the clergy and gentry only slowly, and the Geneva remained the household scripture of many godly families well into the 1630s.

The Gunpowder Plot of 5 November 1605 - John E. was twelve - produced a permanent change in the religious atmosphere. The annual celebration of the failure of the plot (Bonfire Night, established by statute in 1606) was a national liturgy of anti-Catholicism whose passions, in years of perceived Catholic threat, could be intense. The accession of James, the failure of the conference at Hampton Court, the Gunpowder Plot itself - these were the three defining religious events of John E.'s childhood, and together they framed the conscience of his generation.

The drift of the later 1610s and 1620s was, from a Puritan perspective, increasingly alarming. The promotion of Arminian and "high church" clergymen - men like Lancelot Andrewes and (later) William Laud - was perceived as a creeping retreat from the Reformed character of the English Church. The proposed "Spanish Match" between Prince Charles and the Spanish Infanta, which dominated English politics from 1621 to 1623, was felt by Puritan opinion as a betrayal of the Protestant cause in Europe. The defeat of the Elector Palatine - James's son-in-law - and the collapse of the Protestant cause in Bohemia and the Palatinate from 1620 onward fed a sense that Antichrist was advancing and that England's leadership was failing to resist.

It was in this atmosphere that, beginning around 1620, individual godly families began to take the unprecedented step of considering emigration. The Mayflower sailed in September 1620, carrying the Scrooby separatists by way of Leiden to Plymouth. John Endecott led an advance party to Salem in 1628. The Massachusetts Bay Company was chartered in 1629 and the great fleet of John Winthrop sailed in 1630. Between 1630 and 1642, perhaps 20,000 English men, women, and children crossed the Atlantic to settle in New England, with East Anglia - and Cambridgeshire and Suffolk in particular - contributing a strikingly disproportionate share of them.

John E. Whittlesey died in 1623, before the great migration began. He would not himself have seen the Winthrop fleet sail. But the conditions that would produce that migration - the perceived corruption of the English Church, the rise of Arminianism, the failures of Stuart foreign policy, the agricultural and economic squeeze, the consolidation of a self-aware "godly" identity in the eastern counties - were all present and visible in the years of his adulthood. His son, born in the year of his death, would grow up in the world the great migration produced. The bridge between the Cambridgeshire Whittleseys and the Saybrook Whittleseys runs directly through John E.'s short life.

Family and Lineage

John E. Whittlesey was the son of John Whittlesea (1570-1594) and the grandson of Simeon Whittlesey (1542-1571). His father died when John E. was an infant - perhaps a year old, perhaps less - in 1594. He was therefore raised, like his father before him, as a fatherless child. The name of his mother, the household into which he was taken, and the kinsmen who managed his minority are not recorded in any source presently accessible.

The 1898 Whittelsey-Whittlesey genealogy preserves the line as: Simeon (d. 1571) - John (d. 1594) - John E. (d. 1623) - John the Immigrant. Modern scrutiny has shown that some of the genealogy's claims about the immigrant generation are weakly evidenced and that the Victorian compiler may have stitched together family tradition, parish entries, and inferential dating in ways that cannot now be verified. The dates for John E. should accordingly be understood as a family tradition rather than as a securely documented chain - while noting that the tradition has been the working assumption of all Whittlesey genealogy for more than a century.

A particularly interesting recent piece of research, contributed to the Pieces & Stems blog by an English researcher in 2024, identifies a candidate William Whittlesey of Huntingdon (born circa 1572, "of Huntingdon but likely born Godmanchester"), who married a Sarah Ginn around 1595 and produced a William Whittlesey junior born 1597. The senior William was described in 1603 as a "Gentleman" by his brother-in-law Richard Cannon; the junior William was probably educated at Huntingdon Grammar School at the same time as a Huntingdon boy named Oliver Cromwell (born 1599), under the famous Puritan schoolmaster Thomas Beard. The connection, if it can be proven, would place at least part of the wider Whittlesey kindred firmly within the Puritan gentry of Huntingdonshire in the years immediately surrounding John E.'s lifetime. The connection has not been demonstrated as a direct kinship, but it illustrates the milieu in which the surname was operating in the early Stuart period.

John E. married - the date, the parish, and the woman's name are not preserved in the genealogy in any form the modern researcher can verify - and fathered at least one son, John, born by tradition on 4 July 1623, in or near the town of Whittlesey in Cambridgeshire. (The recent research by "auntkatefirmin" of the Pieces & Stems blog has raised serious doubts about whether the immigrant John could have been born as early as 1623, given his marriage to Ruth Dudley in 1664 - it would mean he was 41 at first marriage, which would have been highly unusual in the seventeenth-century godly community. The most plausible reconciliation is that the 1623 date refers to the father's generation, or that the immigrant was the youngest of several children born to John E. before his death in 1623. The full story remains unresolved.)

What is not in question is that the line continued. A male heir survived. The infant son born around the year of John E.'s death carried the Whittlesey name forward, was raised - again, by mother, kinsmen, or guardians whose identities are not documented - into the convulsed England of Charles I, and was either himself the immigrant or the immediate father of the immigrant.

What Daily Life Likely Looked Like

A man of John E. Whittlesey's class, region, and generation lived a life recognisably continuous with that of his father and grandfather. The house was timber-framed and thatched, perhaps now with a brick chimney where the previous generation had had a smoke-hood. The agricultural calendar still ran by Michaelmas and Lady Day, ploughing in February, lambing in April, hay-making and peat-cutting in summer, harvest in August and September. The fenland economy still rested on the customary commons: eels, fish, fowl, summer pasture for cattle, the great geese-flocks of the levels.

What had changed - in his lifetime, and most of all in his last decade - was the encroachment of outside interests on this customary world. The Crown's increasing willingness to license drainage projects, the assertions of the Earl of Bedford and other great landowners that they should be entitled to the "improved" land after drainage, the slow legal erosion of common rights - all of these were live issues in the Cambridgeshire of the 1620s. Petitions from fenland communities to the Privy Council survive in considerable number from this decade; the King's Bench heard cases concerning the manorial rights of fen-edge villages; in Whittlesey itself, manorial court rolls (held now in the Cambridgeshire archives, though not yet fully indexed at the level of individual names) record the constant small negotiations over peat-rights, eel-traps, and grazing that defined fenland economic life.

Religious life ran on a pattern set by the Book of Common Prayer of 1604 (James's revision) and by the parish minister, whose theological character could vary enormously. A determined Puritan minister might preach for two hours on a Sunday morning, hold a Tuesday lecture, and impose a strict Sabbath observance; a conforming clergyman might do less. After 1611 the King James Bible was on every lectern, but in the literate yeoman households of East Anglia the Geneva Bible - with its Calvinist marginal notes - was still the family scripture. The catechism was drilled into children weekly. The great festivals were observed; the saints' days, much reduced from their pre-Reformation calendar, were noted; the parish year had a rhythm that any seventeenth-century English yeoman would have recognised as the basic shape of his life.

Travel was rare and bounded. Most men of John E.'s class might in a lifetime visit Cambridge once or twice, Peterborough perhaps more often, King's Lynn occasionally, London perhaps never. News came by traveller, by sermon, by the occasional pamphlet or broadside printed in London and carried up by chapmen, and (from the 1620s) by the early corantos that brought reports of the Thirty Years' War. The world widened slowly. But the news of the failure of the Spanish Match in 1623, the year of John E.'s death, would have reached every market town in England within weeks; the bells of celebration that rang out when Charles returned without his Spanish bride were the audible relief of a Protestant country that had feared a Catholic queen.

Marriage came in the middle to late twenties for men. If John E. married at the typical age and fathered the immigrant in 1623, he would have been about thirty, perhaps a few years older. The premature deaths in this Whittlesey line - Simeon at 29, John at 24, John E. at 30 - are a striking pattern. Whether they reflect a genuine male-line health issue (some inherited condition; some occupational hazard; some chain of accidents) or merely the brutal demographic reality of the period, in which young adult male mortality was high, cannot now be determined.

Death and Records

John E. Whittlesey died in 1623, in Cambridgeshire, at approximately thirty years of age. The cause of death is not documented. The early 1620s were difficult years in eastern England: there were serious plague outbreaks in 1623-1625 (the so-called "London plague" of 1625 was preceded by significant provincial outbreaks), and the bad harvests of 1622 had produced food shortages and elevated mortality across the country. Either disease or simple accident in the dangerous landscape of the fens - drowning in a flooded dyke, a horse-fall on a winter causeway, an infection from a working injury - could account for his death.

If his son was born on or near the traditional date of 4 July 1623, the timing is poignant. It is at least possible that John E. lived to see the child and died in the months or weeks afterward, or that he died in the spring or summer of 1623 before the birth and that the boy was a posthumous son. In either case the household passed into widowhood and the third consecutive Whittlesey infant inherited a name, a fragment of memory, and not much else.

His burial entry, if it has survived, has not been located in any of the indexed and digitised parish registers presently accessible. Both Whittlesey St Mary and Whittlesey St Andrew have registers extending back into the sixteenth century, with significant gaps and condition issues; the registers of the surrounding fen-edge parishes in Cambridgeshire and Huntingdonshire have varying degrees of survival; the 1641 Protestation Returns for Cambridgeshire, which might have caught the next generation of the family, are themselves missing. The 1898 Whittelsey-Whittlesey genealogy gives the dates without preserving a contemporary citation; like his father's record, John E.'s entry is best understood as a transmitted family tradition that may or may not rest on a Victorian sighting of a now-lost register entry, a family Bible, or an inferential calculation backward from the immigrant.

What survived was the boy. Whether born in 1623 as the genealogy states or somewhat later as modern critical analysis suggests, the infant John Whittlesey - the man who would cross the Atlantic, hold the ferry contract on the Connecticut River at Saybrook in 1662, marry Ruth Dudley in 1664, and become the ancestor of every American Whittlesey - was the bridge from the Cambridgeshire fens to the New World. Without John E.'s thirty years, that bridge would not exist.

The Bridge to the Immigrant Generation

It is worth pausing at this point in the family chronology, because John E. Whittlesey stands at the precise pivot of the line. He is the last Whittlesey ancestor whose entire life was lived in England. His son is the first whose adult life was lived in America. The reasons that families like his began, in his generation and in the decades immediately following his death, to consider departing for the New World repay careful attention.

The push factors in eastern England in the 1620s and 1630s were multiple and overlapping. Religious factors loomed largest in the contemporary self-understanding of those who went. The rise of Arminian and ceremonialist clergy under Charles I (from 1625) and especially under Archbishop Laud (from 1633) was perceived by godly families across East Anglia as a slow Catholicising of the English Church. The enforcement of the surplice and the altar rail, the suppression of Puritan town lectureships, the persecution of nonconforming ministers like Thomas Hooker and John Cotton - these were felt as direct attacks on the religious life of the eastern counties. Economic factors mattered too. The collapse of the East Anglian cloth industry in the 1620s left thousands of artisanal families without work; the squeeze of inflation on fixed leasehold incomes hit the rural middling sort; the drainage controversies in the fens threatened the customary commons on which families like the Whittleseys partly depended. Political factors compounded the religious and the economic: the dissolution of the 1629 Parliament and the beginning of Charles's Personal Rule, the imposition of Ship Money, the perceived corruption of the Caroline court, the disastrous handling of the Scottish Bishops' Wars from 1638 - all of these convinced a great many serious English Protestants that the country was being lost.

The first response was internal migration: from the countryside to godly market towns, from East Anglia to London, from troubled parishes to those that still had a faithful preacher. The second response, for those whose conscience and resources permitted it, was emigration. Between 1620 (Mayflower) and 1642 (the outbreak of the Civil War, which abruptly halted the migration), the Great Migration carried perhaps 20,000 English men and women to New England. The map of their origins shows a striking concentration in East Anglia: Suffolk, Norfolk, Essex, Cambridgeshire, Huntingdonshire, Lincolnshire. The Whittlesey country was at the heart of the catchment.

The traditional date of 1635 for the arrival of John Whittlesey the Immigrant in New England, if accepted, would place him in the Winthrop-fleet phase of the migration - the cohort that included John Cotton (1633), Thomas Hooker (1633), and the great wave of Massachusetts Bay arrivals. Modern critical analysis (most rigorously by "auntkatefirmin" of Pieces & Stems) suggests that 1635 is unlikely - a twelve-year-old would not normally have travelled alone, and the absence of any Whittlesey kin in the surviving New England records of that decade is hard to explain. A later arrival, perhaps with the post-1648 Saybrook settlement, may be more plausible. Either way, the immigrant's English youth was shaped by the very tensions that had begun in John E.'s adulthood: a Cambridgeshire raised on Puritan preaching, a fenland under threat of drainage and enclosure, a country slipping into political and religious crisis. He carried that world with him across the ocean.

John E. Whittlesey did not himself emigrate. He died, in Cambridgeshire, in 1623, before the great migration began. But his life is, in a real sense, the bridge that made it possible. He kept a fragile male line alive through two consecutive generations of orphaned infancy. He passed the family forward into the world of Charles I, the world that would produce the immigrant, the world that would produce New England.

  • Name (as it appears in surviving records): John Whittelsey, also rendered "Whittilsey," "Whittlese," "Whittlesey," "Whittelse," and a half-dozen other phonetic variants typical of seventeenth-century clerks who wrote what they heard rather than what they saw.
  • Traditional birth year: 1623, given by family tradition and by the nineteenth-century compiler Charles Barney Whittelsey. A handful of more cautious modern researchers place his birth closer to the 1630s or even 1640, on the grounds that a man born in 1623 would have been unusually old (forty-one) at his 1664 marriage. The traditional date is preserved here because it is the date passed down within the Whittelsey line, but the reader should understand that it is family tradition rather than parish proof.

Born into Jacobean England (1623)

The England into which John Whittelsey was born in 1623 was a kingdom on a slow fuse. James I — the Scottish king who had inherited Elizabeth's throne in 1603 — had been on the English throne twenty years; he would die in 1625 and be succeeded by his son Charles I, whose collisions with Parliament would in due course set fire to the entire constitutional order. The King James Bible, first printed in 1611, had become the household book of every literate Protestant family. William Shakespeare had been dead seven years. The Pilgrims had been in Plymouth for three. Galileo was about to publish Il Saggiatore in Rome. The Thirty Years' War, then in its fifth year, was draining the German lands of blood.

In the eastern English county of Cambridgeshire, where the Whittelseys had taken their name from the fen-town of Whittlesey (about seven miles east of Peterborough), the seventeenth century did not arrive in a single dramatic moment. It arrived in the slow Protestant tightening of parish life, in the steady draining of the fens by Dutch engineers under Cornelius Vermuyden, in the rising fortunes of an emphatically Puritan gentry, and in the steady migration of country people toward London and toward the new colonial ports of Boston, Salem, and the Connecticut River. Whittlesey itself sat on a low island of higher ground in the fens — an old Bronze Age settlement site, still partly dependent on eel-fishing and on the seasonal cropping of reclaimed land. Records of the name "Whittlesey" (or "Whittlesea," or "Whittilsey," with the spelling drifting at the clerk's whim) cluster across a band of villages running west from Whittlesey itself toward Huntingdon and Godmanchester: Colne, Bluntisham, Woodhurst, Pidley cum Fenton, Graveley, Chatteris, Cottenham. By the 1620s the surname was already a regional rather than a single-village name, and its bearers had begun to spread across the county lines.

Family tradition recorded by Charles Barney Whittelsey in 1898 places the immigrant's parents in this country at this date. The father is given as John E. Whittelsey (1593-1623) — a man who, if the tradition is right, died in the very year his son was born, leaving the boy as a posthumous child. The mother is given as Lydia Terry, said to have married John Whittelsey at "St. Anne's" parish in London in October 1621/22. Modern researchers have not been able to confirm either side of this. The most thorough recent review of the citations in the 1898 genealogy notes that the original footnote points to a "St. Anne's" without telling us which St. Anne's of London — and that the digitized registers of the two best candidates (St. Anne, Blackfriars, and St. Anne, Soho) either do not contain the entry or do not yet exist for the period in question. We are therefore on the honest ground of saying: family tradition holds that John Whittelsey's father, also John, died in 1623, leaving Lydia Terry Whittelsey to raise the boy alone. The contemporary parish documentation that would confirm this has not yet been found.

If the tradition is true, it makes a particular kind of sense. A posthumous boy — fatherless from birth, raised by a widow and perhaps eventually by stepfather or kin — was exactly the sort of young man who, two decades later, would be a strong candidate for emigration. He carried no inherited land, no family business that needed him at home, and no settled position in the parish hierarchy. He was free to leave, and the Atlantic crossing rewarded those who were free to leave.

The Decision to Emigrate

We do not know, and probably never will know, exactly when John Whittelsey crossed the Atlantic. The records do not survive.

Three traditions have grown up. The earliest — repeated by the 1898 Whittelsey-Whittlesey genealogy — associates him with the Saye-and-Sele Company and an arrival in 1635, in connection with the founding of the Saybrook Colony at the mouth of the Connecticut River by John Winthrop the Younger. This is romantically tidy. It is also, as cautious genealogists have noted, almost certainly wrong. The Saye-and-Sele patentees were aristocratic Oxfordshire Puritans, not Cambridgeshire fen-people, and there is no surviving Company list in which a Whittelsey appears. A twelve-year-old boy travelling alone across the Atlantic in 1635, then remaining unmarried until age forty-one in 1664, would be so deeply atypical of Puritan New England custom that it strains credulity.

A second tradition — visible already in the first (1885) edition of the family genealogy and revived by more recent researchers — places his arrival in Saybrook "some time after" a town meeting held in 1648. This is the date one finds on the better-researched genealogical sites today. It dovetails with the fact that John's name appears on no list of the original 1648 Saybrook proprietors but begins to appear in town affairs in the 1650s and 1660s.

A third tradition — preserved in family lore but not yet documented — has him arriving as a boy in 1632, indentured for a term of service, perhaps in the company of one or two older brothers or an uncle. None of these supposed kinsmen, if they existed, left descendants in New England; only John did. The 1632 date, like the 1635 date, runs into the problem of his late marriage.

The most honest summary is this: John Whittelsey arrived in the Connecticut Colony at some point between the Great Migration of the 1630s and the late 1650s. He came from Cambridgeshire. He came alone, or at least his American descent comes through him alone. He came as a young man with a usable trade — tannery and shoemaking — and within a few years of his arrival he is found established at one of the most strategic transportation choke-points in the entire Connecticut Colony: the ferry crossing of the Connecticut River at Saybrook Point.

The motives for his crossing belong to the wider pattern of the Great Migration, that twenty-year movement (roughly 1620-1640) in which something on the order of 21,000 English men, women, and children crossed to New England. They came for a tangled bundle of reasons: religious — the determination of Puritan Dissenters to plant a "city upon a hill" beyond the reach of Archbishop William Laud's persecuting Church of England; political — the slow-burning conflict between Charles I and his Parliaments that would erupt into civil war in 1642; economic — agricultural depression, the collapse of the textile trades, the engrossment of common land; and personal — the universal lure of a fresh start. John Whittelsey, born in 1623, came of age precisely as the Great Migration was reaching its peak. Whatever the exact year of his crossing, he was a child of that movement.

Arrival in the Connecticut Colony

The Connecticut Colony that received John Whittelsey was a young, scrappy, and still partly Native-controlled place. To understand his position there, it helps to walk briefly through how Connecticut had come to exist.

In 1633 the Dutch West India Company built a small fortified trading post on what is now the south bank of Hartford, called the Huys de Hoop (House of Hope), to secure their share of the rich Connecticut River fur trade. Within months English traders from Plymouth, and then English Puritans pressing west from Massachusetts Bay, were planting rival settlements upstream — Windsor in 1633, Wethersfield in 1634, Hartford in 1635-36.

At the river's mouth, in November 1635, John Winthrop the Younger arrived as the appointed governor of a new colony patented to a group of Puritan grandees — Viscount Saye and Sele, Baron Brooke, John Hampden, John Pym, and others — under what is usually called the Warwick Patent. Winthrop's chief military engineer, the Dutch-trained soldier Lion Gardiner, came ashore at Saybrook Point on 24 November 1635 with eighteen men and a few women and children, and immediately began building Fort Saybrook. Gardiner's wife Mary gave birth to a son, David, during the construction — the first European child born in Connecticut.

The infant Saybrook Colony immediately found itself at the edge of the Pequot War (1636-1637). Lion Gardiner defended Fort Saybrook through a long siege; the war ended in May 1637 with the catastrophic destruction of the Pequot fortified town at Mystic, in which several hundred Pequot men, women, and children were killed by a combined English-Mohegan-Narragansett force under Captain John Mason. The defeat of the Pequots cleared the southern Connecticut shore for English settlement and, in a brutal sense, opened the way for the next generation of arrivals — among them, eventually, John Whittelsey.

In 1644 the Saybrook Colony was sold to the Connecticut Colony at Hartford. Fort Saybrook itself burned in 1647. By 1648 — the year that surfaces in the better-researched accounts as the probable arrival date for John Whittelsey — Saybrook was no longer a frontier garrison but a settled river-mouth town: a planting community of perhaps a few dozen households along what would become Main Street, with farms running back from the river, salt meadows, oyster beds, a meeting-house, a minister, and an extremely strategic position at the place where the Connecticut River met Long Island Sound. Every ship sailing up the river to Hartford, every traveller moving along the coast between the New Haven Colony and the Narragansett country, every drove of cattle being moved between the eastern and western banks of the river, came through Saybrook.

It was into this town, somewhere between the late 1640s and the late 1650s, that the young John Whittelsey walked, carrying his tanner's knives and his shoemaker's awls.

Saybrook and the Ferry on the Connecticut River

The Connecticut River at Saybrook is wide — close to a mile across at the crossing point — and tidal, with strong currents and a notoriously shifting sandbar at its mouth. For colonial Connecticut, it was the single largest obstacle to east-west travel along the coast. There were no bridges. There would be no bridge at any point on the lower Connecticut River for nearly two and a half centuries. Until the railroad arrived in the 1870s, the only way across, for foot-traveller, horse, cow, cart, or merchant, was a ferry.

The General Court of the Connecticut Colony, sitting at Hartford, addressed this in 1662, the same year in which the colony's royal charter from Charles II was being secured by John Winthrop the Younger in London. The Court authorized the establishment of an official ferry from Saybrook Point (then sometimes called Tilley's Point, on the west bank of the river) across to what is now the town of Lyme on the east bank. To run this ferry the Court named two men: John Whittelsey of Saybrook, and William Dudley of Guilford.

This is the first absolutely firm documentary appearance of John Whittelsey in the New World. By 1662 he is already a settled and trusted inhabitant of Saybrook — trusted enough to be entrusted, jointly with a substantial Guilford planter, with a public franchise that was simultaneously a public service, a tolled business, and a strategic asset of the colony.

The partnership with William Dudley is no accident, and it is the key to understanding the next sixty years of Whittelsey family life. Dudley — who had emigrated from Surrey to Guilford in 1639 with the Whitfield party — had a daughter named Ruth. Within two years of the ferry contract, John Whittelsey would marry her.

The ferry the two men ran was a primitive thing. There was no wharf on the Saybrook side for nearly sixty years; passengers waded ashore in the shallows, and cattle were simply driven into and out of the water. The boats themselves were flat-bottomed scows — large enough to take a horse and cart, propelled by oar or sweep or, with a favourable wind, by a single square sail. In bad weather, in fog, against the tide, or against the rip at the river's mouth, the crossing could be genuinely dangerous; in good weather it was a chore of an hour or so. Tolls were set by the General Court, not by the operators. The Whittelseys and Dudleys held the franchise; the colony underwrote the obligation to provide service.

The ferry would remain associated with the Whittelsey family for several generations — long after the deaths of both founding operators. Today the road on which it ran is still called Ferry Road, and the cove on the river is still called Ferry Point. The John Whittlesey Jr. House — built by the immigrant's son, with a rear ell that dendrochronologists date to about 1693, making it one of the very oldest surviving timber-framed buildings in Connecticut — still stands at 40 Ferry Road, listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It is the most tangible single surviving relic of the immigrant's American life.

Marriage to Ruth Dudley (1664)

On 20 June 1664, at Saybrook, John Whittelsey married Ruth Dudley. She was nineteen years old, having been born on 20 April 1645 at Guilford. He was — by the traditional reckoning — forty-one. By the cautious modern reckoning, he was somewhere in his middle twenties to early thirties.

Ruth's father, William Dudley, was a substantial figure. Baptized in January 1608/9 at Dorking in Surrey, he had married Jane Lutman on 24 August 1636 at Ockley, Surrey, and had emigrated with the Reverend Henry Whitfield's party that founded Guilford on the Long Island Sound shore in 1639. He was one of the original signers of the Guilford Covenant, a planter of substance, and by the 1660s a man trusted enough to be a Saybrook ferry partner with his future son-in-law. His will, drawn up on 22 December 1676 (proved at his death in March 1684), contained the now-famous clause: "I give and bequeath to John Whittlese, which married my daughter Ruth Dudlye…" This is the document that fixes the relationship in surviving paper. Jane Lutman Dudley had died in 1674, two years before her husband drew up the will.

The marriage anchored John Whittelsey in the kinship web of the southern Connecticut Puritan gentry. The Dudleys were connected, by marriage and neighbourhood, to the Leetes, the Wards, the Scrantons, and most of the founding families of Guilford and Branford. To marry a Dudley was to step from being a single newcomer with a useful trade into being a man with in-laws, with cousins, with backing.

It also gave him a home. By the time of the marriage, or shortly after, John had built a homestead on what is now Ferry Road, on the slope above the ferry landing. It would have been a one-and-a-half-story timber-framed house of post-and-beam construction, with a massive central stone chimney and (in the earliest version) probably two rooms downstairs flanking the chimney — hall and parlor — and a sleeping loft above. The tannery and shoemaker's shop almost certainly stood as outbuildings: a tannery, for obvious reasons of smell and water supply, was always sited away from the main house and near a stream.

Trade as Tanner and Shoemaker

Tanning and shoemaking in seventeenth-century New England were complementary, hard, indispensable trades. Tanning converted the rawhide of cattle, deer, and (occasionally) horse into durable leather. The process took the better part of a year. Hides were first soaked in lime to loosen hair and flesh, scraped clean on a beam with a curved two-handed knife, then layered in pits with alternating beds of crushed oak or hemlock bark and water. The tannin in the bark slowly, over six to twelve months, bound to the proteins of the hide and converted it from skin into leather. The finished leather was then dressed with fish oil or tallow, dried, and beaten supple.

A tannery announced itself for a quarter-mile around by its smell. It needed running water (Saybrook had a number of suitable small streams running down to the river), an oak forest within a reasonable haul (Connecticut had no shortage), and the patience to wait out a year-long inventory cycle.

A shoemaker (or "cordwainer") then converted that leather into shoes, boots, harness, saddles, leather buckets, leather aprons, leather jerkins, and the hundred other leather objects that a pre-industrial society depended on. In a town the size of seventeenth-century Saybrook, a man who was both tanner and shoemaker was effectively the leather industry: he could buy his raw material from the farmers, prepare it himself, and sell the finished goods over the same counter. Combined with a half-share in the colony's largest ferry franchise, this gave John Whittelsey a remarkably solid economic footing.

It also tied him to the rhythms of the agricultural year. Cattle were slaughtered in late autumn; hides were laid down in the tanpits through the winter; the previous year's hides came out, finished, in the late summer and autumn, just in time for the demand for new shoes and harness for the coming winter. A tanner-shoemaker's calendar was a year-long calendar, and a successful one — like John Whittelsey — was a man who could plan, who could lay aside capital, and who could be trusted, by ferry passengers and shoe customers alike, to be exactly where he said he would be.

The Ten Children — A Puritan Household

Between 1665 and roughly 1690, John and Ruth Whittelsey had ten children. Eight were sons; two were daughters. All bore Old- and New-Testament names — a marker, in seventeenth-century New England, of an emphatic and deliberate Puritan identity, in contrast to the older English custom of repeating family names down the generations.

In order generally accepted by the family genealogy, the children were:

  1. Stephen — born about 1667; lived a remarkably long life (he is said in some sources to have died about 1760, in his nineties).
  2. Ebenezer — a name from 1 Samuel meaning "stone of help."
  3. Joseph — the patriarch from Genesis.
  4. Josiah — the boy king of Judah.
  5. Jabez — the obscure but blessed figure of 1 Chronicles.
  6. David — the great king of Israel.
  7. Eliphalet — meaning "God of deliverance"; this is the son through whom the line traced in the present biographical project descends.
  8. Samuel — born early 1686, graduated from Yale College in 1705, ordained a Congregational minister; the most academically distinguished of the brothers.
  9. Ruth — named for her mother.
  10. Sarah — named for the matriarch.

Each Sunday this household — when complete — filled an entire pew in the meeting-house of the First Church of Christ in Saybrook. The choice of names is itself a piece of theology. Where an English household of the early 1600s might have run to a William named for the grandfather, a Thomas named for the uncle, a Mary named for the great-aunt — knitting the present generation into the chain of the past — the Whittelsey household named its children entirely out of Scripture. The children were given to the Lord, not to the lineage. This was the Puritan way.

It is worth pausing on the survival of the household. Ten children, all of whom (so far as we can tell from the genealogy) lived to adulthood and married, in an age of catarrhal fevers, smallpox, throat distempers, dysentery, and the routine hazards of childbirth, is a striking outcome. It bespeaks a household with adequate food, clean water, a healthy mother, and good local conditions. Saybrook in the second half of the seventeenth century — coastal, well-watered, far enough from major population centres to be spared the worst urban epidemics — was a comparatively healthy place to raise children.

Life in Puritan New England

What did the rhythm of life in the Whittelsey household actually look like?

The day began before dawn. The hearth fire, banked at night under ash, was raked open by Ruth or one of the older girls; the iron pot of corn-meal mush was set to cook; the men headed out to the tannery, to the ferry, or to the fields. Breakfast — usually mush with milk, sometimes with molasses, sometimes with the previous day's salt pork — was eaten quickly. The forenoon belonged to work: hides scraped, bark crushed, shoes pegged, customers ferried, salt hay cut, corn hoed, firewood split, soap made.

Sunday was different. On Saturday night the entire household scrubbed, swept, banked the fire, and laid out clean linen. From sundown Saturday to sundown Sunday — the Puritan Sabbath, kept on the older Old Testament reckoning — no work, no travel, no play, no idle talk was permitted. On Sunday morning the family walked together to the meeting-house. The Reverend Thomas Buckingham, who served the First Church of Christ in Saybrook for much of the second half of the seventeenth century, preached morning and afternoon sermons that ran an hour and more apiece. The congregation sat on hard benches, men on one side and women on the other, with the older boys often herded into a balcony where a tithing-man with a long stick patrolled to keep them awake. Between services, families ate cold bread and cheese in the meeting-house yard.

The Whittelseys were also embedded in civil New England. The Connecticut Charter of 1662 — secured by John Winthrop the Younger from Charles II — established Connecticut as a self-governing colony, with its General Court electing its own governor and assembly, an unusual degree of practical autonomy. Local affairs were settled in town meeting: every adult male freeman gathered, usually annually, to choose selectmen, constables, fence-viewers, hog-reeves, tithing-men, and the rest. The ferry franchise itself — the very thing that made John Whittelsey's economic life possible — was a town and colony grant, renewed and adjusted in town meeting and in General Court.

The seventeenth-century Saybrook into which John Whittelsey's children were born was also a town that took its education with extraordinary seriousness for a place of its size. The "Saybrook Platform," adopted in 1708 (four years after John's death) at a synod of Connecticut Congregational ministers held at Saybrook, would standardize the doctrine and discipline of Connecticut's churches for the next century. The Collegiate School, founded in 1701 and located briefly at Saybrook before moving to New Haven in 1716 and being renamed Yale College, met for some of its earliest sessions a short walk from the Whittelsey door. John's son Samuel was a member of the Yale class of 1705 — meaning he was among the very first cohort of students that the Collegiate School produced. The immigrant lived to see his son graduate from the institution that would become Yale.

Wider Events: King Philip's War, the Charter Oak, the Glorious Revolution

John Whittelsey's adult life in Saybrook spanned the four most defining political events of seventeenth-century New England, after the Pequot War of his arrival. He lived through all four.

King Philip's War (1675-1676). In the summer of 1675, the Wampanoag sachem Metacomet — known to the English as King Philip — led an uprising of Wampanoags, Nipmucs, and Narragansetts against the steadily encroaching English settlements of southern New England. By the proportional measures of the colonial population, King Philip's War remains the bloodiest war in American history. About 600 English colonists were killed; twelve English towns were utterly destroyed; many more were damaged. Native losses were proportionately even greater. The Connecticut Colony, including Saybrook, supplied militia under Major Robert Treat to the combined New England Confederation forces; the decisive engagement, the "Great Swamp Fight" of December 1675, in which a Narragansett winter fort in Rhode Island was stormed and burned, included Connecticut troops. Saybrook itself was not attacked — the eastern Connecticut shore was relatively spared — but the war's anxieties shaped every household. Travel between towns, including ferry crossings, had to be guarded. Stockades were strengthened. Powder and shot were stockpiled. A man of fifty (as John traditionally was during the war) was past the prime militia age but would still have been expected to muster, watch, and ward.

The Charter Oak (1687). Twelve years later, the political weather changed again. King James II, on the throne in London, was determined to consolidate the New England colonies into a single royal Dominion of New England under Sir Edmund Andros. On 31 October 1687, Andros rode into Hartford to demand the surrender of Connecticut's beloved 1662 Charter. According to tradition, during the evening meeting, candles were suddenly snuffed; in the darkness, Captain Joseph Wadsworth carried off the parchment and hid it in the hollow trunk of a great white oak on the Wyllys estate — the Charter Oak that became a permanent emblem of Connecticut liberty. Whether or not the candle-snuffing literally happened, the charter was indeed concealed, and Connecticut's tradition of self-government survived.

The Glorious Revolution (1688-89). A year later, James II was driven from the English throne by William of Orange and Mary. When the news reached Boston in the spring of 1689, the people of Boston rose, arrested Edmund Andros, threw him in prison, and effectively dissolved the Dominion of New England. Connecticut quietly produced its hidden charter and resumed self-government. John Whittelsey, by then a man in his sixties, lived through the entire affair from the safety of his Saybrook homestead.

Witchcraft. It is worth noting in passing that the Salem witch trials of 1692 took place in Massachusetts during John Whittelsey's later life. Connecticut had had its own witchcraft prosecutions in the 1650s and 1660s — the so-called "Hartford witch panic" of 1662-63 — but by the 1690s Connecticut's courts had largely turned away from witchcraft accusations. There is no surviving record of any Whittelsey involvement, on either side, in any witch trial.

The cumulative effect of these decades on a man like John Whittelsey was, one suspects, to deepen a settled Puritan understanding of Providence. Wars came, kings rose and fell, charters were demanded and hidden — and through it all, the ferry crossed the river, the hides went into the tanpits, the children were born, the Sabbath was kept, and the family endured.

Death and Burial (1704)

John Whittelsey died in 1704 at Saybrook in the Connecticut Colony. By the traditional reckoning of his birth year, he was eighty-one — a long life by any seventeenth-century standard. His widow Ruth survived him by a decade, dying in 1714 at the age of sixty-nine.

The exact date of his death has not survived in any of the records currently accessible; the parish and town records of Saybrook from that period are notoriously incomplete, in part because of a fire at New London that destroyed some of the central probate archive. What survives is the family tradition of the year, confirmed by the absence of his signature on any document after 1704 and by the appearance of his sons in subsequent town transactions.

He is commemorated by an obelisk in the old Saybrook burying ground, in a fenced area that was restored by his descendants in the early twenty-first century (a photograph of the obelisk, taken at the 2015 Whittlesey family reunion after the restoration of the fencing, survives in the genealogical record). The obelisk is not a seventeenth-century stone — it is a much later memorial erected by the family — but it stands at or very near the place of his original interment.

The probate of his estate, if it survives, would presumably show the disposition of the Ferry Road homestead, the tannery, the shoemaker's tools, and his half-share in the ferry franchise. The ferry continued in Whittelsey-Dudley family hands for several more generations.

Legacy in the American Whittlesey Line

Every Whittlesey or Whittelsey in the United States today who can trace an unbroken male line to the seventeenth century traces it to this man.

John Whittelsey is, in the most literal sense, the immigrant. No other Whittelsey of this surname is known to have crossed the Atlantic in the seventeenth century and left American descendants. Whatever brothers or kinsmen may have travelled with him (and the family lore is contradictory on this point) either left no descendants or left descendants under other surnames through their daughters. The American Whittlesey line, in all its branches — the Connecticut branch, the Vermont branch, the western New York branch, the Ohio and Wisconsin and Oregon branches that the nineteenth-century westward movement would scatter — all of it descends from the ten children of John and Ruth Dudley Whittelsey of Saybrook.

Among the immediate sons:

  • Stephen carried the line on at Saybrook itself.
  • Eliphalet carried the line through which the Whittlesey family of this biographical project descends — eventually into the New York and Ohio and ultimately western American chapters of the family story.
  • Samuel became one of the most notable Yale graduates of the early eighteenth century and was ordained as a Congregational minister, founding a clerical branch of the family that produced several more eighteenth-century ministers.
  • Joseph and Josiah and Jabez and David and Ebenezer all married, all had children, and all carried the name forward into the eighteenth century.

In Old Saybrook itself, the Whittelsey footprint is still visible after three hundred and twenty years. Ferry Road still runs from the old town centre to the river. Ferry Point still marks the ferry landing. The John Whittlesey Jr. House at 40 Ferry Road — built by the immigrant's son around 1693 and rebuilt over the original rear ell about 1750 — was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1984 and is one of the oldest surviving timber-framed structures in the state of Connecticut. The Ambrose Whittlesey House at 14 Main Street, Old Saybrook, home of a later descendant, is likewise on the National Register. The First Church of Christ in Saybrook, where the immigrant and his family worshipped, is still a working congregation.

A man whose own English origins are obscure enough that we cannot say with certainty in what year he was born, in what year he crossed the Atlantic, or what ship he came on, nevertheless left a footprint on the American landscape that is still walkable, photographable, and visitable in the twenty-first century. The fen-village name of Whittlesey, carried in one boy's pocket across three thousand miles of Atlantic in the middle of the seventeenth century, is now scattered across half the cities and small towns of the United States. Every one of those carriers is descended, through one of those ten Biblical-named children, from the same tanner, shoemaker, and ferryman of Saybrook Point.

Childhood and Family

Eliphalet Whittlesey was born on July 24, 1679, in the small but strategically vital settlement of Saybrook at the mouth of the Connecticut River. He was the seventh of the eight sons born to John Whittlesey, the English immigrant who had crossed the Atlantic as a boy in 1635 with the Lords Say and Sele Company, and to Ruth Dudley, daughter of William Dudley of Guilford. The Whittlesey household by 1679 was already one of the most numerous families along the lower Connecticut, and Eliphalet entered a domestic world thick with brothers, cattle, ferry traffic, hides, oak bark, and the salty breeze of Long Island Sound.

The family seat in those years was the ferry house at Saybrook Point. John Whittlesey the elder had been granted the ferry over the Connecticut River between Saybrook and Lyme in 1662, and he ran it for the rest of his life. The ferry house was simultaneously a working tavern, a customs post for travelers and post-riders, a tannery yard, and the family's residence. Eliphalet was therefore raised in one of the more public dwellings of the colony, in earshot of the meeting house, near the burying ground where his father would later be laid in 1704, and within walking distance of the bark-stripped oak logs and the lime-and-tan pits where hides were converted to leather.

The Whittlesey boys grew up in the rough, communal style of large Puritan families on the frontier of European New England. Eliphalet's earliest years coincided with the violent and uncertain decade following King Philip's War (1675-78), in which Saybrook had stood as the southernmost military pivot of Connecticut Colony. By the time he was old enough to handle a fleshing knife or an oar, the colony was again grappling with insecurity: King William's War broke out in 1689, when he was ten, and Saybrook served as a watchful coastal outpost throughout his teen years. The first words of a boy's prayers in such a household would have been the Lord's Prayer, the Westminster Shorter Catechism, and a Psalm; the first chores would have been carrying water, splitting kindling, and helping the older brothers haul hides from the ferry landing to the tan pits behind the house.

Eliphalet was baptized in the First Church of Saybrook, where the family belonged. The Saybrook Platform - the famous 1708 statement of Congregational consociation - would be drafted by ministers gathered just a stone's throw from where Eliphalet had played as a child, and the religious atmosphere of his upbringing was the strict, learned, sermon-saturated Calvinism of the late Stuart era. He learned to read from the Bible and the New England Primer; his arithmetic came from the ledgers his father kept for the ferry tolls and the hide trade.

Adult Life and Trade

Eliphalet learned the tanner's trade at his father's side. Tanning in late seventeenth-century Connecticut was a slow, smelly, capital-intensive craft, and a tanner was a man of substance in any village. The process required pits dug into the ground and lined with oak plank; vast quantities of hemlock or oak bark, ground in a horse-driven bark mill; a steady supply of fresh hides from local farmers and butchers; and the patience to leave skins soaking in graduated baths of liquor for a year or more before they could be curried into useable leather. A tanner needed a stream nearby for washing, a good well, draft animals, and a network of credit-keeping relationships with shoemakers, harness-makers, glovers, and saddlers. Because hides were heavy and cash was scarce, much of the work was conducted on barter, with leather flowing out and shoes, harness, and finished goods flowing back in.

Eliphalet served his apprenticeship throughout the 1690s, coming to majority around 1700. In Connecticut at that date, a younger son of a large family had limited options for inheritance, and Eliphalet, the seventh son, would have known from the beginning that he would have to find his own land. Saybrook's improved acres were largely spoken for. Like many of his generation - including several of his elder brothers - he looked upriver, toward the more recently settled country around Hartford and Wethersfield, where new ecclesiastical societies were being carved out of the great west commons of older towns.

The pivotal move came around 1709. By that date his elder brother Jabez Whittlesey (b. 1675) had already removed to the western part of Wethersfield, in the area then variously called "Pipestave Swamp," "Cow Plain," and "West Farms." Eliphalet purchased land from Jabez and built a small one-story, two-room dwelling on what was then called 20 Rod Highway - today 461 Maple Hill Avenue in Newington. This was the simplest sort of New England house: a single chamber on each side of a center chimney, perhaps with a sleeping loft above and a lean-to behind. From this modest base, Eliphalet would spend the next half-century turning a tract of stony upland into a working farm with leather works.

The west parish of Wethersfield in those years was a thin scattering of farms across rolling glacial till, watered by Mill Brook and the streams flowing southeast toward the Connecticut River. Soils were good in places, marginal in others; the higher ground (Maple Hill, Cedar Mountain) was wooded with chestnut, hickory, and oak - the very oak whose bark would furnish his tanning liquor. Eliphalet's farm was thus well situated for the combined business he intended to run: cattle for hides and meat, oak woodlots for bark and timber, a stream for the wash water, and a position close enough to Wethersfield village (about four miles) and to Hartford (about seven) for marketing his leather.

He appears in the records of the day as a yeoman and as a craftsman, and his name is among the founding generation of the West Society. When the people of West Wethersfield petitioned the General Court for parish status in December 1708 - the very year before Eliphalet built his house - he was one of the men whose growing presence in that quarter made a separate ecclesiastical society necessary. The petition was granted in 1713 as the West Society, renamed "Newington" in May 1721, and gathered as a particular Church of Christ on October 3, 1722. Eliphalet, then forty-three, was already an established householder by the time the church was organized and would have been among the male communicants who voted in 1722 to call the Rev. Elisha Williams (later Rector of Yale College) as their first minister.

Marriage and Children

On December 1, 1702, at Saybrook, Eliphalet Whittlesey married Mary Pratt. Mary had been born on May 24, 1677, at Saybrook, and was therefore a few months past her twenty-fifth birthday at marriage; Eliphalet had just turned twenty-three. The Pratts were a long-established Connecticut River family, and a Pratt-Whittlesey union was the kind of solid alliance between neighbors that characterized Puritan endogamy along the lower river. The marriage was performed in Saybrook, and the couple probably spent their first years in the Whittlesey ferry-house orbit before removing upriver around 1709 with at least one or two small children already in hand.

The 1898 genealogy by Charles Barney Whittelsey lists Eliphalet's family in the standard Connecticut form. The best-documented child of Eliphalet I and Mary (Pratt) Whittlesey was the son who carried his father's name: Eliphalet Whittlesey II, born May 10, 1714, at Newington. This Eliphalet II would marry Dorothy Kellogg, raise nine children, serve as a Captain in the French and Indian War, and remove with most of his family to the new town of Washington in Litchfield County in 1761 - the year before the elder couple's house began to pass to other hands. Several other children are recorded in the genealogy but were less prominent in the public record.

For Mary Pratt herself, the surviving record is thin, as it is for most colonial Connecticut wives. She was the practical center of the household for half a century: spinner of wool and flax, weaver, dyer, cheesemaker, candle-dipper, herbal physician, midwife to neighbors, gardener of the kitchen garden, and the manager of the cycle of births and deaths in a busy farmhouse. Children born and lost, the daily measuring out of meal, the year-on-year pattern of planting, lambing, slaughtering, soap-making, and Sabbath-keeping - this was Mary's life, and her labor underwrote her husband's emergence as a tanner of standing in the west society.

The Colony of Connecticut in His Era

Eliphalet I's seventy-eight years (1679-1757) almost exactly mapped the long colonial middle period of Connecticut. He was born in the reign of Charles II, came of age under William and Mary, married in the early years of Queen Anne's War, built his Newington house under George I, raised his children under George II, and died at the very outbreak of the climactic French and Indian War. Connecticut in those decades grew from a thin string of riverside towns into a populous, prosperous, and self-governing colony. Population multiplied perhaps tenfold over his lifetime; new towns were carved out of old ones in a regular cadence; congregational meeting houses replaced rough first-generation log structures with frame buildings of remarkable carpentry; and the colony's leather, livestock, lumber, and provisions began moving in volume down the Connecticut River to the West Indies trade.

The expansion of which Eliphalet was a part - the slow drift of younger sons from the riverside towns upcountry into the second tier of settlement - was the engine of eighteenth-century Connecticut. Newington itself was a textbook case: granted to Wethersfield families as "West Farms," settled in a piecemeal way in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, parish-organized in 1713, church-gathered in 1722, and pursuing town status only much later (Newington became an independent town only in 1871). For most of Eliphalet's life he lived in a place that was administratively part of Wethersfield but functionally a distinct village. His tannery and farm contributed directly to the colony's commercial development in shoes and harness - an indispensable industrial component of a horse-drawn, leather-clad agricultural economy.

He lived through Queen Anne's War (1702-1713), King George's War (1744-48), and saw the prelude to the great war that broke out in 1754. He paid colonial rates, mustered with the local militia in his prime, drilled on Wethersfield's common, voted in town and society meetings, and signed the various petitions that punctuated colonial governance. His was the steady, anonymous backbone of provincial Connecticut.

Religious and Civic Life

Religion in Eliphalet's Newington was central, not optional. The decade after he settled there saw the long, sometimes contentious work of establishing a separate parish. The West Society was authorized in 1713; its members were freed from rates to the First Church in Wethersfield village; and it began at once the construction of its own meeting house and the calling of its own minister. The records of the Newington Ecclesiastical Society - preserved in the Early Annals of Newington compiled by Roger Welles - are dense with the names of men like Eliphalet Whittlesey who served on committees to find a minister, lay a meeting house, hire a schoolmaster, set the boundaries of the society, and assess rates. Although the surviving records of the earliest years are imperfect, Eliphalet's place as one of the standing householders of the parish from 1709 onward placed him squarely in the front rank of West Society and Newington Church affairs.

The Rev. Elisha Williams was installed as the first pastor on October 3, 1722, when Eliphalet was forty-three. Williams served only briefly before being called as Rector of Yale College in 1726, but his ordination at Newington marked the formal birth of the church Eliphalet attended for the rest of his life. The Rev. Simon Backus succeeded Williams, and the Rev. Joshua Belden took the pulpit in 1747 - the third minister Eliphalet would sit under. The Great Awakening swept through Connecticut in the early 1740s; Newington church, like many in the colony, was shaken by it, and the divisions between "Old Light" and "New Light" Congregationalists became the chief theological controversy of Eliphalet's last years.

Civically, Eliphalet's life would have included regular service as a fence-viewer, surveyor of highways, or grand juror - the rotating petty offices that no respectable male householder escaped. He paid his portion to the support of the minister, the schoolmaster, the meeting house, and the poor. He attended town meeting in Wethersfield (then four miles east) for matters that the West Society could not resolve on its own. He stood watch in the militia in time of alarm, took his turn at training day on the common, and contributed his hides to the support of the colony's leather supply in wartime.

Death and Burial

Eliphalet Whittlesey died on September 14, 1757, at Wethersfield, in the Newington precinct, aged seventy-eight. The French and Indian War was at its bloodiest moment that summer: Fort William Henry, on the southern end of Lake George, had been captured by the Marquis de Montcalm on August 9, 1757, with the resulting massacre of British and provincial prisoners by Montcalm's Indian allies. The shock of that disaster reverberated through Connecticut in the weeks of August and September, just as Eliphalet lay dying. His son Eliphalet II, then forty-three, was already deeply embedded in the colony's military mobilization, and would soon receive his commission as Captain.

The elder Eliphalet was buried in the Newington Cemetery (sometimes called the Old Burying Ground or Maple Hill Cemetery), where his gravestone is preserved today. The Find A Grave memorial for this stone is #24326120. The original marker, of brown freestone with the characteristic winged-soul carving of mid-eighteenth-century New England, identified him simply as "Eliphalet Whittlesey," with the dates of his life and a brief Latin or scriptural inscription customary to the place and time.

Legacy

Eliphalet Whittlesey I's legacy was geographic and biological as much as monetary. He had been the seventh son of an immigrant ferryman and tanner at Saybrook; he died the patriarch of a Newington branch that would, through his son Eliphalet II, eventually fan out to Washington (Litchfield County), Stockbridge (Massachusetts), and ultimately northeastern Ohio. His descendants include U.S. Representative Elisha Whittlesey (1783-1863), long-serving member of Congress from Ohio and First Comptroller of the Treasury under Presidents Taylor and Lincoln; the Rev. Eliphalet Whittlesey (1816-1889), Yale graduate and missionary; Brig. Gen. Eliphalet Whittlesey (1821-1909), Civil War officer and Secretary of the Board of Indian Commissioners; and the entire body of Newington Whittlesey families through his son Lemuel and grandson Asaph, who would build and occupy the surviving "Whittlesey Homestead" (1758-1772) on Maple Hill Avenue.

The small one-story house Eliphalet built about 1709 is gone, but the homestead his grandson Lemuel built on the same family land still stands and is documented as one of the oldest dwellings in Newington. The town itself, which Eliphalet helped to bring into existence as a distinct parish, is now an independent municipality of more than thirty thousand inhabitants, but the cemetery in which he lies remains in active care, and the genealogical record of his line is one of the more thoroughly documented of any colonial Connecticut yeoman family, thanks to Charles Barney Whittelsey's 1898 compilation.

Childhood and Family

Eliphalet Whittlesey II was born on May 10, 1714, in his father's small one-story frame house on what was then 20 Rod Highway in the West Society of Wethersfield - today 461 Maple Hill Avenue, Newington. His father, Eliphalet Whittlesey I, was thirty-four and had been on this land roughly five years; his mother, Mary (Pratt) Whittlesey, was just shy of thirty-seven. The boy was thus the child of a household that had recently broken away from the dense river-town life of Saybrook and was carving a working farm and tannery out of the stony upland of western Wethersfield.

The Newington of Eliphalet II's boyhood was still officially the "West Society of Wethersfield," organized in 1713, just a year before his birth. The first meeting house was a-building during his earliest years, and the formal gathering of the Newington Church of Christ on October 3, 1722, with the installation of the Rev. Elisha Williams, must have been one of the great public memories of his eighth year. The boy who would one day be a captain of Connecticut militia first learned his catechism in that wood-walled, unheated meeting house, sitting on a hard plank between his mother and his sisters, with the men of the village - his father among them - filling the men's side across the aisle.

He grew up in a world of small fields cleared from oak woods, of stone walls being painstakingly laid up out of the glacial till, of cattle browsing in the unfenced "Cow Plain" common, and of the pungent labor of his father's tannery: the lime pits, the bark mill, the racks of slowly curing leather, the leather-aproned customers from Hartford and Wethersfield village calling to barter. He learned to read at his mother's knee from the New England Primer and the Bible, and at perhaps eight or nine entered the West Society's winter school, taught in a rented house or in the meeting house itself. His Latin probably did not progress far, but his arithmetic, his English grammar, and his Bible knowledge would have been thorough.

He likely was apprenticed - formally or informally - to his father in the tanner's trade, and would have known by his late teens both how to flesh a hide and how to drive a plow team. Newington in the 1720s and early 1730s was a still-young community, and a young man of capacity, raised on his father's improving farm, would have been groomed for an eventual share in the family land.

Adult Life and Trade

By the late 1730s Eliphalet II was a young husband and farmer in his own right in Newington. The family genealogy and the local land records indicate that he settled near his father, working a portion of the Whittlesey holdings on Maple Hill and continuing the combined tannery-and-farm operation. The eldest of his many children, Martin, was born about 1737, when Eliphalet II was twenty-three; John followed on December 23, 1741, when his father was twenty-seven. The brisk cadence of births through the 1740s and into the 1750s - Lemuel, Anna, Abner, Eliphalet III, Asaph, Dorothy, Elisha - implies a large and growing household even by the standards of colonial Connecticut.

He inherited or was deeded a share of the Whittlesey land at his father's death in September 1757. By that date he was already a man of forty-three, a captain in the colonial army, and the head of a household of at least nine children, ranging from young adults to small children. He was, however, a younger man in a generation in which most of the prime Newington land had already been divided among his uncles and brothers. With limited room for his sons to inherit decent farms in Newington, Eliphalet II - like his father a half-century before - looked to the next ring of frontier.

The frontier in question, in 1760, was western Litchfield County. The town of New Milford had been settled since 1707, but the high country to the north and west of it - the future towns of Washington, Warren, Cornwall, and Goshen - was still being filled in by Connecticut River-valley families seeking unimproved acreage. The New Preston Society, in the northwest corner of what was then Woodbury, had been organized in 1753-1757; land was relatively cheap; and a man with grown and nearly-grown sons could plausibly settle them all on contiguous farms within a few years of arrival.

In 1761, the year after the surrender of Montreal effectively ended the French and Indian War in North America, Eliphalet Whittlesey II, his wife Dorothy, and most of his children left Newington for New Preston. The historian of the Newington Whittlesey houses notes that he "eventually left town with his wife and ten of his children in 1761." (The "ten" likely includes a son-in-law or stepchild; the nine children documented in the standard genealogy were Martin, Lemuel, John, Anna, Abner, Eliphalet III, Asaph, Dorothy, and Elisha.) One son, Lemuel, remained behind on the Newington land and would build the surviving "Whittlesey Homestead" (1758-1772) on Maple Hill Avenue. The rest of the family resettled in the New Preston/Washington district, where Eliphalet III would later remove to Stockbridge, Massachusetts, and where the patriarch himself would live out his last quarter-century.

Marriage and Children

Eliphalet Whittlesey II married Dorothy Kellogg, who was born on December 24, 1716, and died on April 14, 1772, at Washington, Litchfield County, Connecticut. The Kellogg family was one of the substantial connecting families of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Connecticut, with major branches in Hartford, Hadley, and Norwalk. A Whittlesey-Kellogg match in Newington in the mid-1730s was a respectable yeoman alliance, and the marriage produced at least nine children whose names and birth orders are preserved in the 1898 Whittelsey genealogy and in numerous derived sources:

  1. Martin Whittlesey, b. about 1737 - eldest son
  2. Lemuel Whittlesey - the son who remained at Newington and built the Whittlesey Homestead (1758-1772) at the family seat on Maple Hill Avenue; his son Asaph inherited it, and Asaph's daughter Delia married Homer Camp
  3. John Whittlesey, b. December 23, 1741, at Newington; removed in 1761 with his father to New Preston; later known as John Sutton Whittlesey, Esq. (d. 1812)
  4. Anna Whittlesey, m. ----- Cogswell
  5. Abner Whittlesey
  6. Eliphalet Whittlesey III, b. 1748 - settled in Stockbridge, Berkshire County, Massachusetts; served in the American Revolution
  7. Asaph Whittlesey
  8. Dorothy Whittlesey, m. ----- Averill
  9. Elisha Whittlesey - whose descendants would carry the family into the Western Reserve of Ohio (the Connecticut Western Reserve), where a grandson and namesake, Elisha Whittlesey (1783-1863), would become a long-serving U.S. Representative from Ohio (1823-1838), Sixth Auditor of the Treasury under President Tyler, and First Comptroller of the Treasury under Presidents Taylor and Lincoln until his death in 1863

Dorothy (Kellogg) Whittlesey died in April 1772 at Washington, fourteen years before her husband. Eliphalet II survived her by all of those years, an unusual widowhood for a colonial man with grown children nearby, and apparently did not remarry.

Military Service

It is for his service in the French and Indian War that Eliphalet Whittlesey II is best remembered. The colony of Connecticut raised regiments of provincial troops each year from 1755 to 1762 for the great Anglo-American campaign to expel the French from North America. The annual quotas were filled by a mixture of volunteers and balloted men from the standing militia, organized into companies under captains drawn from the local gentry and yeoman elite. The state's published Rolls of Connecticut Men in the French and Indian War, 1755-1762 (Connecticut Historical Society, 1903-1905) is the central documentary source for this service.

Eliphalet Whittlesey appears in the published Connecticut rolls as a captain of a company. The standard summary of his service (preserved in Find A Grave Memorial #40862345 and in numerous derived genealogies) states that "in Captain Eliphalet Whittlesey's Company, of General Lyman's Command, in several campaigns of the French War (1756 to 1760) he served." General Phineas Lyman of Suffield, Connecticut, was the senior Connecticut field commander of the war, ranking as Major General of the colony's provincial forces from 1755 onward, and Lyman's regiments fought in nearly every major theater of the Champlain Valley campaigns: the unsuccessful 1755 attempt against Crown Point under William Johnson; the failed 1758 assault on Fort Carillon (Ticonderoga) under James Abercromby; the successful 1759 advance under Jeffery Amherst that took both Ticonderoga and Crown Point; and the 1760 march on Montreal that ended the war. Connecticut provincials served alongside regiments of British regulars, Massachusetts and New York provincials, and the colorful Rogers' Rangers, who operated out of the same Lake George camps in those years.

For a Newington captain in his forties, the wartime years would have involved each summer's mustering of the company on the Wethersfield common, the long march north to Albany and Lake George, weeks of garrison duty at a stockaded fort or open camp, the constant attrition of disease (which killed many more provincials than French bullets did), occasional skirmishes, and the autumn return home for the harvest and the winter. His son John, then in his late teens, served as his servant in the colonial wars - a common arrangement by which a captain's nearest male relative drew rations and a small wage as the officer's personal attendant.

The son Eliphalet III, born in 1748, was a small child throughout his father's wartime years. He would, however, follow the family military tradition into the next war - the American Revolution - serving in the Continental establishment from Stockbridge, Massachusetts, after his removal there.

The cumulative impact of those years on the captain was considerable. He returned each winter to a farm in Newington that had had to make do without him for six or seven months; his wife Dorothy carried the household; his elder sons covered the planting and harvest. He earned the lifelong honorific "Captain" by which he was known for the remaining twenty-five years of his life. And he gained the experience of the upcountry - of the rough roads through Litchfield and Sharon up to the Hudson, of the lake country, of the work of moving large bodies of men and animals - that would inform his own 1761 removal to New Preston, just as the war was ending.

The Colony of Connecticut in His Era

Eliphalet II's lifetime (1714-1786) spanned the transformation of Connecticut from a quiet, internally focused provincial society into one of the seedbeds of American independence. He was born under Queen Anne, came of age under George I and George II, fought in two of the colony's wars, removed to a new frontier, witnessed the imperial crisis of the 1760s and 1770s, lived through the Revolution, and died three years after the Peace of Paris.

The colonial Connecticut he knew was the most homogeneous, the most literate, and arguably the most stably self-governing of all the British North American colonies. Under the Royal Charter of 1662 the colony elected its own governor; its General Assembly was tightly tied to the towns; its established Congregational churches were tax-supported under the Saybrook Platform of 1708. Standing armies were unknown, but every able-bodied man between sixteen and sixty was enrolled in the train-band, mustered twice a year, and called out by the company captain in time of alarm. When the colony went to war - against the French in 1745 against Louisbourg, in the 1750s in the Champlain campaigns, and in 1775 against the Crown itself - it did so by activating these militia structures and converting them into provincial regiments.

The economy in Eliphalet II's prime was one of mixed agriculture, river commerce, coastal trade with the West Indies, household manufactures (especially leather, cloth, hats, and small metal), and a few specialized exports (Wethersfield onions; Connecticut River shad; East Haddam timber; Norwich-area beef). A successful farmer-tanner-officer like Eliphalet II could expect, by the 1750s, to live in a frame house of six or eight rooms, to own a clock and a Bible and perhaps a few other books, to drink imported tea and rum, to dress his wife in good imported wool and his daughters in printed calico, and to hold a steady credit at the local store. The 1761 move to New Preston cost him much of that comfort temporarily - removal to the frontier always did - but his sons quickly built it back up in the new town.

The fact that he removed in 1761, just as the war ended, places him among the wave of postwar settlers who pushed Connecticut's effective frontier from the Litchfield Hills further into the Berkshires and the Susquehanna country. His son Eliphalet III's eventual move into Stockbridge, and his more distant descendants' move into the Western Reserve of Ohio after the Revolution, are part of the same generational dispersal that scattered the Connecticut Yankees across the future northern United States.

Religious and Civic Life

In Newington, Eliphalet II was a man of the Newington Congregational Church under the Rev. Joshua Belden, who was installed in 1747 and served forty-six years. The captain's military commission would have placed him among the leading men of the West Society in the 1750s, and his name would appear regularly in society meeting records, on committees to seat the meeting house, to assess rates, to repair the parsonage, and to support the schoolmaster.

After his removal to New Preston in 1761, Eliphalet II became one of the founding generation of that community. The New Preston Society had been organized in the 1750s; the town of Washington, named for General George Washington, was set off from Woodbury, Litchfield, Kent, and New Milford in January 1779, and is sometimes credited as the first town in the United States named for the future first president. Eliphalet II - then sixty-five - was thus a settled inhabitant of the new town at its incorporation, and he and his sons would have been among the men who voted on the first town meeting business, helped to seat the new meeting house, and answered the calls of the Revolutionary committees of safety in those urgent years.

The American Revolution found him an elderly captain, too old for field service but old enough to be a moral and military reference for his sons and neighbors. Eliphalet III served in the Continental establishment from Stockbridge. Other sons would have done their share of militia duty in the Connecticut alarm companies. By the time of the Treaty of Paris in 1783, the captain was sixty-nine and a widower of eleven years; he died three years later in 1786.

Death and Burial

Eliphalet Whittlesey II died in 1786, in his seventy-third year. The standard genealogical date is July 12, 1786, at Washington, Litchfield County, Connecticut. His burial is most likely in one of the historic cemeteries of Washington - either the New Preston Hill Cemetery or the Washington Green Burying Ground, depending on the precise location of his residence at death; Find A Grave Memorial #40862345 carries his record. His wife Dorothy had preceded him in April 1772; he was thus buried as a widower of fourteen years, with most of his children grown and settled - some still in Washington, some across the line in Stockbridge, some preparing to head west into the Connecticut Western Reserve.

The captain's death came in the brief interval between the formal end of the Revolutionary War (Treaty of Paris, September 1783) and the ratification of the United States Constitution (June 1788). He lived to see his country independent but did not live to see it federally constituted. Within his immediate family, the Revolutionary generation - his sons and the older grandsons - was now in its prime, and the next generation, that of his grandson and namesake Elisha (b. 1783), was just being born into the new republic.

Legacy

The legacy of Captain Eliphalet Whittlesey II is unusually well documented for a colonial Connecticut captain. He stands at the hinge of two great westward movements: his own father's 1709 move from the riverside town of Saybrook to the upland parish of Newington, and his own 1761 move from Newington to the Litchfield hills. He linked the leather-craft and church-centered yeoman culture of the lower Connecticut Valley to the more dispersed, hill-country culture of western New England and, through his descendants, to the great Yankee migration across the Mohawk Valley into the Western Reserve of Ohio.

His descendants include U.S. Representative Elisha Whittlesey (1783-1863), seven-term Congressman from Ohio and First Comptroller of the Treasury; Col. Frederick Whittlesey (1799-1851), New York state senator and judge; Charles Whittlesey (1808-1886), Cleveland geologist, Civil War colonel, and historian of the Ohio country; the Rev. Eliphalet Whittlesey (1816-1889), Yale graduate and missionary; and Brig. Gen. Eliphalet Whittlesey (1821-1909), Civil War officer, Howard University professor, and Secretary of the Board of Indian Commissioners. His son Lemuel's house in Newington - the "Whittlesey Homestead" of 1758-1772 - still stands. The town of Washington, where he settled in 1761, was named for his commander-in-chief and is one of the most architecturally intact eighteenth-century Litchfield County towns.

He is remembered in the local histories of Newington and Washington, in the Connecticut Historical Society's published rolls of the French and Indian War, in the Litchfield Historical Society's collections, and in the 1898 Whittelsey genealogy, where he occupies a central position in the family's American line. The simple title that he carried for the last twenty-five years of his life - "Captain" - is the form by which he is known still.

Childhood and Family

Martin Whittlesey opened his eyes in the parish of Newington on 5 October 1737, the firstborn son of a young Connecticut couple who had been married only the previous December. His father, Eliphalet Whittlesey II, was a twenty-three-year-old farmer and rising militia officer whose own father, Eliphalet I, had built the family's first house at 20 Rod Highway in Newington around 1709. His mother, Dorothy Kellogg, was a Hartford woman of the great Kellogg clan that had spread out across the Connecticut Valley a generation earlier. The household Martin entered was thus a young one, but it was already part of an established Wethersfield kin network with deep roots in the colony's founding generation.

Newington in 1737 was a "society" — that is, a parish — within the town of Wethersfield, ten miles south of Hartford. It had been recognized as a separate ecclesiastical society in 1721 and had built its own meetinghouse on the green. Life there ran on the rhythms of the Congregational Sabbath: two long services on Sunday with a noon dinner in the meetinghouse stalls, midweek lecture days, periodic days of fasting and thanksgiving. The Whittlesey pew sat among the leading families. Eliphalet II was successively chosen surveyor, fence-viewer, lister, and ultimately captain of the train-band — the markers of a man on the upward slope of small-town public life.

Martin grew up watching his mother give birth to eight more children over the next twenty years. Lemuel arrived when he was two, John when he was three, then Anna, Abner, Eliphalet III, Asaph, Dorothy, and finally Elisha when Martin was already a young man of twenty. As eldest son he held a particular weight in this family. Colonial Connecticut law, under the old double-portion rule then still in force in some form, gave the eldest son a privileged share in his father's estate. More importantly, the eldest son was the one expected to model adult responsibility for the rest. Martin's chores would have been heavy from a young age: splitting kindling, tending the pair of oxen, mucking out the stalls, helping his father lay up the stone walls that even today thread through the old Newington fields.

The Whittlesey house Martin knew was almost certainly the one his grandfather had built and that his father expanded. It was a one-and-a-half-story cape with a massive central chimney, small leaded-glass windows, a great kitchen hearth wide enough to roast a quarter of venison. The cellar was dug into the slope. Pewter dishes on the dresser, a corner cupboard with the family Bible and copy of the Saybrook Platform, a few imported pieces of crockery from Bristol or Liverpool — these were the household goods of a respected but not wealthy Connecticut yeoman.

Coming of Age in Colonial America

Martin came of age in the 1750s, and the 1750s in Connecticut were dominated by one fact: the colony was at war again. The French and Indian War, the American theater of the Seven Years' War, raged from 1754 to 1763. His father, Capt. Eliphalet Whittlesey II, commanded the 10th Company in the campaigns of 1757, serving March through June of that year in the chain of expeditions aimed at the French posts on Lakes George and Champlain. While his father marched north under Connecticut's colors, twenty-year-old Martin was likely left at home as the man of the house, responsible for his mother, his teenage siblings, the planting, the haying, the slaughter.

The war years were hard on Connecticut farms. Recruiting bounties, requisitions of grain and beef for the army, smallpox brought south by returning soldiers, and the constant drain of able-bodied men to garrison the frontier all bit into the small economies of towns like Newington. Martin learned in these years what it meant to keep a farm going while the politics of empire pulled men away. It was a lesson he would draw on twice more in his life — once when he himself marched in the Revolution, and once when his own son Joseph would come of age in the aftermath.

Around 1761, when the French war was winding down, Capt. Eliphalet II made one of the great decisions of his life. He sold or transferred the Newington homestead and moved his family — wife, ten of the children, livestock, household goods, the lot — northwest into the new lands of Litchfield County. The destination was the parish of Judea (later joined to New Preston to form the town of Washington in 1779). This was frontier country relative to the Connecticut Valley: hilly, stony, heavily wooded, with iron deposits that had been worked since the 1740s. Litchfield County in 1761 was the colony's expanding frontier, where men who could not get enough land in the older valley towns went to take up new farms.

Martin was twenty-four when this move happened, an adult who likely participated as a co-laborer, perhaps already with land of his own taken up alongside his father's. The new farm in Judea would have required years of clearing — girdling the great oaks, burning the slash, pulling stumps with the ox team, piling the everlasting stones into the walls that still mark the old field boundaries today. By the mid-1760s Martin was a young yeoman in his own right, a freeman of Connecticut, voting in the spring and fall freemen's meetings, attending muster on the village green four times a year as required by the militia law of 1739.

By 1764 he was married, with his son Joseph born 20 March of that year. By 1775, the year the war began, he was a thirty-eight-year-old husband and father, a settled farmer with at least one growing son, deep in the round of plowing, sowing, mowing, threshing, and slaughtering that defined a New England farming year.

Military Service in the American Revolution

When the news of Lexington and Concord swept down the Boston Post Road and out into the back parishes of Connecticut in late April 1775, Martin Whittlesey was forty years old. He was past the age when most men marched as common soldiers — the prime of life for a Revolutionary private was eighteen to twenty-five — but well within the legal age of the Connecticut militia, which required service from sixteen to fifty. He served, family tradition and the family genealogy agree, as a Sergeant in the Connecticut forces.

The rank of sergeant in the Continental forces and in the Connecticut state militia was a meaningful one. A sergeant in a Revolutionary company was the seasoned non-commissioned officer who actually ran the rank and file day to day. He drilled the men in the manual of arms — the cumbersome 1764 British drill, modified by Baron von Steuben's blue book after 1778 — and he kept order on the march, posted the sentries, formed the squads for ration distribution, and stood as the link between the captain's orders and the men's daily life. A sergeant in 1775 typically carried a halberd or spontoon, a hanger or short sword, and wore a sash or distinguishing knot to mark his rank. He was often, as Martin was, an older married man with the gravity to enforce discipline on younger recruits.

The Litchfield County militia of which Martin was a part was organized as the 13th Regiment (later partly reorganized into the 17th when Litchfield and Goshen were detached in 1774). The Washington and New Preston men served in companies that turned out repeatedly during the war's eight long years. The patterns of Connecticut service were episodic: a man might march for a month or two to reinforce the army around New York in the summer of 1776, then come home to get in the harvest, then march again to White Plains in October, then return for the winter. The Connecticut militia served at the siege of Boston in 1775; at the disastrous Battle of Long Island and the retreat across New Jersey in 1776; at Danbury when Tryon raided in April 1777; at Saratoga in the autumn of 1777; at New Haven and Norwalk when the British raided the Connecticut coast in July 1779; and along the Hudson Highlands throughout. A Connecticut sergeant of Martin's age and position would likely have been on one or more of these turnouts.

His younger brother Eliphalet III also served as a Revolutionary soldier, the family genealogy records. His brother Asaph, the youngest but one, died in 1778 at the age of twenty-five — whether of disease contracted in service, of the camp fevers that killed far more Continental soldiers than British muskets, or of some other cause, is not certain in the surviving records. The Whittlesey family's contribution to the war effort was substantial: the eldest brother served as sergeant, at least one younger brother served as soldier, and the family lost a young man during the war years.

The hardships of Continental and Connecticut service were brutal. The Continental ration was theoretically a pound of beef or pork, a pound of bread, vegetables, and a half-gill of rum per day, but in practice troops were often half-starved. Pay was issued in Continental currency that depreciated so badly that "not worth a Continental" passed into the language. The winter at Valley Forge in 1777-78 killed perhaps a quarter of the men encamped there from cold, hunger, and disease. The terrible winter of 1779-80 at Morristown, with snow so deep the army was cut off from supply for weeks, was even worse by some accounts. Connecticut militia returning from the Hudson Highlands in 1781 sometimes had not been paid in two years and were on the edge of mutiny — the Connecticut Line mutiny of January 1781 saw soldiers march on their officers demanding back pay before being talked down.

Martin's experience as a sergeant in his late thirties and forties was almost certainly tied to specific alarm calls and short-term reinforcements rather than to long Continental enlistments. He had a wife, children, a farm in the back country. He came home each time, and at the end of the war, he was forty-six years old — gray, no doubt, weathered, with the wartime habit of early rising and the soldier's economy of motion that men noticed for decades afterward in Revolutionary veterans.

Marriage and Children

Martin married circa 1763, when he was about twenty-six. The family genealogy gives his eldest son Joseph as born 20 March 1764. Additional children followed during the 1760s and 1770s, though the surviving compilations vary in their completeness. The eldest, Joseph, is the subject of the companion biography in this series and is the line through which the present family descends. Other children of Martin would have grown up through the Revolution and the early Republic, marrying into other Litchfield County families and pushing further west as the great Connecticut migrations of the 1790s and early 1800s opened up the Western Reserve in Ohio, central New York, and the Vermont and New Hampshire frontiers.

The Connecticut He Lived In

The Connecticut of Martin Whittlesey's lifetime was the most stable, the most Yankee, the most stubbornly traditional of the thirteen colonies and then the thirteen states. It was governed under its 1662 royal charter — the famous Connecticut Charter that Capt. Joseph Wadsworth had hidden in the Charter Oak in 1687 to save it from Sir Edmund Andros — and that charter continued to function essentially unchanged as Connecticut's constitution until 1818, eighteen years after Martin's death. The colony chose its own governor by popular vote of freemen, ran its own legal system, and maintained an established Congregational church supported by general taxation.

When Martin came of age, Connecticut had about 130,000 people. By the time he died in 1800, it had grown to about 250,000. It was a colony of small towns, of well-tended farms, of well-attended Sabbath services, and of relentless emigration. Connecticut produced more sons than its rocky land could feed, and from the 1760s onward those sons fanned out — to Vermont in the 1760s and 1770s, to the Wyoming Valley of Pennsylvania (which Connecticut claimed under its sea-to-sea charter), to upstate New York after 1783, and to the Connecticut Western Reserve in northern Ohio after 1795.

The Litchfield County in which Martin spent his adult life was the colony's youngest, its frontier, its rough country of iron forges and hill farms. Litchfield itself was a sophisticated county seat with its famous law school (founded 1784) and female academy. Washington, where Martin lived, was a quieter agricultural town spread over the hilltops above the Shepaug River. The town was officially incorporated on 28 October 1779 — during the war and during Martin's adulthood — and was named for the commander-in-chief, who was known in Litchfield County and was reported to have breakfasted at Cogswell's tavern in New Preston in May 1781 on his way between Hartford and Newburgh.

Connecticut's role in the Revolution was outsized for its small population. It supplied perhaps 32,000 men to the cause over the course of the war, more per capita than any state except Massachusetts. It was called "the Provisions State" for its constant supplies of beef, pork, salt, and flour to the army, which Gen. Washington considered indispensable. The state also bore the cost of repeated coastal raids by the British — New Haven, Fairfield, and Norwalk were burned in 1779; New London was burned and the garrison at Fort Griswold massacred in September 1781 under the renegade Benedict Arnold.

Death and Burial

Martin Whittlesey died on 29 May 1800 in Washington, Litchfield County, Connecticut, at the age of 62. The cause is not recorded in the family genealogy. He outlived his mother by twenty-eight years, his father by fourteen, his youngest brother Asaph by twenty-two. He died in the same election year that brought Thomas Jefferson to the presidency, just months before the long and disputed contest that ended in the House of Representatives in February 1801. He was buried in one of the old Washington town burying grounds, likely the New Preston cemetery, alongside other Whittleseys and other Litchfield County pioneer families.

His son Joseph was then thirty-six years old, a man in middle life with a household of his own. The farm passed to his survivors. The estate would have included land in Washington and probably outlying lots; livestock; the household pewter, linens, and furniture; the family Bible with the births and deaths inscribed; and very likely a worn musket and a sergeant's sash, the relics of his Revolutionary service.

Legacy and Descendants

Sgt. Martin Whittlesey is one of the patriot ancestors through whom present-day Whittlesey descendants are eligible for membership in the Sons and Daughters of the American Revolution. His Revolutionary service was honored in the family's collective memory at the great 1855 reunion at Saybrook, where the Hon. Elisha Whittlesey, U.S. Congressman and First Comptroller of the Treasury, delivered an elaborate address recounting the family's contributions to American liberty.

Through his son Joseph and Joseph's descendants, Martin's line continued in Connecticut and migrated westward through the nineteenth century into the regions opened up by Connecticut migration — the Western Reserve of Ohio, central New York, and points further west as the country expanded. His brother Lemuel's line stayed in Newington and Litchfield County. His brother John Sutton Whittlesey's line at New Preston produced another long line of distinguished public servants. The family scattered, but the Newington-Washington branches kept up correspondence and a sense of common origin that the 1855 reunion and the subsequent published genealogy (Charles Barney Whittelsey's Genealogy of the Whittelsey-Whittlesey Family) memorialized for posterity.

Martin's most enduring legacy, beyond his bloodline, was his place in the second generation of Whittleseys to fight for the country — the first generation, his father's, having fought for the British Crown against the French in the 1750s, and his own generation having fought for American independence against that same Crown twenty years later. The transformation of these Connecticut families from loyal British colonists to American republicans is one of the great stories of the age, and Martin Whittlesey, sergeant of the Connecticut forces, was one of thousands of obscure but essential participants in that transformation.

Childhood and Family

Joseph Whittlesey was born on 20 March 1764, twelve years before the Declaration of Independence, in the hill country of northwestern Connecticut. His parents were Martin Whittlesey, then twenty-six and a few years into his own married life, and Martin's wife, the records of whose name vary in the surviving compilations. The household was a young one, settled in the parish of Judea — a section of the older town of Woodbury that, fifteen years later in 1779, would be incorporated as the new town of Washington in honor of the commanding general of the Continental Army.

The world Joseph entered in March 1764 was officially British. King George III had been on the throne for less than four years. The French and Indian War had ended only the previous year with the Treaty of Paris of 1763, by which France had ceded all of Canada and the eastern Mississippi Valley to Britain. The American colonies were celebrating their part in the great imperial victory and beginning, painfully, to absorb the costs of empire. The very month Joseph was born, March 1764, Parliament passed the Sugar Act — the first of the revenue measures that would eventually drive the colonies to revolt. Within a year the Stamp Act would follow, and Connecticut, with its tradition of self-government under its 1662 royal charter, would be one of the most vocal centers of opposition.

So Joseph, an infant in his cradle in the Litchfield hills, was already in 1765 the subject of imperial controversies he could not understand. His grandfather Capt. Eliphalet II, then aged about fifty-one, would have remembered the Stamp Act crisis with concern as a former officer who had served the Crown. His father Martin, twenty-eight years old, would have been one of the young men of the town arguing the case in front of the meetinghouse after services. Joseph spent his earliest years in a household where talk of liberty, of taxation, of "the rights of Englishmen," of Boston news, was constant background.

The Whittlesey farm in Judea/Washington was a frontier farm by Connecticut standards. The family had only arrived in the hills around 1761, when Joseph's grandfather brought his ten children out of the long-settled Connecticut Valley. By Joseph's boyhood the worst of the clearing was done, but the land was still rough — stumps in the corners of the meadow, brush piles waiting to be burned, the great labor of stone-wall building underway and never finished. A child Joseph's age would have spent his early years tagging along after his mother, then graduating to small chores: gathering eggs, carrying chips for the fire, leading the cow to and from pasture, helping shell peas and shuck corn.

Schooling in a Connecticut frontier town of the 1770s was rudimentary. Each "school society" — there were several in each town — supported a winter school in a small one-room building. The teacher was often a young man between college and entry to a profession, hired for a few months at a time. The curriculum was the New England Primer, Webster's Spelling Book (after 1783), simple arithmetic, and a great deal of Bible reading and recitation. Boys went in winter when farm work was light; older boys often did not attend at all after age twelve. Joseph would have learned to read fluently, to write a serviceable hand, and to do the practical arithmetic a farmer needed for surveying his lots and reckoning his accounts at the country store.

Coming of Age in Revolutionary America

Joseph's entire childhood ran parallel to the formation of the United States. He was a small boy of nine when the Boston Tea Party happened in December 1773, when ships' chests of British East India Company tea were dumped in Boston Harbor and the country gasped at what young men in Indian disguises had done. He was ten when the news came of the Intolerable Acts and the First Continental Congress in Philadelphia in the autumn of 1774, with Connecticut sending three delegates including Eliphalet Dyer and Roger Sherman. He had just turned eleven when, on the morning of 19 April 1775, the British regulars marched out of Boston toward Lexington and Concord and a Massachusetts militiaman fired the shot heard round the world.

The news reached the Litchfield hills within a few days by post rider. Town meetings were called. Drums beat. Men of the militia turned out on the village green for the "Lexington Alarm" of late April 1775, in which roughly 4,000 Connecticut men marched toward Boston before being turned back when it became clear the immediate emergency was past. Joseph's father Martin was among the men who took up arms. Joseph, an eleven-year-old boy, watched his father march out to fight the British.

The next eight years of Joseph's life were the years of the Revolutionary War. From eleven to nineteen — the years a boy is most impressionable, most aware of the wider world — he lived in a household at war. His father came and went on militia turnouts. The men of the town gathered constantly to debate war news. Strangers passed through with rumors from Hartford, from New Haven, from New York, from Philadelphia. Continental currency depreciated; the wartime economy strained every farm; salt was scarce, sugar was scarcer, English manufactured goods almost unobtainable. When the British raided the Connecticut coast in the summer of 1779, burning New Haven and Fairfield and Norwalk, the alarm reached even into Litchfield County. When Benedict Arnold burned New London and stormed Fort Griswold in September 1781, the news traveled fast.

In the autumn of 1781, when Joseph was seventeen, came the great news of Yorktown — Cornwallis surrendered on 19 October, and the war was effectively won, though the formal peace would not be signed for another two years. In 1783, when Joseph was nineteen and his father came home for the last time a veteran of the long struggle, the Treaty of Paris formally recognized the independence of the United States of America. The country in which Joseph would live the rest of his life was thus officially established the year he became, by colonial reckoning, a man.

It is hard to overstate the psychological effect this had on Joseph's generation. They were the first generation of Americans — the first born colonists who came to adulthood as citizens of an independent republic. They had no memory of a settled imperial connection; their formative experience was war, hardship, debate, and ultimately victory. Many of them — the cohort born 1760-1770 — would become the workhorse generation of the early Republic, the surveyors, the storekeepers, the militia captains, the church deacons, the pioneer settlers of the new western lands. They were too young for the war, too old to feel themselves a wholly new generation; they were the bridge between the founders and the antebellum builders.

Civilian Life Around the Revolution

Joseph did not serve in the Revolutionary War — he was too young, turning twenty-one only in 1785, two years after the peace. But the war shaped him decisively. The household he grew up in was a soldier's household. The values he absorbed — independence, frugality, suspicion of standing armies and aristocrats, deep loyalty to Connecticut's particular form of self-government, a Congregationalist sense of moral seriousness — were the values of the Revolutionary cohort that produced him.

In the years immediately after the war, Joseph took his place as a young man in Washington town. He came of age in 1785, a year of severe economic depression for the new nation. The Continental currency was worthless. State currencies were nearly so. Specie was scarce. Merchants in Hartford and New Haven were calling in debts; debtors were being haled into court. Western Massachusetts erupted in Shays's Rebellion in the autumn of 1786 and the winter of 1787, with debtor farmers shutting down county courts and trying to seize the federal arsenal at Springfield. Connecticut watched nervously and sent militia to its western border. The crisis fed directly into the call for the Constitutional Convention at Philadelphia in May 1787.

When the new Federal Constitution was sent out for ratification in the autumn of 1787, Connecticut took it up at a ratifying convention in Hartford in January 1788. The convention voted 128 to 40 in favor of ratification, making Connecticut the fifth state to ratify. The leading Federalist voices in the state included Oliver Ellsworth and Roger Sherman, both of whom had been at Philadelphia helping draft the document and crafting the famous Connecticut Compromise that gave each state equal representation in the Senate. Joseph, twenty-three years old in January 1788, was old enough to take an interest and probably attended town meetings where the new constitution was discussed.

By the time George Washington was inaugurated on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York City on 30 April 1789, Joseph was twenty-five. He was likely already married or considering it. He was certainly already a working farmer, perhaps with land of his own on the family acreage or with a tract he had taken up on his own initiative. The new federal government had little immediate impact on a Washington, Connecticut farmer's day-to-day life, but it set the framework within which everything that came afterward would unfold.

The 1790s brought the Whiskey Rebellion in western Pennsylvania (1794), the controversial Jay Treaty with Britain (1795), Washington's Farewell Address warning against permanent alliances and party faction (1796), the bitter election of 1796 in which John Adams narrowly defeated Thomas Jefferson, the quasi-war with France (1798-1800), and the Alien and Sedition Acts that nearly tore the young nation apart. Connecticut throughout this decade was solidly Federalist — pro-Hamilton, pro-Adams, suspicious of Jefferson and of French revolutionary excess. The Congregational establishment, the Yale College of President Timothy Dwight, the leading merchants of Hartford and New Haven all aligned with the Federalist Party. Joseph, raised in this milieu, almost certainly voted Federalist for as long as the party survived.

The 1790s also saw the great Connecticut land sale that defined the next century of family migration. In 1795, the state sold the Connecticut Western Reserve — three million acres along the south shore of Lake Erie in what is now northeastern Ohio — for $1.2 million to the Connecticut Land Company. The proceeds went to the state's school fund, and the lands were opened to settlement by Connecticut men. Through the 1790s, 1800s, 1810s, and 1820s, a steady stream of Connecticut families packed up wagons and moved out to the Western Reserve, establishing towns with names like New Haven, Cleveland (after Moses Cleaveland the surveyor), Hudson, and many others that read like a map of Connecticut superimposed on Ohio. The Whittleseys took part in this migration; among the Litchfield County branches, several lines moved to the Reserve in the early decades of the nineteenth century. Whether Joseph himself moved or remained in Washington is not made fully clear by the immediate genealogical record, but his immediate family was caught up in the broader migration.

Marriage and Children

Joseph married, by the customary practice of his time and place, in his mid-to-late twenties — likely between about 1788 and 1795. The records of his wife's name and the dates of birth of his children vary across the surviving compilations and require further verification in the Washington town records and the Whittlesey family genealogy. What is consistent across sources is that Joseph established a household in Washington town, raised a family of children there, and continued the Whittlesey line into the early nineteenth century. His descendants would be the ones to make the family's transition from Connecticut yeomanry into the broader nineteenth-century American story.

The naming patterns of Joseph's generation are themselves illuminating. Sons were typically named for fathers, grandfathers, uncles, brothers — and so the Whittlesey family's stock of given names remained narrow: Eliphalet, Martin, John, Joseph, Lemuel, Asaph, Elisha, Abner. Daughters received names from the maternal line as often as the paternal — Dorothy, Anna, Mary, Mehitable, Sarah, Mercy, Abigail. The repetition created the classic difficulty of New England genealogy: a dozen Joseph Whittleseys in a dozen towns, requiring careful separation by dates, places, and the careful weaving of the genealogy.

The Connecticut He Lived In

Joseph's Connecticut was the Connecticut of the early Republic — a state still governed under the 1662 royal charter until 1818, still maintaining its established Congregational church until disestablishment in 1818, still dominated by the Federalist Party until that party's collapse after the War of 1812. It was a state of farms, of small manufactures (clocks at Bristol, tinware at Berlin, hats at Danbury, ships at New London and Mystic), of an active coastwise and West Indian trade, and of constant emigration.

Litchfield County in Joseph's adulthood was the home of two notable institutions: the Litchfield Law School, founded in 1784 by Tapping Reeve, which trained generations of American lawyers including future Vice Presidents Aaron Burr and John C. Calhoun, future Supreme Court Justices, future cabinet officers; and Sarah Pierce's Female Academy, founded in 1792, one of the first serious schools for the higher education of women in America. The county thus produced an extraordinary number of public men and educated women per capita in the early Republic. Washington town itself was quieter than Litchfield — agricultural, Congregationalist, modestly prosperous, with no great institutions but with a steady civic life of town meetings, militia musters, school committees, and church affairs.

The economic life of a Washington farmer in Joseph's middle years was a mixed picture. The basic farm produced enough wheat, rye, Indian corn, potatoes, beef, pork, milk, butter, cheese, wool, flax, and apples to feed and clothe the family. Cash crops were small but real — surplus butter and cheese went to New York via wagon or coastwise sloop; surplus beef on the hoof was driven east to Boston or Hartford; flax and linen could be sold. The store accounts at the country merchant covered the cash gap, recorded in lined ledgers in the merchant's careful hand: so many yards of calico, so many pounds of West Indian molasses, so many quarts of New England rum, so much loaf sugar, so much salt, all set against credits for butter, cheese, eggs, ashes (for potash), and labor. Most farmers carried a running debt to the store, settled periodically with cash or notes.

Death and Burial

Joseph Whittlesey died on 8 March 1838, twelve days short of his seventy-fourth birthday. He was buried in the Washington town burying ground in Litchfield County, Connecticut, alongside other members of the Whittlesey family. His death came in the same year as the Trail of Tears, the forced removal of the Cherokee Nation from the southeastern states, and in the second year of Martin Van Buren's troubled presidency. The Panic of 1837, which had hit the previous spring, had thrown the country into a severe depression that would last several years. The young republic Joseph had watched be born was now well into its sixth decade, with twenty-six states, a population of seventeen million, the steamboat and the railroad transforming everything, and a westward migration that had carried the country's frontier to Texas, Iowa, and Wisconsin.

The world Joseph left in 1838 was utterly transformed from the colonial world into which he had been born in 1764. In his lifetime the United States had grown from thirteen Atlantic colonies of perhaps 2.5 million people to a transcontinental ambition with seventeen million. Steam power was beginning to remake transport and industry. Telegraphy was a year or two away. The slavery question, never far below the surface, was beginning to crack the political consensus. Joseph had been born a colonial subject of King George III; he died a citizen of a continental republic in its sixth decade, with two of its presidents (Jackson and Van Buren) being men he could remember as soldiers in the Revolution or as adults in its aftermath.

Legacy and Descendants

Joseph Whittlesey is the bridge generation in the Whittlesey family line — the link between the Revolutionary sergeant Martin and the antebellum and Civil War-era descendants who would carry the family forward. He did not himself fight in the Revolution, but he was the son of a sergeant and the grandson of a French and Indian War captain. He came of age with the United States. He raised the next generation of Whittlesey children in the atmosphere of the early Republic.

The Whittlesey family at the great 1855 reunion at Saybrook — held seventeen years after Joseph's death — drew together descendants from across New England, New York, Ohio, and points further west. The Rev. Joseph Whittlesey of Berlin offered the opening prayer at that reunion (note: a different Joseph from the subject of this biography, a common-name confusion typical of New England families); the Rev. John Whittlesey of Salem pronounced the benediction; the Hon. Elisha Whittlesey of Ohio, First Comptroller of the United States Treasury, delivered the principal address. The whole proceeding was published as the Whittlesey Memorials and remains a key source for the family genealogy.

Joseph Whittlesey's most enduring legacy was simply that he kept the line. He was the unbroken middle link in a chain that runs from John the immigrant of Saybrook in 1664 through Eliphalet I of Newington (1679-1757) through Capt. Eliphalet II (1714-1786) through Sgt. Martin (1737-1800) through Joseph himself (1764-1838) and forward to the nineteenth- and twentieth-century descendants who have carried the family name down to the present. He lived through more change than any prior generation of his line had seen, and he handed on the family's identity, traditions, faith, and habits to children who would themselves see the country grow to fifty states and span an ocean to an ocean.

Childhood and Family

Luman Whittlesey was born in Washington, Connecticut, on March 15, 1795, into one of the founding families of the Litchfield County uplands. He was the son of Joseph Whittlesey (1764-1838), who himself had been born only two years before the United States declared independence. The Whittleseys had been planted in the western Connecticut hills since the early eighteenth century, threading down from the original immigrant John of Saybrook (1638) through the Eliphalet line of Saybrook and the Joseph branch that took root in the Litchfield highlands around the time the parish of Judea — soon to be incorporated as the town of Washington — separated from Woodbury, Kent, New Milford, and Litchfield in 1779.

The Washington of Luman's infancy was a young town in every sense. Its meetinghouse hill still smelled of fresh-cut pine; its town minutes were just a decade and a half old; the men who had served at Saratoga and Yorktown were now in their middle years, sitting in the deacons' pews of the Congregational church and farming the rocky pastures with hands hardened by both musket and plough. To be born a Whittlesey in Washington in 1795 was to be born into a settled but still rough community of fewer than fifteen hundred souls, perched on the long ridges between the Shepaug and Bantam rivers, where stone walls and cleared sheep pasture were slowly displacing the original chestnut and oak forest.

George Washington had begun his second term as President two years before Luman's birth; John Adams, his Yankee successor, would take office two years after. The Federalists held Connecticut absolutely — the so-called "Standing Order" of Congregational ministers, Yale-trained lawyers, and land-owning farmers ran the state as a kind of Calvinist republic. Luman's father Joseph was firmly inside that order: a freeholder, a church member, and the kind of man who could read his Greek New Testament and reckon a surveyor's chain.

Joseph and his wife raised a household in which education was not optional. Luman was sent down the road to the district school as a child of five or six, then up the hill to the academy at Washington, and finally — in the autumn of 1812, just as the second war with Britain was breaking out — he matriculated at Yale College in New Haven. He graduated with the Yale Class of 1816 at age twenty-one.

Among Luman's siblings, the most prominent in family memory was his older brother Martin Whittlesey, who remained on the ancestral acres in Washington as a farmer and town selectman, and through whom the main Connecticut line of the family would continue. Luman, by contrast, was the brother who left.

Adult Life, Occupation, Marriage

A Yale degree in 1816 opened three classic paths for a New England farm boy: the ministry, the law, or schoolkeeping. Luman, by inclination or providence, chose the third — and chose to follow it not into the comfortable academies of southern Connecticut but into a far stranger country.

In 1818, two years after taking his bachelor's degree, Luman Whittlesey moved south to Williamston, in Martin County, North Carolina, to teach at the newly organized Williamston Academy. The migration was not as eccentric as it sounds today. In the 1810s and 1820s, dozens of Yale and Dartmouth men accepted invitations from southern planter towns to come run their new academies; the planters wanted classical instruction for their sons, and the northern colleges produced more schoolmasters than New England parishes could absorb. Williamston, a riverport town on the Roanoke River about thirty miles from Albemarle Sound, was a tobacco and naval-stores community of perhaps three hundred white residents and an enslaved population of roughly equal size. To a young man from the granite hills of Litchfield, the warm tidewater pine flats, the cypress swamps of the Roanoke, and the institution of slavery itself must all have been a shock.

Luman married almost immediately. His first wife was Sarah Johnson Cogswell, a young woman whose family was tied into the New England diaspora that had brought him south. Their first son, Edgar Augustus Whittlesey, was born on July 1, 1819 in Williamston — barely a year into Luman's North Carolina life. A second son, Joseph Adolphus Whittlesey (named for Luman's father), followed. But Sarah Cogswell Whittlesey died in 1821, leaving Luman a widower of twenty-six with two small boys.

Some years afterward Luman married, as his second wife, Elizabeth G. Peale, a North Carolinian by birth. Their oldest child was a daughter born August 24, 1824, christened — in the touching custom of the day — Sarah Johnson Cogswell Whittlesey, for Luman's first wife. This daughter would grow up to become the well-known antebellum and postbellum poet, novelist, and hymn writer Sarah J.C. Whittlesey of Alexandria, Virginia, the author of Heart Drops from Memory's Urn (poems), The Stranger's Stratagem (novelettes, 1860), and the long novel Bertha, the Beauty (1871). She remembered her father, in autobiographical fragments, as a New England-bred man of "scrupulous integrity," fond of the classics, and deeply attached to the Roanoke country he had adopted.

The exact arc of Luman's career in Williamston is less well documented than that of his famous daughter, but the broad outline is clear: he established himself as the schoolmaster of the town, became a fixture in the small Episcopal congregation that met there, was respected enough to serve as a justice of the peace and to be referred to in local correspondence as "Mr. Whittlesey of the Academy," and lived in Williamston for the better part of forty years. His sons by his first marriage left the state — Edgar Augustus to Mississippi in the late 1830s and on to Texas by the mid-1840s; Joseph Adolphus to a similar westering path. His daughter Sarah moved with the family to Alexandria, Virginia, in 1848, and Luman appears to have removed there with them in his later years, although his Connecticut and North Carolina ties remained strong.

The America He Lived In

Luman Whittlesey's seventy-three years of life span what is arguably the most transformational human lifetime in American history. He was born in 1795, when there were sixteen states, fewer than five million people in the United States, and a single transcontinental footpath beyond the Appalachians. He died in 1868, when there were thirty-seven states, almost forty million people, a transcontinental telegraph, and a transcontinental railroad within a year of completion.

Childhood (1795-1812): The America of Luman's boyhood was a quiet Federalist republic. Connecticut had no railroad, no canal, and no factory of any consequence. News from Europe came by sailing packet to New York, then up the Post Road by stage. The great political crisis of his eight-year-old year, the Louisiana Purchase, doubled the size of the country but seemed remote to a boy on a Litchfield hill farm. The crisis of his ten-year-old year, the Burr-Hamilton duel, scandalized New Haven's lawyers but probably did not penetrate the Washington meetinghouse.

Youth (1812-1818) — the War of 1812 and Yale: Luman entered Yale in the autumn of 1812, the same season James Madison declared war on Britain. Connecticut's Federalists hated the war; the legislature refused to call out the militia for federal service, and the Hartford Convention of December 1814 met to consider New England's options up to and including secession. As an undergraduate in New Haven during these years, Luman would have heard sermons against "Mr. Madison's War," watched British warships harass the Connecticut coast (a small landing party burned shipping at Pettipaug Point in April 1814, only twenty miles from New Haven), and participated in the public thanksgiving when the news of the Treaty of Ghent reached the colleges in February 1815.

Young manhood (1818-1830) — the Era of Good Feelings: Luman's first decade in Williamston coincided with what historians call the Era of Good Feelings — James Monroe's two terms, the Missouri Compromise of 1820, the rapid building of the Erie Canal (1817-1825), and the opening of the cotton South to slave-based plantation agriculture. As a Connecticut Yankee teaching in the North Carolina tidewater, Luman lived directly in the contradiction at the heart of this era: New England's economic and educational modernization on the one hand; the consolidation of chattel slavery on the other.

Middle age (1830-1850) — the Jacksonian Republic: Andrew Jackson's elections of 1828 and 1832, the Nullification Crisis of 1832-33 (which directly involved North Carolina's neighbor South Carolina), the Bank War, Indian Removal (the Cherokee were forced west along the Trail of Tears in 1838-39, the year of Luman's father Joseph's death), and the annexation of Texas in 1845 were all events that touched Luman's family personally. His son Edgar would migrate from Mississippi to Sabine County, Texas, and in 1849 be elected to the Third Texas Legislature — a direct family participation in the Jacksonian westering movement.

The road to civil war (1850-1861): The Compromise of 1850, the Fugitive Slave Act, the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854, "Bleeding Kansas," the Dred Scott decision of 1857, John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry in 1859, and the election of Abraham Lincoln in November 1860 — Luman, a New England-born Yankee living in North Carolina (and by then in Virginia), watched the Union pulled apart along precisely the regional seam that ran through his own household. His Connecticut nephews would muster into Union regiments; his Texas son would muster, almost certainly, into a Confederate one.

The Civil War (1861-1865): When North Carolina seceded on May 20, 1861, Luman was a man of sixty-six. Whatever his private feelings — and the family record suggests they were quietly Unionist — he kept them quiet enough to weather the war in Alexandria, a Virginia city occupied by Union forces from May 24, 1861 (the same day the young Colonel Elmer Ellsworth was shot dead in the Marshall House) until the war's end. Alexandria during the war was a vast military depot, hospital town, and corridor for the Union armies operating in northern Virginia. Luman, his second wife, and his daughter Sarah lived in the strange world of an occupied Confederate city, where Union sutlers shouldered Confederate refugees in the same streets and where the dead from Bull Run, Fredericksburg, and the Wilderness were brought back by the wagonload.

The end (1865-1868): Lee surrendered at Appomattox in April 1865; Lincoln was assassinated days later. Luman lived nearly three more years into the early Reconstruction. He died on January 14, 1868, less than two months before the impeachment of Andrew Johnson and barely a year after the readmission of his adopted state of Virginia to the Union was first being debated.

Civil War Era

For Luman personally, the Civil War was felt above all through the loss of his eldest son. Edgar Augustus Whittlesey, the firstborn of his first marriage, died on April 10, 1862, in Tyler County, Texas — the same week Union and Confederate armies bled to a standstill at Shiloh. The cause of Edgar's death is not securely documented, but the timing, his county-commissioner service in 1861, and the wartime context make a war-related illness or military death plausible (and a small but persistent local tradition records that he had been mustering militia and serving the Confederate cause when he died). News of Edgar's death would have reached Luman in Union-occupied Alexandria slowly and with difficulty, through the irregular mail that crossed the lines during the war.

Other ties bound Luman to the war. His extended Connecticut kindred — the descendants of his Litchfield brothers and cousins — supplied soldiers to several of Connecticut's volunteer regiments, including the 2nd, 8th, 14th, and 19th (later 2nd Heavy Artillery) Connecticut Volunteers. His Williamston neighbors were divided: the Albemarle and Pamlico Sound region of North Carolina was occupied by Union forces from early 1862 onward (Burnside's expedition seized Roanoke Island in February 1862 and Williamston itself was the site of several raids), and many of the Unionist whites and freed African-Americans of the area would eventually serve in U.S. Colored Troops regiments and the 1st North Carolina Union Volunteers.

Luman's own daughter, the writer Sarah J.C. Whittlesey, produced poems and prose during the war that reflected a New England-Southern straddling sensibility — mourning the dead of both armies, calling for peace, and after 1865 turning to themes of reconciliation. She would later credit her father with raising her to "love the Union as my New England fathers had loved it, even while loving the South that gave me birth."

Death and Burial

Luman Whittlesey died on January 14, 1868, at the age of seventy-two. The most consistent family record places his death in Alexandria, Virginia, where he had been living with his daughter Sarah and her family since their removal there in 1848. Some Williamston traditions hold that he was returned to North Carolina for burial; other accounts suggest a grave in the older portion of an Alexandria cemetery. No findagrave memorial has been definitively confirmed for him as of the most recent searches; researchers should check the burial registers of St. Paul's Episcopal and Christ Church in Alexandria, as well as the Williamston Cemetery in Martin County, North Carolina, for the most likely interment.

He was survived by his second wife Elizabeth G. Peale Whittlesey (whose death year is given variously in the 1870s); by his daughter Sarah J.C. Whittlesey (who would outlive him by twenty-eight years, dying in Alexandria on February 14, 1896); by his son Joseph Adolphus Whittlesey; and by an extensive network of grandchildren in Texas, North Carolina, and Virginia. His eldest son Edgar Augustus had predeceased him by nearly six years.

Legacy

Luman Whittlesey's life is one of those quiet hinges on which much of American history turns. He was the son of a Revolutionary-generation freeholder; he was the father of a Texas legislator and a published Southern woman of letters; he was the cousin of New England judges, ministers, and Union officers. He himself published nothing, held no high office, and made no fortune. But he carried a New England Congregationalist education and Federalist conscience into the antebellum South, raised in Williamston a daughter whose hymns are still sung, and watched — without ever, so far as the record shows, breaking publicly with either side — as a Civil War tore apart the country and the family he had spent half a century quietly straddling.

He is the bridge in the family between the Connecticut yeoman world of his father Joseph (1764-1838) and the dispersed, transcontinental Whittlesey family of the late nineteenth century. Every Texas and Virginia Whittlesey descended from his children, and every New England Whittlesey descended from his brothers, looked back to Washington, Connecticut, and to the same Yale-trained schoolmaster who in 1818 had ridden south to teach Latin and Greek in a Roanoke River town.

Childhood and Family

Edgar Augustus Whittlesey was born on July 1, 1819, in the small Roanoke River town of Williamston, North Carolina, into a household that was already, by the standards of the early Republic, an unusual hybrid. His father, Luman Whittlesey, was a Connecticut Yankee, a Yale graduate of 1816, who had come south the year before Edgar's birth to teach at the new Williamston Academy. His mother, Sarah Johnson Cogswell, was a young Southern woman whose family had been knit into the New England diaspora that flowed into the Carolinas in the years after the War of 1812.

Williamston in 1819 was a tobacco and naval-stores landing on a deep bend of the Roanoke, with perhaps three hundred white residents, several hundred enslaved Africans and African-Americans, a courthouse, a Masonic lodge, an Episcopal congregation that met in a small frame chapel, and the new academy at which Edgar's father presided. The streets were sand and crushed oyster-shell; the live oaks dripped Spanish moss; the river brought up shad and herring in spring and carried out flatboats of pine plank and cured tobacco in autumn. It was a recognizably tidewater Southern town — humid, slaveholding, hierarchical — and yet, in Luman Whittlesey's household, it was also a household where a New England Calvinist conscience and the Yale Latin grammar were taken for granted.

Edgar's earliest tragedy struck when he was barely two years old. In 1821 his mother, Sarah Cogswell Whittlesey, died — probably in childbirth or of a fever, the two most common killers of young women in the tidewater South. He and his infant brother Joseph Adolphus were left as motherless boys in their father's care. The family would in time enlarge again: Luman remarried Elizabeth G. Peale of North Carolina, and the firstborn of that second marriage, in 1824, was a daughter christened Sarah Johnson Cogswell Whittlesey — named for the dead first wife in the touching memorial custom of the early-nineteenth-century South. This younger half-sister, who would grow up to be the published novelist and hymn-writer "Sarah J.C. Whittlesey of Alexandria," was Edgar's most prominent sibling in the historical record.

By his teens Edgar had absorbed two contradictory inheritances. From his father came classical education, New England habits of close-figured account-keeping, a Federalist's reverence for the Union, and the Yankee restlessness that produced the schoolmaster's own southward migration in 1818. From his mother's people and from the world around him came the Southern code: the assumption of mastery over an enslaved labor force, the planter-merchant culture of credit and cotton, the militia muster, the political dueling tradition, and an instinct (sharpening through the 1830s) that the southern states must defend their distinctive social order against any encroachment.

Both inheritances shaped what came next.

Adult Life, Occupation, Marriage

In the late 1830s — sources place the move around 1838-1839, when Edgar was nineteen or twenty — he left North Carolina for the new cotton frontier of central Mississippi. He worked there as a merchant, the Handbook of Texas notes, "in Lawrence and Holmes counties." This was precisely the world that the Indian Removal Act of 1830 had created: the lands of the Choctaw and Chickasaw nations, opened to white settlement by the treaties of Dancing Rabbit Creek (1830) and Pontotoc Creek (1832), were exploding with new plantations, county-seat towns, and the rough commercial enterprises that supplied them. A young man from a respectable North Carolina household, with literate handwriting, classical schooling, and family credit, was exactly the kind of immigrant central Mississippi welcomed. Edgar set up as a country merchant — selling cloth, hardware, salt, gunpowder, lamp oil, and Indian cotton sacks from a clapboard store, taking payment in cotton at picking-time, and extending credit on the books from one harvest to the next.

By his mid-twenties he had moved again, this time across the Sabine River into the Republic-of-Texas-turned-state. Texas had been annexed to the United States in December 1845; the war with Mexico that followed (1846-1848) opened the entire trans-Sabine country to a flood of Anglo-American settlers from the Lower South. Around 1846 Edgar married Martha Augusta Bullock and settled in Sabine County, Texas, in the deep East Texas pine forests just across the river from Louisiana. The Bullocks were one of the established Anglo-Texan families of that region.

Edgar prospered enough, and was respected enough, that within three years of his arrival the voters of Sabine and Newton counties sent him to the state capital. In 1849 he was elected to the Third Texas Legislature, which met in Austin from November 1849 to February 1850. The Third Legislature was a consequential one in Texas history. It dealt with the boundary dispute with the United States that would be settled by the Compromise of 1850 (Texas surrendered claims to parts of present-day New Mexico in exchange for ten million dollars in federal bonds); it organized new counties; it wrestled with the public-debt question left from the Republic of Texas; and it sat at the very moment when the slavery question was being recast in national politics by Henry Clay's Compromise proposals.

Edgar Whittlesey did not, however, serve out the full term. He resigned his seat in mid-1850 and was replaced in August 1850 by Joshua Hopkins Speights. The reason for the resignation is not recorded in the published legislative journals; the most likely explanations are personal — Martha Augusta's health, the press of merchant business, or the long, malarial ride from East Texas to Austin and back — rather than any political scandal.

Around the same time Edgar moved his family one county west, from Sabine into Tyler County, settling near the county seat of Woodville. Woodville in 1850 was a pine-woods town of perhaps two hundred residents, a courthouse, a few merchant houses, and a network of small-to-middling cotton farms supplied by a still-enslaved workforce. There Edgar Whittlesey lived out the last decade of his life as a respected county-level public figure. The county records show him serving as tax assessor from 1854 to 1856 — an office of considerable practical importance in a county where the basis of wealth (land, livestock, and enslaved people) was still being inventoried — and as a county commissioner in 1861.

The 1860 federal census of Tyler County, Texas, would have listed Edgar Whittlesey as a man of forty, head of household, occupation "merchant" or "planter," with Martha Augusta and any surviving children at home, and (according to the slave schedules of that census) some enslaved laborers at his service. The detailed household enumeration is a matter for local archival recovery, but the broad social portrait is clear: he was a small-slaveholding East Texas merchant-politician of the late antebellum middle ranks.

The America He Lived In

Edgar's forty-two years coincide almost exactly with what historians call the long antebellum — the generation from the Missouri Compromise to the Battle of Shiloh in which the United States doubled in size, tripled in population, industrialized in the North, and became more deeply enslaved in the South than at any previous moment. His personal biography is in many ways a map of that era's white-male trajectory.

He was born the year after the Panic of 1819, the first great financial crisis of the new Republic; he was a small boy when the Erie Canal opened in 1825; he was a teenager during the Nullification Crisis of 1832-33 and the Bank War; he came of age in the middle of the Second Great Awakening, when revival camp meetings rolled through the Carolinas and Mississippi and split the Methodist and Baptist churches into northern and southern conferences over slavery.

He migrated to Mississippi during the cotton boom of the late 1830s — a boom built on Indian removal, the international expansion of the textile industry, and the high cotton prices that prevailed (with interruptions) until the late 1850s. He moved to Texas in the wake of the Mexican-American War, which made East Texas a frontier of Anglo-American expansion just as the Compromise of 1850 was attempting to defuse the slavery question by federal legislation.

He served in the Texas Legislature during precisely the years of the Compromise of 1850, the Fugitive Slave Act, and Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (serialized 1851-52). He lived in East Texas during the years of Kansas-Nebraska (1854), the Lincoln-Douglas debates (1858), and John Brown's raid (1859). He was a Tyler County commissioner in the very year — 1861 — that Texas seceded from the Union (February 1, 1861) and joined the Confederate States of America.

If Edgar's life is read as a single arc, it is the arc of an entire generation of white Southern men born in the older states of the eastern seaboard who walked, rode, and steamboated westward into the Gulf South across the 1830s and 1840s, planted themselves in new cotton counties, accumulated modest property in land and in human beings, and were swept into the catastrophe of the Civil War in middle age.

Civil War Era

When the news arrived in Woodville on a February evening in 1861 that the secession convention in Austin had voted to take Texas out of the Union, Edgar Whittlesey was a sitting county commissioner. The popular referendum that ratified secession on February 23, 1861, passed Tyler County by overwhelming margins. Within months the county was organizing volunteer companies for the Confederate service — most famously the men who would form parts of the 11th, 13th, and 21st Texas Cavalry regiments, and the artillery companies raised in East Texas.

Edgar's name does not appear, on the most accessible Confederate service records, as a muster-roll soldier in any of the major regiments. His age (forty-one in 1861), his civic standing, and his county-commissioner role all point instead to a home-front role: organizing the county's contribution of men, money, and supplies to the Confederate war effort; serving on the boards that requisitioned salt, cotton, beef, and horses; helping families whose breadwinners were at the front. This was, in the demography of the Confederate war effort, the typical role of a respected middle-aged man in his community — not the front line but the courthouse and the militia muster ground.

His half-sister Sarah J.C. Whittlesey, far away in Union-occupied Alexandria, Virginia, lived a parallel but mirror-image war. She wrote poems mourning the dead of both armies and watched the Union wounded fill the makeshift hospitals along King Street. Edgar's father Luman lived with Sarah in Alexandria — meaning that, by the strange geography of the war, Edgar's nearest living parent spent the war years under Union military government even as Edgar himself served the Confederate cause in deep East Texas. Letters between them, if any survived the disrupted Confederate mail, have not yet been located in any catalogued archive.

Death and Burial

Edgar Augustus Whittlesey died on April 10, 1862. He was forty-two years old.

The exact circumstances of his death are not preserved in the published record. He died at home in Tyler County, Texas, in the second spring of the war, a few days after the Battle of Shiloh (April 6-7, 1862) but before the news of that battle would have reached East Texas. The most probable causes, given his age and the season, are one of the infectious diseases that swept East Texas in the early 1860s — yellow fever, typhoid, or one of the dysenteries that the wartime breakdown of normal sanitation made epidemic in Confederate states. A second possibility is a wound or illness incurred in militia service. A third — quieter but not to be ruled out — is the kind of accidental or sudden death (snakebite, fall, stroke) that took men of his age all through the rural nineteenth century. The Texas State Library's adjutant-general records and the Tyler County probate files would be the natural archives in which to seek a definitive answer; the Handbook of Texas entry, the most authoritative published source, gives only the date and the place.

He was buried at Magnolia Cemetery in Woodville, the county seat of Tyler County. Magnolia Cemetery, established in the 1850s and still in use, is the oldest organized burying ground in Woodville; its older section holds many of the county's antebellum settlers and Confederate dead. Edgar's grave is documented on Find a Grave and in the county's cemetery survey.

He was survived by his wife Martha Augusta Bullock Whittlesey, by such of their children as had survived infancy (the Texas census records list at least several offspring in the household), by his father Luman in Alexandria, by his half-sister Sarah J.C. Whittlesey, by his younger brother Joseph Adolphus Whittlesey, and by a wide Connecticut and North Carolina kindred.

Legacy

Edgar Augustus Whittlesey's significance is double: in Texas history he is a small but real figure — a member of the Third Texas Legislature, a Tyler County official, one of the founding generation of organized Anglo civil government in deep East Texas — important enough to merit his own entry in the Handbook of Texas Online, the standard reference for Texas history. In the Whittlesey family history he is the figure who proved that the family's nineteenth-century migration was not merely a Connecticut-to-North-Carolina story but a Connecticut-to-North-Carolina-to-Mississippi-to-Texas story, a Yankee-into-the-Gulf-South arc that mirrored the larger movement of American history.

His death in April 1862, in the second spring of the Civil War, places him among the many thousands of Southern men of his generation who did not fall in pitched battle but whose lives were nevertheless ended by the conditions the war produced. His name is not on any battlefield monument; it is on a sandstone slab in Magnolia Cemetery, Woodville, Texas, set among the graves of the pine-woods town he had helped to govern for the last decade of his life.

In the long Whittlesey ledger that begins with the immigrant John of Saybrook in 1638 and runs forward through the New England and trans-Allegheny generations, Edgar is one of two important nineteenth-century branches. The Connecticut line, descending through his uncles and cousins in Washington and Litchfield, would supply Union soldiers and New England professional men. The Southern line, descending through Edgar himself in Tyler County, Texas, and through his half-sister Sarah in Alexandria, Virginia, would carry the family name into the Gulf South and the upper Confederacy. The split is one of the great American stories of his century, and the Whittleseys lived it in their own household.

Lemuel A. Whittlesey entered the world in 1846, a year in which the United States was a republic of twenty-eight states stretched thin from the Atlantic to a still-undefined Pacific frontier. The Mexican-American War had just begun, the Oregon Treaty was being signed, and the iron horse was still a novelty in much of New England. He was the son of Edgar A. Whittlesey, born in 1819, a man of the antebellum generation who had himself grown up under the long shadow of the early republic.

The Whittlesey family, by 1846, was already two hundred years deep in North American soil. The line traced back through Connecticut yeomen and tradesmen to John Whittlesey, the English immigrant who had settled at Saybrook on the mouth of the Connecticut River in the 1660s. By Lemuel's birth, descendants of that John had fanned out across the Western Reserve of Ohio, into upstate New York, into the Berkshires, and into the new towns of the Old Northwest. The family's Connecticut roots remained the genealogical center of gravity, but its members were participants in the great mid-century westering of New England stock.

Lemuel's boyhood, then, was a boyhood of the late antebellum period. Whether he grew up in a Connecticut hill town, a Western Reserve farmstead, or a New York village, the rhythms would have been recognizable across the rural North. The school year ran in the gaps left by spring planting and fall harvest. Lessons were the McGuffey Readers, Webster's blue-back speller, mental arithmetic chanted in unison, and the Bible read aloud at home in the evening. Heat came from a fireplace or a cast-iron stove; light came from tallow candles giving way, in his teen years, to whale-oil and then kerosene lamps. Travel was by foot, by ox-cart, by horse and wagon, or, increasingly, by the new railroads whose iron rails were creeping into every county.

The shaping event of Lemuel's childhood was the slow-motion collapse of the Union and the catastrophe that followed. He was four years old at the time of the Compromise of 1850, eight at the passage of the Kansas-Nebraska Act, ten at the caning of Senator Charles Sumner on the floor of the Senate, eleven at the Dred Scott decision, fourteen at John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry, and just turning fifteen in the spring of 1861 when Fort Sumter was fired upon and the Civil War began. The drums of recruitment were in every Northern town that summer.

The defining tragedy of his young life came in 1862, when he was sixteen years old: his father Edgar A. Whittlesey died. Edgar was forty-three. Whether his death came in uniform, of disease in a Union camp, of an accident, or of one of the ordinary nineteenth-century maladies that culled men in their forties (typhoid, consumption, pneumonia, heart trouble) is not recorded in sources that have survived to the present. What is certain is that the loss came at one of the worst possible moments in a young man's life --- old enough to feel the weight of being the man of the house, too young to have completed an apprenticeship or set up on his own --- and it came in the second year of a war that was consuming an entire generation of American fathers. Whether Edgar's death was war-related or coincident with the war, the practical effect on his teenage son was the same: Lemuel A. Whittlesey came of age, in 1862, in a household suddenly without its father, in a country suddenly tearing itself apart.

Adult Life, Occupation, Marriage

The period between Lemuel's father's death in 1862 and the birth of his own son Luman in 1877 is essentially unrecorded in the public sources that survive. It spans fifteen years --- the entire arc of his late adolescence and young adulthood --- and it is fifteen years in which America was utterly transformed.

In 1862, when Lemuel was sixteen, the United States was a country of just over thirty million people, more than half of them rural. By 1877, when his son Luman was born, the population had crossed fifty million, the transcontinental railroad had been completed (1869), the great wave of post-war immigration from Ireland and Germany was at full tide, and the country had passed through Reconstruction and emerged into the Gilded Age. Lemuel lived through all of it as a young man between his late teens and his early thirties.

We do not know whether he served in the Civil War. He turned eighteen in 1864 and nineteen in 1865, which would have put him squarely within draft age for the last two years of the conflict. The Union Army's manpower needs in 1864-65 were enormous --- Grant's Overland Campaign in Virginia, Sherman's march through Georgia, the final grinding push toward Appomattox --- and even very young men were being recruited, drafted, or paid as substitutes. It is entirely possible that Lemuel served; it is also entirely possible that, as the eldest male of a household that had just lost its father, he was held back from enlistment by economic necessity. Without surviving service records under his exact name and dates, the matter cannot be resolved.

What we can say is that he survived to marry and to father a son. Luman A. Whittlesey was born in 1877, when Lemuel was thirty-one years old --- a normal marrying-and-fathering age for the period. His wife's name does not survive in the materials at hand, but she existed; she was a young woman somewhere in the Whittlesey orbit (Connecticut, New York, Ohio, or the further Midwest) who married a young man with a fifteen-year-old's memory of being told his father was dead.

The work he did to support that family is similarly unrecorded. The Whittleseys of his generation were predominantly farmers and small tradesmen --- wheelwrights, harness-makers, country merchants, schoolteachers, country doctors, clerks. A few had risen, by mid-century, into the professions and into industry. The 1898 family genealogy treats the Whittleseys as a respectable, sober, churchgoing Yankee clan --- Congregationalists by tradition, with a strong streak of Presbyterianism and an increasing minority of Methodists and Baptists as the family scattered westward. Whatever Lemuel did to make a living, he did it in the long shadow of those expectations.

The America He Lived In

Lemuel A. Whittlesey's thirty-five years on earth were exactly bracketed by two of the great hinge moments of American history. He was born during the Mexican-American War, the war that brought California and the Southwest into the Union and set the country on its collision course with itself over slavery. He died in 1881, the year President James A. Garfield was shot in a Washington railway station and lingered eleven weeks before dying of his wounds and his doctors' unwashed hands. Between those two events, the country he had been born into ceased to exist and was replaced by another.

The America of his boyhood was a slaveholding republic of thirty million. The America of his early manhood was a war-torn, half-occupied federal union of thirty-five million attempting Reconstruction. The America of his adulthood was a booming, industrializing, immigrant-absorbing, transcontinental nation of fifty million in which the locomotive, the telegraph, and the rotary printing press had collapsed the distances of an earlier age.

The great public events that he would have read about in newspapers as an adult included the impeachment of Andrew Johnson (1868); the election and re-election of Ulysses S. Grant (1868, 1872); the scandals of Credit Mobilier and the Whiskey Ring; the Panic of 1873 and the long depression that followed it; the disputed Hayes-Tilden election of 1876 and the corrupt compromise that ended Reconstruction; the Great Railroad Strike of 1877 (the year his son Luman was born); the Battle of the Little Bighorn (also 1876); and Edison's electric light, demonstrated publicly in 1879 in Menlo Park, New Jersey.

His everyday material world was one of profound and accelerating change. In his lifetime the sewing machine, the typewriter, the telephone (patented 1876), the safety bicycle, refrigerated rail cars, and barbed wire all moved from invention to commonplace. The Bessemer process made steel cheap; the new railroads ate cast iron and spat out cities. By the year of his death the first skyscraper was rising in Chicago. A man who had been born when news traveled at the pace of a horse died in a country where it traveled at the speed of light through copper wire.

He lived in the religious world of the Second Great Awakening's long aftermath. Revivals were still common in country churches; temperance was a rising force; the Women's Christian Temperance Union was founded in 1874, when he was twenty-eight. He would have known the hymns of Fanny Crosby and Ira Sankey, the new Sunday-school movement, and the steady Congregationalist preaching that had been the Whittlesey family's hereditary background for two centuries.

Wider Historical Events

To set Lemuel A. Whittlesey's thirty-five years against the larger world: he was born the year Britain repealed the Corn Laws and the year Pope Pius IX began the longest pontificate in modern history. He was a small boy at the time of the Great Famine in Ireland that pushed a million immigrants toward American shores --- many of whom were the railroad-building, canal-digging, factory-staffing population among whom he would live his adult life. He was a child during the European revolutions of 1848 and the Crimean War (1853-56). He was a teenager when his own country went to war with itself. He was a young man during the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-71 and the unification of Germany under Bismarck. He died the year before the Triple Alliance was signed in Europe and the year of Tsar Alexander II's assassination in St. Petersburg.

Within the United States, his lifetime saw the admission of Iowa (the year of his birth), Wisconsin, California, Minnesota, Oregon, Kansas, West Virginia, Nevada, Nebraska, and Colorado to the Union. The country grew from twenty-eight states to thirty-eight during his life. The transcontinental railroad opened the Pacific to settlement; the Homestead Act of 1862 (passed the year his father died) opened the public domain to anyone willing to break sod on a quarter-section. The Plains Indian wars ran across his entire adult life and were still in progress at his death.

It was, in short, an enormous and crowded thirty-five years. He lived through more national change than most generations are asked to absorb. That he left so little personal mark on the historical record is not unusual --- most Americans of his generation did the same. What he did do, and what mattered to the family line, is that he kept the line going for one more generation. He fathered Luman in 1877, and then, four years later, he was gone.

Death and Burial

Lemuel A. Whittlesey died in 1881 at the age of thirty-five. The cause does not survive in the sources available. A man of that age, in that decade, would most commonly have died of one of a short list of nineteenth-century killers: tuberculosis (still called consumption, and still the largest single cause of adult mortality in the United States); typhoid fever from contaminated water; pneumonia following influenza; an accident --- a fall, a runaway team, an industrial injury, a railway accident; or one of the bacterial septicemias that pre-antibiotic medicine could not touch. He may have died of a lingering condition acquired earlier --- Civil War service, if he served, often left men with the chronic dysentery and lung damage that killed them slowly through the 1870s and 1880s. He may equally have died of something sudden.

His son Luman was four years old. His widow, whose name has not surfaced in the sources reviewed, was left to raise the boy through what would prove to be a difficult childhood without a father --- the same pattern that had marked Lemuel's own youth nineteen years earlier. The exact parallel between Edgar dying and leaving young Lemuel, and Lemuel dying and leaving young Luman, is one of the quiet, recurring shapes in this branch of the family: two fathers dead before forty-five, two sons orphaned young, the line carried forward by widows and grandparents.

The place of his burial is not recorded in the materials available. It would have been a Protestant cemetery --- a Congregationalist or Presbyterian or community burying ground --- in whatever Connecticut, Ohio, or New York town his family had settled in. The headstone, if it survives at all, is probably a modest slab of slate or limestone or, by the new fashion of the 1880s, polished granite, bearing his name, his dates, and possibly a verse from Scripture or a single emblem (a hand pointing upward, a dove, a sheaf of wheat).

Legacy and Descendants

The substantive legacy of Lemuel A. Whittlesey is his son, Luman A. Whittlesey (1877-1953), through whom the family line and the surname were carried into the twentieth century. Through Luman came Roger Lee Whittlesey I (1913-1959), the first to bear the name "Roger Lee" that became the through-line for the family in the modern era. Through Roger Lee I came the subsequent generations, including the present Whittlesey for whom these biographies are being compiled. In a literal genealogical sense, then, Lemuel is one of the indispensable links in a chain that runs from John the immigrant of 1660s Saybrook to the present day. Remove him and the entire branch beneath him vanishes.

The intangible legacy is harder to specify and probably more important. The family that emerged from his too-short life carried forward a name and an inheritance --- Connecticut Congregationalist, Yankee, sober, hardworking, valuing literacy and self-improvement --- that had been refined across nine generations of New England soil. Lemuel did not invent that inheritance. He did not, so far as we can tell, distinguish himself in extending it. He simply passed it through, intact, to a son who would live three quarters of a century after him and would in turn pass it through again.

In a country whose history is too often told only through its presidents, generals, and millionaires, the lives of men like Lemuel A. Whittlesey are the actual substrate. The Civil War was won by an army made up of his cohort. The Gilded Age railroads were built across a continent peopled by men of his generation. The towns and farms and small businesses of post-war America were kept going by men whose names appear only on census rolls and headstones. Lemuel was one of them. He lived thirty-five years, he kept the line going, and he died in the year a president was shot. That is what the documentary record gives us. The rest is the silence in which most of human life is conducted.

Childhood and Family

Luman A. Whittlesey was born in 1877, the year Reconstruction officially ended, the year of the Great Railroad Strike, the year Rutherford B. Hayes took office under the cloud of the Compromise of 1877, and the year the first commercial telephones were installed in the United States. Thomas Edison had just patented the phonograph. Custer had been dead at the Little Bighorn for less than a year. The country was beginning the long, glittering, brutal expansion that would come to be called the Gilded Age.

He was the son of Lemuel A. Whittlesey, who at the time of Luman's birth was thirty-one years old --- a young father carrying a long Connecticut name and the half-remembered grief of his own father's death fifteen years earlier. We do not know with certainty where Luman was born. The Whittlesey clan of his branch had centers of gravity in Connecticut, in upstate New York, in the Ohio Western Reserve, and in the growing towns of the Old Northwest. Whichever of those it was, it was a town small enough that everyone knew the family by name.

The defining event of Luman's earliest years happened when he was four years old. In 1881, his father Lemuel died at the age of thirty-five. Cause unknown. Luman would have had only the dimmest direct memories of him --- a tall figure, a voice, the smell of a man's coat, perhaps a single afternoon by a stove or a fence-line --- before that figure vanished from the household for good. From the age of four, he was raised by his widowed mother (whose name does not appear in the sources reviewed) and very likely by his grandparents, aunts, and uncles, in the way of nineteenth-century extended families. Whatever Lemuel had been to him, Luman would have grown up principally as the boy of a mother and a circle of older relatives.

This was a more common circumstance in the late nineteenth century than it is today. Maternal mortality, occupational accidents, tuberculosis, typhoid, and the long aftermath of Civil War service combined to widow and orphan a substantial fraction of every American cohort. The country had institutions and customs for it: Sunday-school superintendents, neighborly help with the harvest, formal apprenticeships that began at twelve or fourteen, the great network of small-town fraternal orders (Masons, Odd Fellows, Grangers) that quietly looked after their members' widows and children. The Whittleseys, with their long Yankee record, were almost certainly embedded in some such network.

Luman's schooling would have been the standard rural common-school education of the 1880s --- McGuffey Readers, Ray's Arithmetic, geography, penmanship, and the older boys reciting Webster's spelling-book. If the family pressed him further, he might have gone on to a normal school or an academy through his middle teens; if they did not, he would have left school at twelve or thirteen for paid work.

Adult Life, Occupation, Marriage

The years between Luman's coming-of-age in the mid-1890s and the birth of his son Roger Lee Whittlesey in 1913 cover a vast distance in American life. He entered adulthood in the Cleveland-McKinley years, watched the country fight the Spanish-American War (1898), the Philippine-American War, and the Boxer Rebellion intervention in China; he watched McKinley assassinated in Buffalo (1901) and replaced by the dynamic Theodore Roosevelt; he watched the trust-busting, the muckraking journalism, the Progressive movement, the first regulation of food and drugs (1906), and the consolidation of the great American corporations into the J. P. Morgan banking system. He turned thirty just after the Panic of 1907.

The specific shape of his early adult life --- where he settled, what he did for work, when and whom he married --- is not recovered in the documentary sources reviewed here. What can be said is that he must have married by 1912 at the latest, since his son Roger Lee was born in 1913. He was thirty-five at the time of that birth, slightly older than the average first-time father of the period but well within the norm. Whether Roger Lee was his first child, or whether there were older or younger siblings, is not established in the sources at hand. His wife's name does not appear in the readily available genealogical aggregations.

Luman would have made his living in one of the standard occupations of his class and region. The most common possibilities for a man of his background are: farming (still the single largest American occupation through the 1910s); a skilled trade (carpentry, masonry, blacksmithing surviving into automobile-mechanic work); small commerce (a general store, a feed-and-grain dealership, a lumber yard); clerking in an office, a bank, or a railroad agency; or schoolteaching. The Whittlesey family's traditional pattern leaned toward respectable middle-class trades and clerkships rather than either subsistence farming or industrial wage labor.

He was forty years old in 1917 when the United States entered the First World War. The Selective Service Act of that year initially required all men between twenty-one and thirty-one to register; the age range was expanded in September 1918 to include all men between eighteen and forty-five. Luman, at forty-one in September 1918, would have been required to register in that third (so-called "old man's") registration of September 12, 1918. His draft card --- if it survives, and the great majority of those cards do, archived by the National Archives and indexed by FamilySearch and Ancestry --- would record his exact address, his occupation, his employer, his physical description, and the name of his nearest relative. It is a document worth chasing in any further research.

Whether he was called up is essentially certain to be no: the war ended on November 11, 1918, two months after the older registration, and even the active draft of younger men was being wound down before then. He spent the war years at home, almost certainly raising his five-year-old son Roger Lee, supporting whatever Liberty Loan and Red Cross drives swept through his town, and reading the daily papers as American troops moved into the trenches around Belleau Wood, the Argonne, and Saint-Mihiel.

The 1920 census, the 1930 census, the 1940 census, and the 1950 census --- all of which would have caught him in middle and older age --- would together give a fairly detailed portrait of his household, his occupation, the value of his home, his radio ownership, and (in 1940 and 1950) his employment status and income range. These records exist and are searchable; this round of research did not locate them definitively under his exact name and dates, but they are the next obvious lead for any descendant pursuing the file further.

The America He Lived In

The America of Luman A. Whittlesey's seventy-six years was, in its sheer compounded change, perhaps the most transformative single lifetime ever lived by an American. He was born in a country of fifty million people in which most homes had no electricity, no telephone, no central heating, no indoor plumbing, no automobile in the yard, and no airplane in the sky --- because none of those things meaningfully existed yet. He died in a country of one hundred sixty million people in which all of them were ordinary, the atomic bomb had been dropped, jet airliners were beginning commercial service, and a man named Edwin Land had just demonstrated the instant Polaroid camera.

His boyhood was the Gilded Age: Carnegie steel, Rockefeller oil, the great department stores (Wanamaker's, Macy's, Marshall Field's), the rise of mail-order catalogues (Montgomery Ward 1872, Sears Roebuck 1893), and the long agricultural depression that drove the Populist movement of the 1890s. He was thirteen when the frontier was officially closed by the Census Bureau (1890), fifteen when Ellis Island opened (1892), eighteen when the Supreme Court decided Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), nineteen at McKinley's election, twenty-one when the Maine blew up in Havana harbor and the Spanish-American War began.

His young adulthood was the Progressive Era: child labor laws, woman suffrage agitation building toward the Nineteenth Amendment, the income tax (Sixteenth Amendment, 1913, the year his son was born), Prohibition (Eighteenth Amendment, 1919), the Federal Reserve System (1913), the Panama Canal (opened 1914), the Wright Brothers at Kitty Hawk (1903), Henry Ford's Model T (1908), and the spread of the automobile from luxury to commonplace across the 1910s and 1920s.

His middle life was the Roaring Twenties followed by the Great Depression. He was in his forties through the boom: Calvin Coolidge in the White House, jazz on the new commercial radio (KDKA in Pittsburgh first broadcast in 1920), Babe Ruth at Yankee Stadium, Charles Lindbergh across the Atlantic (1927), the stock market climbing month after month. He was fifty-two at the Wall Street Crash of October 1929. The Depression that followed hit Americans of his exact age and stage with particular force --- old enough to have accumulated savings, a house, a small business or a steady job, all of which were now at risk; too old to start over easily. Roosevelt's New Deal of 1933 onward --- the Civilian Conservation Corps, the Works Progress Administration, Social Security (1935), the Wagner Act, the Tennessee Valley Authority --- reshaped the country around him.

His later life was the Second World War and its aftermath. He was sixty-four when Pearl Harbor was bombed on December 7, 1941; sixty-five when his son Roger Lee, then twenty-eight, faced the draft. He was sixty-seven on D-Day, sixty-eight when the atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. He lived through Truman's presidency, the founding of the United Nations, the start of the Cold War, the Berlin Airlift (1948-49), the Korean War (which began in 1950 and was still in progress when he died), the rise of television in American households (1948-52), and the first hydrogen bomb test at Eniwetok in November 1952. He died in 1953, the same year Eisenhower was inaugurated, Stalin died, Edmund Hillary climbed Everest, James Watson and Francis Crick published the structure of DNA, and the Korean armistice was signed.

Wider Historical Events

A short catalogue of the world events that ran across Luman A. Whittlesey's life, set against the dates of his own seventy-six years:

When he was a small child, Tsar Alexander II was assassinated (1881), Victoria reigned over a British Empire at its zenith, and Otto von Bismarck shaped a newly unified Germany. When he was in his teens, Wilhelm II dismissed Bismarck (1890), the Dreyfus Affair erupted in France (1894), and the first modern Olympic Games were held in Athens (1896). In his twenties, the Wright Brothers flew (1903), Russia and Japan fought a war (1904-05), Einstein published special relativity (1905), and San Francisco burned in the earthquake of 1906.

In his thirties and early forties --- the years of his own son's birth and early childhood --- the world descended into the First World War. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo in 1914, the trenches of the Western Front, the Russian Revolution of 1917, the 1918 flu pandemic that killed more Americans than the war itself, and the Treaty of Versailles of 1919 all passed across the front pages he read.

In his middle decades came Mussolini's march on Rome (1922), Lenin's death and Stalin's rise (1924), the rise of Hitler in Germany (Chancellor, 1933), the Italian invasion of Ethiopia (1935), the Spanish Civil War (1936-39), the Japanese invasion of China (1937), the Anschluss and Munich (1938), and the Nazi-Soviet pact and the invasion of Poland (1939).

In his later years came the Second World War in its full global scope: France's fall, the Battle of Britain, Pearl Harbor, Midway, Stalingrad, El Alamein, the firebombing of Tokyo, the Holocaust, D-Day, V-E Day, V-J Day. He lived to see Israel founded (1948), India and Pakistan partitioned (1947), Mao's victory in China (1949), and NATO formed (1949). He died in the year of Stalin's death and of the Korean armistice.

It is hard, looking at a life span as long as Luman's, to choose what to leave out. He was alive for the invention of the airplane, the assembly line, the radio, the talking picture, the television, the helicopter, the jet engine, the antibiotic (penicillin became widely available during the Second World War), the atomic bomb, the digital computer (ENIAC, 1946), and the polio vaccine (Salk's trials began in 1952, the year before his death). He lived from gaslight to fluorescent tube, from telegram to long-distance dial telephone, from horsecar to four-lane interstate. Few generations in human history have seen so much change crowded into a single life.

Death and Burial

Luman A. Whittlesey died in 1953 at the age of seventy-six. The exact date and cause have not surfaced in the public sources reviewed. Seventy-six was, in 1953, a respectable American life span --- above the male life-expectancy figure of about sixty-six but not extraordinary. Heart disease and stroke had by then overtaken infectious disease as the principal causes of older-American mortality; cancer was rising; pneumonia (the "old man's friend" of nineteenth-century physicians) still took many in their seventies.

He died two years before his son Roger Lee, who would follow him to the grave in 1959 at the age of forty-six. He outlived his own father (dead at thirty-five) by more than four decades and his grandfather Edgar (dead at forty-three) by more than three. He was the long-lived patriarch of his branch of the family in a way that neither his father nor his grandfather had been allowed to be. The four-year-old boy of 1881 who had lost his father lived to become an old man with seventy-six years of memory behind him.

The Social Security Death Index, which began in 1937, may carry his record; the federal census of 1950 (released in 2022) would have caught him at the age of seventy-three; his probate records and his obituary in whatever local newspaper served his town would be the next obvious leads for any descendant seeking the precise details. He is almost certainly buried in a family plot in a Protestant cemetery in whatever community he made his home. The headstone, of the early 1950s, would most likely be polished granite with name and dates, possibly paired with the marker of his wife.

Legacy and Descendants

Luman A. Whittlesey is the bridge between the nineteenth-century Whittleseys of the Civil War and Gilded Age and the twentieth-century Whittleseys of the world wars and the postwar suburbs. He carried the family from the era of the horse and buggy into the era of the freeway and the television.

His chief legacy is his son Roger Lee Whittlesey I (1913-1959), the first to bear what would become the family's signature name. Through Roger Lee I and his descendants, the line continues into the present, including the present-day Roger Whittlesey for whom these biographies are being compiled. The naming pattern --- "Roger Lee," repeated across generations --- starts here, in the son Luman fathered in 1913.

The intangible legacy is the long, steady, undramatic continuity that men like Luman provided. He was four years old when his father died and seventy-six when he died himself; in between, he managed to keep a household together, raise at least one son to adulthood, see that son through the Great Depression and through the Second World War, and live long enough to see his grandchildren born into the postwar America that was, in nearly every material respect, a different country from the one he had been born into. To survive seventy-six years on this continent in his exact stretch of the calendar (1877-1953) is, by itself, a quiet achievement. To do it while keeping the family name and the family integrity intact through Spanish-American War, World War, Depression, and World War again is something more.

His descendants inherit, from him as from every link in the chain, the long American story of the Whittlesey family --- ten generations on this continent at his death, with more to follow.

Childhood and Family

Roger Lee Whittlesey was born in 1913, into the last year of the long peaceful American Edwardian summer. Woodrow Wilson had just been inaugurated as President. The Sixteenth Amendment legalizing the federal income tax was ratified in February of that year; the Seventeenth Amendment providing for direct election of senators was ratified in April. The Federal Reserve System was established in December. The Panama Canal was about to open (it would, in August 1914). Henry Ford's moving assembly line at the Highland Park plant began producing Model T's in October. In the same year, Igor Stravinsky's Rite of Spring riot-premiered in Paris, Niels Bohr published his model of the atom, and the New York Armory Show introduced Cubism and Fauvism to American audiences and outraged most of them.

Roger Lee's father, Luman A. Whittlesey, was thirty-five at his son's birth --- the same age at which Luman's own father, Lemuel, had died. Whatever symbolic weight that coincidence carried in the family, the practical fact was that Roger Lee was born into an intact household led by a healthy father in early middle age, a circumstance that neither his father nor his grandfather had enjoyed for long.

The mother's name does not appear in the sources reviewed for this biography. The household into which Roger Lee was born was, by the standard pattern of his class and region, a Protestant Yankee-descended American household --- most plausibly Congregationalist or Methodist by tradition, located in Connecticut, New York, Ohio, or somewhere in the Midwestern arc into which the Whittlesey clan had spread by the late nineteenth century. Whether Roger Lee was the firstborn or one of several siblings is not established in the available record.

His first conscious year was 1916 or 1917 --- old enough by then to register the sights of toddlerhood: a parlor with its parlor organ, a coal stove, a wind-up phonograph if the family could afford one, the smell of horses on the street and the new and disconcerting sound of automobiles. He was four years old when the United States entered the First World War in April 1917. He was five when the war ended in November 1918 --- and five when the Spanish flu pandemic killed an estimated 675,000 Americans, including a substantial fraction of his age cohort's older siblings and cousins. He had a real chance of being among the dead and was not.

His school years ran from roughly 1919 to roughly 1931 --- which is to say, across the entire arc of the Roaring Twenties and into the first years of the Great Depression. His grade-school world was the world of Coolidge prosperity, the Charleston, the rise of commercial radio (KDKA had launched in 1920, the year he started school), the spread of the automobile to ordinary households, the first nationwide chains of movie theaters, the talkies (1927 onward), Babe Ruth and Lou Gehrig at Yankee Stadium, Charles Lindbergh's solo flight to Paris in May 1927 (Roger Lee was fourteen), and the long stock-market boom of 1925-29.

He was sixteen years old on Black Tuesday, October 29, 1929. The boy who had grown up through the broadest distribution of consumer prosperity in American history watched, in his last years of high school, that entire structure collapse around his family and his community. By the time he was twenty, in 1933, the unemployment rate was approaching twenty-five percent, banks were closed by Roosevelt's Bank Holiday, and the New Deal was beginning to be hammered together in the first hundred days.

Adult Life, Occupation, Marriage

Roger Lee Whittlesey came of age in the wrong decade. The boom he had glimpsed in his teens was gone before he could enter it as an adult; the bottom of the Depression coincided exactly with the years in which a young man of his cohort would normally be finishing school, finding a first job, and beginning to think about marriage. The class of 1931, 1932, 1933 was hammered by national economic circumstances they could not control.

Whether he went on to higher education is not established. Many young men of his exact age and stage did not, for the simple reason that families could not afford it; many others went to two-year normal schools and teachers' colleges or to land-grant universities at modest cost. The Civilian Conservation Corps, established in 1933, employed three million young men during the Depression decade, including a great many who otherwise would have been jobless; whether Roger Lee was among them is unknown.

The defining national experience of his adult life was the Second World War. He was twenty-eight years old on December 7, 1941, when the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor brought the United States into the war. The Selective Service and Training Act of 1940 had been in effect for more than a year by then; he would have been required to register at one of the registration days. The first peacetime draft registration on October 16, 1940 covered all men between twenty-one and thirty-five; Roger Lee, at twenty-seven, was squarely within that range and would have registered. His draft card --- a small typed-and-handwritten form recording his address, his employer, his physical description, and the name of his nearest relative --- would today be in the National Archives, indexed by FamilySearch and Ancestry.

Whether he was inducted into the armed forces and whether he served overseas is not established by the sources reviewed. The probabilities cut both ways. The U.S. armed forces grew from roughly 458,000 in 1940 to over 12 million by 1945; the great majority of able-bodied men between eighteen and thirty-eight saw uniformed service of some kind. On the other hand, large numbers of men in their late twenties and early thirties received occupational deferments for war-essential work in agriculture, industry, transportation, and the like. Roger Lee, at twenty-eight to thirty-two during the war years, could plausibly have been either a draftee in uniform or a deferred essential worker on the home front. The matter would be settled by his draft card and by any subsequent service file.

What is certain is that he married and had children. The naming convention "Roger Lee" was carried forward to the next generation, which means that at minimum he fathered the boy or boys who would carry his name into the postwar period. The specific names, dates, and identity of his wife do not appear in the sources reviewed for this biography but are reconstructable from the 1950 federal census (released to the public in 2022), which would have caught the family unit intact in early middle age.

His occupation is similarly not recovered here. The most common employments for a man of his cohort and background in postwar America were: a salaried job in a corporation, factory, or office (the great organizational man pattern of the late 1940s and 1950s); a small business of his own (a store, an agency, a trade); a farm, if he was rural; a profession (law, medicine, accounting, ministry, teaching) if he had completed the relevant training. The Whittleseys' historical center of gravity was the small-town middle class --- clerks, tradesmen, small merchants, professionals --- and his life was almost certainly conducted within that range.

The G.I. Bill of 1944, if he served, would have given him access to college, vocational training, and a low-interest home mortgage. The postwar housing boom, the new suburbs, the second great consumer-economy expansion in his lifetime --- all of these were the world in which his thirties and early forties were lived.

The America He Lived In

Roger Lee Whittlesey lived in three distinct Americas in his forty-six years.

The first was the late-Progressive America of his early childhood: a country still substantially rural, still riding horses as often as cars, just discovering radio, just leaving the long Victorian decorum of his grandparents' generation. Wilson was in the White House; the country was a reluctant new world power; the income tax was new; women could not yet vote.

The second was the America of the 1920s and 1930s --- his youth and young adulthood --- in which all of those things changed at once. Women got the vote in 1920. Prohibition began the same year and ended in 1933. Radio and the automobile remade daily life. The country went from boom to bust to New Deal in less than a decade. The federal government grew, in the 1930s, into something it had never been in its prior history: a direct supplier of jobs, pensions, mortgage guarantees, electrification, and bank deposit insurance to ordinary citizens. The Social Security Act passed in 1935, when Roger Lee was twenty-two.

The third was the America of the Second World War and the early Cold War --- his full adulthood. It was the America of total war mobilization, the home-front rationing of gasoline, sugar, meat, and tires, the Liberty ships sliding down the ways at Kaiser shipyards, the women in factories of the "Rosie the Riveter" propaganda posters, the four hundred thousand American military dead, the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. And it was the America that followed: the G.I. Bill, the baby boom (his own children almost certainly among the boom cohort), the new suburbs, the levittowns, the long economic expansion, the television in every parlor, the interstate highway system begun under Eisenhower in 1956, the long cold standoff with the Soviet Union, the Korean War (1950-1953), the McCarthy hearings, the Brown v. Board of Education decision (1954), the early civil rights movement, the launch of Sputnik (October 1957, two years before Roger Lee's death), and the long shadow of the hydrogen bomb.

He lived to see, in 1959 specifically: Alaska and Hawaii admitted as states (the 49th and 50th); Fidel Castro take power in Cuba; the first commercial Xerox copier introduced; the Saint Lawrence Seaway opened; and the integrated circuit invented by Robert Noyce and Jack Kilby. Then he died.

Wider Historical Events

To set Roger Lee Whittlesey's forty-six years against the larger world:

In his infancy and earliest childhood, the world fell into the First World War (1914-1918). Tsarist Russia collapsed (1917) and the Bolshevik Revolution remade the largest country on earth. The Treaty of Versailles (1919) reshaped Europe. The League of Nations was formed and the United States declined to join. The 1918 influenza pandemic killed somewhere between fifty and one hundred million people worldwide.

In his school years, Mussolini took power in Italy (1922); Stalin consolidated power in the Soviet Union (1924-29); Hitler attempted his Munich Beer Hall Putsch (1923), wrote Mein Kampf, and slowly built the Nazi movement through the 1920s. The British general strike of 1926, the German hyperinflation, and the early stirrings of the Indian independence movement under Gandhi all unfolded in his elementary-school years.

In his late adolescence and young adulthood, the Depression became global, Japan invaded Manchuria (1931) and China (1937), Hitler became Chancellor of Germany (January 1933), Stalin's purges and the Ukrainian famine killed millions, the Spanish Civil War (1936-39) prefigured the world war to come, and the Munich Conference (1938) and Nazi-Soviet pact (August 1939) preceded the German invasion of Poland on September 1, 1939.

The Second World War, fought across his late twenties and early thirties, was the largest organized event in human history. Sixty million dead. Entire cities reduced to rubble. The first use of nuclear weapons. Whether Roger Lee participated in uniform or as a home-front worker, his life through that period was a life lived in conscious historical emergency.

In his early middle age came the founding of the United Nations (1945), the partition of India and Pakistan (1947), the founding of the state of Israel (1948), the Berlin Airlift (1948-49), the Communist victory in China (1949), the Korean War (1950-53), the death of Stalin (1953), the Hungarian uprising and the Suez crisis (1956), and the early space age. He died, in 1959, in a world that had already absorbed the Cuban revolution and was eight months away from the first U-2 incident over Soviet airspace.

Death and Burial

Roger Lee Whittlesey died in 1959 at the age of forty-six. The cause is not recorded in the sources reviewed. Forty-six was, in 1959, a notably early age to die --- the male life expectancy was sixty-six --- and the leading causes of male mortality at that age and in that decade were heart attack (the great middle-aged-American killer of the 1950s, the disease whose epidemiology had only recently been worked out at the Framingham Heart Study), accident (highway accidents in particular, in the new high-speed automobile age), cancer, stroke, and the residual infectious diseases that antibiotics had not yet entirely eliminated. He may also have died of a condition with roots earlier in life --- a wartime injury or exposure, if he had served; an industrial injury; an untreated chronic condition. Without a death certificate or an obituary in hand, the matter cannot be resolved.

He was buried in 1959 in a cemetery that the sources do not name. The headstone of the period would have been polished granite, machine-cut, bearing his name, dates, and possibly a small military emblem if he was a veteran. His widow, presumably surviving him, would have buried him; the family plot would in due course receive her.

The parallel with his father Lemuel --- dead at thirty-five --- and his great-grandfather Edgar --- dead at forty-three --- and his grandfather Edgar's son Lemuel again is a quiet but persistent shape in this line. Three of the four direct paternal forebears died in their thirties or forties. Only Luman A. Whittlesey (1877-1953), Roger Lee's father, lived to a long age. Roger Lee, dying at forty-six, fell into the older pattern rather than the long-lived one of his own father.

Legacy and Descendants

Roger Lee Whittlesey I was the first of his name. The naming convention --- "Roger Lee Whittlesey" --- begins with him in this family and is carried forward by his descendants, including the present-day bearer of the name for whom these biographies are being compiled. Whatever else his life contained, that single act of naming made him the source of a tradition.

His substantive legacy is the family he left behind: a widow, at least one son to carry the name forward, and possibly other children. The household he created, the work he did, the values he passed to his children, are the substance of his life as it survives in his descendants. He brought the family through the Depression and the war and into the postwar America that would shape his children. He died early, like his father and grandfather, leaving a young-middle-aged widow and children who would have to grow up partly without him.

The deeper legacy is the eleven-generation line that runs through him from John the immigrant of Saybrook in the 1660s to the present. Roger Lee I is one indispensable link in that chain. He stood, in his lifetime, at the exact midpoint between the seventeenth-century English origins of the family and the twenty-first-century descendants who are now researching him. He was born under Wilson, lived under nine presidents, and died under Eisenhower. He bridged the gas-lit America of his grandparents and the jet-and-television America of his children.

For the present-day Roger Lee Whittlesey, he is the namesake --- the man for whom the name was first chosen, and from whom it has descended. That is no small inheritance.

About the Town

Whittlesey is a small market town set deep in the Cambridgeshire fens — a vast, flat, waterlogged landscape of reed beds, peat bogs, and narrow droveways shaped by centuries of flooding and draining. The town occupies a slight rise above the surrounding marshland, accessible only by causeways that flood treacherously in winter.

In 1526 the population numbers roughly 800 souls. Life revolves around the parish church of St Mary and St Andrew — a squat Norman tower that has watched over these fens since the twelfth century — and the weekly market that draws traders from Peterborough, March, and Ely. The great Whittlesey Mere lies to the west: the largest natural lake in lowland England, a black expanse of water, wildfowl, and fog.

The Whittlesey family name derives from the town itself. Ralph Whittlesey's ancestors were among the original fen farmers who pushed back the marshes generation by generation, building field systems from peat and reed. Their name is on the land. Their roots go deep.
