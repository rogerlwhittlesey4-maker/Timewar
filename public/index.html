⚜ The Name and Its Origin

The name Whittlesey derives from the town of Whittlesey in Cambridgeshire — from the Old English Wittlesige, meaning Wittel's island. The town sits on a slight rise above the surrounding fenland: an island of dry ground in a sea of peat, reed, and slow black water. To bear this name is to carry the landscape itself in your family identity.

The Whittlesey family was established in Cambridgeshire for generations before written records clearly document individual members. By the 14th century the name was prominent enough to produce the most distinguished bearer in the family's English history — William de Whittlesey, Archbishop of Canterbury.

⚜ Ralph Whittlesey · 1508-1540

Ralph Whittelse appears in 16th-century parish records of Cambridgeshire, with a recorded baptism of March 11, 1508. He lived in the years between William de Whittlesey's death (1374) and the New World emigration of the family line — a period the records describe sparingly. The 1898 Whittelsey genealogy lists him by baptism date alone; his occupation, holdings, and movements are not documented in publicly accessible sources. The spelling of the surname in this era was unstable (Whittelse, Whittelsey, Witsley) and varied even within single documents.

What is documented is that Whittleseys in the early Tudor period were grouped in Colne, Bluntisham, Godmanchester, Pidley, and the surrounding parishes within fifty miles of Whittlesey town — a tight regional clustering suggesting a single extended family with a shared origin in the eponymous fen island.

⚜ Simeon Whittlesey · 1542-1571

Simeon Whittlesea was baptised June 23, 1542 in Cambridgeshire, in the reign of Henry VIII. His generation lived through the religious upheaval of the English Reformation — Henry's break with Rome (1534), Edward VI's Protestant reforms, Mary I's Catholic restoration, and Elizabeth I's settlement of 1559. The fenland parishes were not insulated from these changes.

Simeon appears in the documented Whittlesey ancestor chain as second-great-grandfather of the John Whittlesey who emigrated to Saybrook. He died around 1571, aged about 29 — early adulthood, the cause and circumstances unrecorded. His descendants would carry the name through the next eighty years until one of them crossed the Atlantic.

⚜ John Whittlesea · 1570-1594

John Whittlesea was born in 1570 in Cambridgeshire, the twelfth year of Elizabeth I\'s reign and just two years before the St. Bartholomew\'s Day Massacre in Paris hardened English Protestant identity for a generation. He grew up in a fenland still mostly drained-by-hand, where the wool trade dominated and the New World was a rumour — the Roanoke colony would not be attempted until he was fifteen.

He died in 1594 at twenty-four — young by any measure. The cause is not recorded. Plague visited the eastern counties twice in the 1590s and was the leading killer of young adults; small wounds easily turned septic; the harvests of 1594-97 failed across England and famine spread. He left at least one son, John E. Whittlesey, born in 1593 — meaning the father knew his son for only a year before dying.

⚜ John E. Whittlesey · 1593-1623

John E. Whittlesey was born in 1593 — his father John dead within a year, his world that of late Elizabethan England in the final years before the Queen\'s death in 1603. He came of age under the new Stuart king, James I. He was twelve when the Gunpowder Plot was foiled, eighteen when the King James Bible was published in 1611, twenty-seven when the Mayflower sailed to Plymouth in 1620.

He died in 1623 at age thirty — the same year his son John (who would become the American immigrant) was born. Like his father before him, John E. never knew his son. The pattern of fathers dying young, leaving infants to be raised by extended families, defined Whittlesey men for three generations.

⛵ John Whittelsey · 1623-1704 · The Immigrant

John Whittlesey is the founder of the American line — the single ancestor from whom all American Whittleseys are descended. He was born around 1623 in Cambridgeshire, England (the exact date of April 8, 1623 appears in some sources but is disputed by modern researchers, who note that his marriage in 1664 at the assumed age of 41 would have been unusually late and suggest a birth nearer 1640). He emigrated to New England in the great Puritan migration, with traditional dates of arrival ranging from 1635 to 1650 depending on the source.

What is firmly documented is that by 1662 John Whittlesey was operating a ferry across the Connecticut River at Saybrook under a contract from the town — sharing the operation with William Dudley, who would become his father-in-law. He worked as a tanner and shoemaker and built a homestead near the ferry landing on what is still called Ferry Road in Old Saybrook. In 1664 he married Ruth Dudley, daughter of William and Jane (Lutman) Dudley of Guilford. Their ten children were given Biblical names — Stephen, Ebenezer, Joseph, Josiah, Jabez, David, Eliphalet, Samuel, Ruth, and Sarah — a marker of the Puritan religious culture he had joined.

John Whittlesey died 1704, and was buried at Saybrook. His grave is marked by an obelisk still standing in a fenced enclosure there, restored in 2015 by a Whittlesey family reunion. The first Whittlesey family reunion of his descendants was held in 1855 at the home of his great-great-grandson Capt. Azariah Whittlesey at the same ferry landing, 200 years after John's house was built on the site.

⚔ Eliphalet Whittlesey — Born 1679

Eliphalet Whittlesey was the seventh son of the immigrant John and Ruth (Dudley) Whittlesey, born July 24, 1679 in Saybrook, Connecticut Colony. He grew up in the family ferry house and learned his father's trade as a tanner. As an adult he moved north up the Connecticut River, away from Saybrook, settling in the Wethersfield area where many second-generation Saybrook families spread out as land became available.

On December 1, 1702, at age 23, he married Mary Pratt (born May 24, 1677, also of Saybrook) at Saybrook. The couple eventually settled in Newington, a precinct of Wethersfield in Hartford County. They had at least one son who carried the name — Eliphalet Whittlesey II, born May 10, 1714 in Newington, who would become a Captain in the Colonial Army during the French and Indian Wars. The elder Eliphalet died September 14, 1757 at age 78 in Wethersfield, and is buried in Newington Cemetery (Find A Grave memorial #24326120).

⚔ Capt. Eliphalet Whittlesey II — French and Indian War (1714–1786)

Eliphalet Whittlesey II was born May 10, 1714 in Newington (then a precinct of Wethersfield), Hartford County, Connecticut Colony — son of Eliphalet and Mary (Pratt) Whittlesey. He came of age in the 1730s and married Dorothy Kellogg, with whom he had at least nine children: Martin, Lemuel, John (b. December 23, 1741), Anna, Abner, Eliphalet (III), Asaph, Dorothy, and Elisha.

The defining event of his life was his service as a Captain in the Connecticut Colonial Army during the French and Indian Wars (1754–1763) — the North American theater of the broader Seven Years War between Britain and France over control of the continent. Connecticut raised regiments for the campaigns against the French at Fort Ticonderoga, Crown Point, and the St. Lawrence, and Whittlesey companies served in those provincial forces. He survived the war and lived another twenty years, dying in 1786, three years after the American Revolution ended.

The line of Eliphalet II is the most prolific in the American Whittlesey genealogy — his sons would themselves serve in the Revolution and his descendants spread west into New York, Ohio, and eventually the broader Republic.

⚔ Sgt Martin Whittlesey · 1737-1800

Martin Whittlesey was born in 1737 in Newington, Connecticut Colony, son of Capt. Eliphalet Whittlesey II and Dorothy (Kellogg) Whittlesey. He came of age in the 1750s — the years his father was campaigning in the French and Indian Wars — and was forty when the American Revolution broke out in April 1775. He served in the Continental forces as a Sergeant, earning the rank by age and competence rather than by family connection.

His brother Elisha would become a United States Congressman and orator of the family\'s 1855 reunion at Saybrook; his brother Eliphalet III served as a Revolutionary soldier. Martin\'s record sits between them — a non-commissioned officer in the war that founded the Republic, then a settled farmer and head of household in the early American decades.

He lived into the year 1800 — sixty-three years old, dying in the same year John Adams lost the presidency to Thomas Jefferson. He passed the Whittlesey name and the family\'s Connecticut footing on to his son Joseph, born 1764.

⚔ Joseph Whittlesey · 1764-1838

Joseph Whittlesey was born in 1764 — twelve years before the Declaration of Independence, in colonial Connecticut. His father Sergeant Martin Whittlesey marched out to fight the British when Joseph was eleven; came home a veteran when Joseph was nineteen. Joseph\'s entire childhood ran parallel to the formation of the United States.

He came of age in the new Republic, married, raised children, lived through the War of 1812, Andrew Jackson\'s presidency, the building of the Erie Canal, the early Industrial Revolution. He died in 1838 at seventy-four — long-lived for the era, having seen the nation go from a coastal Atlantic confederation to a continental power.

⚔ Luman Whittlesey · 1795-1868

Luman Whittlesey was born in 1795, during George Washington\'s second term as President. He was seventeen when the War of 1812 began and twenty when it ended at the Battle of New Orleans. His adult life spanned the great American expansion: Lewis and Clark\'s return, the Missouri Compromise, the Trail of Tears, the Mexican-American War, the California Gold Rush.

He lived to see the Civil War and watched his own son Edgar A. Whittlesey die in 1862 during the conflict. He died in 1868 at age seventy-three, three years after the war ended.

⚔ Edgar A. Whittlesey · 1819-1862

Edgar A. Whittlesey was born in 1819 during James Monroe\'s presidency, in the Era of Good Feelings before sectional tensions over slavery fractured the Republic. He grew up in the Jacksonian decades, married, raised at least one son (Lemuel, born 1846), and lived through the abolitionist movement, the Mexican-American War, Bleeding Kansas, and the election of Lincoln in 1860.

He died in 1862, age 43, during the second year of the Civil War. Whether he died in uniform (Union service was overwhelmingly the choice of Connecticut Whittleseys), of war-era disease, or in civilian life during a year when typhoid killed more Americans than rifles, the date is significant. His son Lemuel was sixteen when his father was lost.

⚔ Lemuel A. Whittlesey · 1846-1881

Lemuel A. Whittlesey was born in 1846 during James K. Polk\'s presidency and the Mexican-American War. He was fifteen when the Civil War began at Fort Sumter, sixteen when his father Edgar was lost in 1862. He came of age fatherless during Reconstruction.

He married and had at least one son, Luman A. Whittlesey, born 1877. He died in 1881 at age thirty-five. The Whittlesey pattern of grandfathers raising grandchildren rather than fathers raising sons continued for one more generation.

⚔ Luman A. Whittlesey · 1877-1953

Luman A. Whittlesey was born in 1877 — the year Reconstruction ended. He was the longest-lived Whittlesey in three generations. His life spanned the second Industrial Revolution, the conquest of the American West, the Spanish-American War, electrification, automobiles, the First World War, the Roaring Twenties, the Great Depression, the Second World War, and the early Cold War.

He had at least one son: Roger Lee Whittlesey, born 1913. Choosing the name "Roger Lee" was a deliberate act — homage to the Whittlesey town. He died in 1953 at age seventy-six.

⭐ Roger Lee Whittlesey I · 1913-1959

Roger Lee Whittlesey — the first to bear the name in the American line — was born in 1913, the year before the First World War began. He was sixteen when the stock market crashed in 1929. He was twenty-eight when Pearl Harbour was bombed. Whether he served in the Second World War or in the civilian war economy, the conflict defined his adult life.

He married, raised at least one son (Roger Lee II, born 1945, the year the war ended), and died in 1959 at age forty-six. JFK was elected the following year. He did not live to see the world his son would inherit.

⭐ Roger Lee Whittlesey IV · 1995-Present

The fourth Roger Lee Whittlesey. Descendant of John Whittlesey who came from England around 1648, who was himself a descendant of the Cambridgeshire family that gave England an Archbishop of Canterbury in 1368. The line runs from the black peat and reed beds of the Whittlesey fens, across the Atlantic in a period of English upheaval, and ends — for now — in the high desert of Cochise County, Arizona.

Owner of 4.5 acres off West Dakota Lane, Leslie Canyon Road, north of Douglas, Arizona — 4,500 feet above sea level, under the Chiricahua Mountains, in the dry country that the Spanish once called New Spain and the Apaches called home. The fen farmer's line has crossed an ocean, a continent, and three centuries, and it has arrived at a different kind of flatland with different kinds of water problems, but the same instinct to hold ground and work it.

Whittlesey
Cambridgeshire, England — Spring 1526 AD
About the Town

Whittlesey is a small market town set deep in the Cambridgeshire fens — a vast, flat, waterlogged landscape of reed beds, peat bogs, and narrow droveways shaped by centuries of flooding and draining. The town occupies a slight rise above the surrounding marshland, accessible only by causeways that flood treacherously in winter.

In 1526 the population numbers roughly 800 souls. Life revolves around the parish church of St Mary and St Andrew — a squat Norman tower that has watched over these fens since the twelfth century — and the weekly market that draws traders from Peterborough, March, and Ely. The great Whittlesey Mere lies to the west: the largest natural lake in lowland England, a black expanse of water, wildfowl, and fog.

The Whittlesey family name derives from the town itself. Ralph Whittlesey's ancestors were among the original fen farmers who pushed back the marshes generation by generation, building field systems from peat and reed. Their name is on the land. Their roots go deep.

England in 1526

Henry VIII reigns in the prime of his life — athletic, educated, absolute. Cardinal Thomas Wolsey dominates English government. England remains Roman Catholic; Luther's ideas are known at Oxford and Cambridge but not yet policy. The King's "Great Matter" — his desire to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon — is just beginning to surface. Anne Boleyn has lately caught the King's eye at court. The earthquake that will become the English Reformation is three years away.

England is technically at peace in 1526. Wool exports flourish. The printing press has been in England for fifty years. The world beyond the fens is changing faster than anyone in Whittlesey fully understands.
