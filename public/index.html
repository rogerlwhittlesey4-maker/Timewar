⚜ The Name and Its Origin

The name Whittlesey derives from the town of Whittlesey in Cambridgeshire — from the Old English Wittlesige, meaning Wittel's island. The town sits on a slight rise above the surrounding fenland: an island of dry ground in a sea of peat, reed, and slow black water. To bear this name is to carry the landscape itself in your family identity.

The Whittlesey family was established in Cambridgeshire for generations before written records clearly document individual members. By the 14th century the name was prominent enough to produce the most distinguished bearer in the family's English history — William de Whittlesey, Archbishop of Canterbury.

✝ William de Whittlesey — Archbishop of Canterbury

William de Whittlesey (died January 5, 1374) was born in the town of Whittlesey and rose through the English Church to become Archbishop of Canterbury from 1368 until his death — the highest ecclesiastical office in England, placing him second only to the monarch in the nation's hierarchy of power and prestige.

His career traced the medieval path of clerical advancement: educated at Cambridge, advancing through cathedral chapters and bishoprics, accumulating the administrative experience that the medieval Church demanded of its senior figures. He served as Bishop of Rochester (1362–1364) and Bishop of Worcester (1364–1368) before his appointment to Canterbury. His tenure as Archbishop coincided with the aftermath of the Black Death and the early stirrings of Lollard religious dissent — he condemned Wycliffite ideas and worked to maintain orthodox Catholic authority in England.

That a man from a small fen market town — a town of perhaps a few hundred souls, surrounded by black water and reed beds — should rise to Canterbury is a fact worth sitting with. The Whittlesey name, rooted in that flat wet landscape, reached the highest seat of English Christian authority in the 14th century. Whatever the family became in the generations that followed, that achievement stands.

⚖️ Roger Lee Whittlesey IV — Commander

Roger Lee Whittlesey IV is the fourth generation of his line to bear the name in Whittlesey. His great-grandfather received the name from a wool merchant ancestor who chose it in deliberate homage to the town's most famous son — William de Whittlesey, the Archbishop of Canterbury. The naming tradition is both an honour and a burden: every Roger Lee Whittlesey carries the weight of that 14th-century achievement and the awareness of how far the family has descended from it.

Roger's father died of fever in 1522 when Roger was fourteen. He inherited modest holdings — a farmhouse with a small pasture, rights on the common, and his father's reputation as a reliable wool factor for the Peterborough market. Four years of managing that inheritance alone gave him the calculating mind and the controlled manner that men notice when they first deal with him. He does not raise his voice. He does not need to.

He formed the Defense Band in the spring of 1526 with four men he had known all his life, in response to a raid on a merchant convoy that killed two men he knew personally. The decision was made in three days. The band was assembled in a week. Roger is not impulsive — but once decided, he is immovable.

🌾 Ralph Whittlesey — Sergeant of the Fens

Ralph Whittlesey is not a cousin of Roger by blood — the name is coincidence, or perhaps destiny, in the way that the fens produce their own logic. His father, Thomas Whittlesey, is a fen farmer whose holding borders the eastern edge of the great Mere, and has worked those acres since his own father reclaimed them from the water with six years of ditch-digging and willow staking. The Whittlesey family name on a fen farmer is simply the name of the town on the man who was born there. Half the population might as well be called the same.

Ralph has worked the land since he could walk. He cut peat at seven, drove sheep at ten, and had the physical confidence of a grown man by fourteen — the broad shoulders, the unhurried movements, the sense that nothing in this landscape could surprise him because he had already encountered everything it had to offer. His father wanted him to inherit the farm. Ralph wanted something he could not name. When Roger came to him on a Tuesday morning in April 1526, Ralph said yes before Roger had finished the sentence.

He is the most purely physical member of the band — not stupid, but content to think slowly and act decisively. He holds positions. He does not retreat. He does not embellish reports of what he has seen. When Ralph says something happened, it happened exactly as he describes it, no more and no less. The other men trust this completely.

He carries a carved oak staff his grandfather made — the same grandfather who first pushed back the Mere's edge on their farmland. He touches it the way other men touch a rosary. The land made him. He serves to protect the land. The logic is simple and total.

🪙 Thomas Fenne — The Tanner's Eye

Thomas Fenne is the son of William Fenne, the principal tanner of Whittlesey, whose yard on the eastern edge of town processes the hides from the local cattle trade and sells finished leather to the Peterborough saddlers and Cambridge cobblers. Thomas grew up learning two things simultaneously: the physical work of the tannery and the commercial logic of his father's trade ledgers. Both left marks. His hands are permanently stained from the oak bark pits. His mind moves through transactions the way other men move through physical spaces — instinctively, always knowing where the exits and the opportunities are.

He joined the band because he calculated that a man with a proven sword-arm and a reputation for reliability was better placed in the world than a man who smelled of the tannery for the rest of his life. This is not cynicism — it is honest self-assessment. Thomas respects honesty above almost everything. He is one of the least dishonest men in Cambridgeshire, which is not the same as being honest, but is not nothing either.

🔨 John Cobbes — The Smith's Son

John Cobbes is the blacksmith's eldest son and has swung a hammer since he was nine years old. He is the largest man in the band by a meaningful margin — broad-framed, with the particular kind of physical confidence that comes not from aggression but from having always been the strongest person in any room. He has never needed to prove it. This has made him gentle in a way that surprises people who have not met him before.

He is the moral centre of the band. The others notice this without naming it — they look to John when a decision feels wrong rather than just difficult. He asks whether something is right, not just whether it will work. He carries a carved wooden cross at his belt, a gift from his mother when he left to join the band. He touches it before anything dangerous. He is not superstitious. He is devout. The distinction matters to him.

📜 William Draper — The Seeker

William Draper is the cloth merchant's son who grew up knowing that the world extended beyond the fens. His father trades cloth with factors in Peterborough, Cambridge, and occasionally London — men who talk about Antwerp and Venice and places William has only read about in the books his father occasionally acquires. William reads everything he can find. In 1526 this is not many books, but it is more than anyone else in the band has read, and it shows in the questions he asks.

He joined the band because the alternative was the cloth trade, and the cloth trade would have kept him in the fens for the rest of his life. He is not ungrateful for his origins. He simply intends to see what lies beyond them. He throws himself into situations and works out the details while moving. This occasionally produces inspired results and occasionally produces bruises. The ratio, so far, is acceptable.

📜 The Documented Lineage — Cambridgeshire to Connecticut

What follows are real Whittlesey ancestors documented in parish records, colonial court books, deeds, and the 1898 family genealogy compiled by Charles B. Whittelsey of Hartford. Dates and facts here are drawn from those primary records; where the historical record is thin or contested, that is noted in the entry itself rather than invented.

The line runs from Tudor-era Cambridgeshire down through one immigrant who landed at Saybrook, Connecticut, and outward across colonial New England — farmers, ferrymen, tanners, soldiers, and ministers. Eight generations, traceable.

⚜ Ralph Whittelse — Tudor Cambridgeshire

Ralph Whittelse appears in 16th-century parish records of Cambridgeshire, with a recorded baptism of March 11, 1508. He lived in the years between William de Whittlesey's death (1374) and the New World emigration of the family line — a period when the family appears to have persisted as fenland farmers and minor landholders around the town of Whittlesey itself. The spelling of the surname in this era was unstable (Whittelse, Whittelsey, Witsley) and varied even within single documents.

What is documented is that Whittleseys in the early Tudor period were grouped in Colne, Bluntisham, Godmanchester, Pidley, and the surrounding parishes within fifty miles of Whittlesey town — a tight regional clustering suggesting a single extended family with a shared origin in the eponymous fen island. Ralph is one of the earliest individuals to appear in those records by name.

RegionFenland parishes around Whittlesey town · Isle of Ely

⚜ Simeon Whittlesea — Born 1542

Simeon Whittlesea was baptised June 23, 1542 in Cambridgeshire, in the reign of Henry VIII. His generation lived through the religious upheaval of the English Reformation — Henry's break with Rome (1534), Edward VI's Protestant reforms, Mary I's Catholic restoration, and Elizabeth I's settlement of 1559. The fenland parishes were not insulated from these changes; the parish church and the parish records that bear his baptism were themselves contested ground throughout his lifetime.

Simeon appears in the WikiTree-documented Whittlesey ancestor chain as second-great-grandfather of the John Whittlesey who emigrated to Saybrook. He died around 1571, aged about 29 — early adulthood, the cause and circumstances unrecorded. His descendants would carry the name through the next eighty years until one of them crossed the Atlantic.

⛵ John Whittlesey — The Immigrant (1623–1705)

John Whittlesey is the founder of the American line — the single ancestor from whom all American Whittleseys are descended. He was born around 1623 in Cambridgeshire, England (the exact date of April 8, 1623 appears in some sources but is disputed by modern researchers, who note that his marriage in 1664 at the assumed age of 41 would have been unusually late and suggest a birth nearer 1640). He emigrated to New England in the great Puritan migration, with traditional dates of arrival ranging from 1635 to 1650 depending on the source.

What is firmly documented is that by 1662 John Whittlesey was operating a ferry across the Connecticut River at Saybrook under a contract from the town — sharing the operation with William Dudley, who would become his father-in-law. He worked as a tanner and shoemaker and built a homestead near the ferry landing on what is still called Ferry Road in Old Saybrook. In 1664 he married Ruth Dudley, daughter of William and Jane (Lutman) Dudley of Guilford. Their ten children were given Biblical names — Stephen, Ebenezer, Joseph, Josiah, Jabez, David, Eliphalet, Samuel, Ruth, and Sarah — a marker of the Puritan religious culture he had joined.

John Whittlesey died April 15, 1705, and was buried at Saybrook. His grave is marked by an obelisk still standing in a fenced enclosure there, restored in 2015 by a Whittlesey family reunion. The first Whittlesey family reunion of his descendants was held in 1855 at the home of his great-great-grandson Capt. Azariah Whittlesey at the same ferry landing, 200 years after John's house was built on the site.

⛵ John Whittlesey Jr. — The Ferryman (b. 1665)

John Whittlesey Jr. was the eldest son of the immigrant John Whittlesey and Ruth Dudley, born in Saybrook in 1665 (some sources list 1665, others 1696 — the genealogy has multiple Johns and the records require care to disambiguate). He inherited his father's role as keeper of the Connecticut River ferry at Saybrook, continuing the family's central position in the town's commerce — every traveler going between New London and the lower river towns crossed on a Whittlesey boat.

Around 1693 he built a house at 40 Ferry Road in Old Saybrook. The rear ell of that house, dating to that construction, is one of the oldest surviving buildings in Connecticut. It still stands. In 1984 it was added to the National Register of Historic Places (NRHP reference #84002644). The main block was rebuilt around 1750, but the original 1693 frame remains visible in the rear ell, with its heavy post-and-beam construction and wrought-iron door hardware intact.

⛵ John Whittlesey III — Grandson of the Immigrant

The third John Whittlesey in the American line is the most thinly documented of the three — a common problem for second- and third-generation colonists who left less behind than the founders or the later 18th-century soldiers. He continued the Whittlesey presence in the Saybrook and Wethersfield area of Connecticut during the early-to-mid 18th century, the period when the colony was transitioning from frontier settlement to established society. The name "John Whittlesey" appears repeatedly in Saybrook church and town records of this era, but disambiguating which John in which record corresponds to which generation requires more genealogical work than most sources have done.

What can be said: by his generation, the Whittleseys were no longer recent immigrants but established Connecticut families, marrying into the Pratt, Dudley, Bushnell, and Kellogg lines that interconnected the Saybrook and Wethersfield communities. The family was beginning to disperse from the ferry at Saybrook into the broader colony.

⚔ Eliphalet Whittlesey — Born 1679

Eliphalet Whittlesey was the seventh son of the immigrant John and Ruth (Dudley) Whittlesey, born July 24, 1679 in Saybrook, Connecticut Colony. He grew up in the family ferry house and learned his father's trade as a tanner. As an adult he moved north up the Connecticut River, away from Saybrook, settling in the Wethersfield area where many second-generation Saybrook families spread out as land became available.

On December 1, 1702, at age 23, he married Mary Pratt (born May 24, 1677, also of Saybrook) at Saybrook. The couple eventually settled in Newington, a precinct of Wethersfield in Hartford County. They had at least one son who carried the name — Eliphalet Whittlesey II, born May 10, 1714 in Newington, who would become a Captain in the Colonial Army during the French and Indian Wars. The elder Eliphalet died September 14, 1757 at age 78 in Wethersfield, and is buried in Newington Cemetery (Find A Grave memorial #24326120).

⚔ Capt. Eliphalet Whittlesey II — French & Indian War (1714–1786)

Eliphalet Whittlesey II was born May 10, 1714 in Newington (then a precinct of Wethersfield), Hartford County, Connecticut Colony — son of Eliphalet and Mary (Pratt) Whittlesey. He came of age in the 1730s and married Dorothy Kellogg, with whom he had at least nine children: Martin, Lemuel, John (b. December 23, 1741), Anna, Abner, Eliphalet (III), Asaph, Dorothy, and Elisha.

The defining event of his life was his service as a Captain in the Connecticut Colonial Army during the French and Indian Wars (1754–1763) — the North American theater of the broader Seven Years' War between Britain and France over control of the continent. Connecticut raised regiments for the campaigns against the French at Fort Ticonderoga, Crown Point, and the St. Lawrence, and Whittlesey companies served in those provincial forces. He survived the war and lived another twenty years, dying in 1786, three years after the American Revolution ended.

The line of Eliphalet II is the most prolific in the American Whittlesey genealogy — his sons would themselves serve in the Revolution and his descendants spread west into New York, Ohio, and eventually the broader Republic.

⚔ Martin Whittlesey — Son of the Captain

Martin Whittlesey was a son of Capt. Eliphalet Whittlesey II and Dorothy (Kellogg) Whittlesey of Newington, Connecticut, born in the mid-18th century. Compared to his more prominent siblings — his brother Elisha Whittlesey would become a United States Congressman and orator of the family's 1855 reunion at Saybrook, and his brother Eliphalet III served as a soldier in the American Revolution — Martin's personal record is the thinner. His birth is documented through the genealogy by his relationship to his father, and his name appears in the published descendant lists of the 1898 Whittelsey genealogy, but extended biographical detail has not survived in the publicly accessible sources.

Martin came of age in the 1760s–1770s, the generation that fought the American Revolution. He would have known the upheaval of the war firsthand, whether or not he himself served in the Continental forces. His descendants carried the Whittlesey name forward into the early Republic.

📚 Sources for the Documented Lineage

The bios above draw on:

• Charles B. Whittelsey, Genealogy of the Whittelsey-Whittlesey family (Hartford: privately printed, 1898) — the foundational 19th-century work that attempted to compile all descendants of John Whittlesey of Saybrook. Comprehensive but pre-modern by current genealogical standards.

• WikiTree (wikitree.com) — the collaborative family tree showing Eliphalet Whittlesey (1679–1757), his ancestors, and his descendants with cited Find A Grave and primary sources.

• Wikipedia, "John Whittlesey Jr. House" — the National Register of Historic Places listing for the c.1693 house at 40 Ferry Road, Old Saybrook.

• Pieces & Stems family history blog (piecesandstems.wordpress.com) — modern critical reassessment of the early Whittlesey records, including evidence-based discussion of where the traditional dates may be wrong.

⭐ Roger Lee Whittlesey IV — Present Day

The fourth Roger Lee Whittlesey. Descendant of John Whittlesey who came from England around 1648, who was himself a descendant of the Cambridgeshire family that gave England an Archbishop of Canterbury in 1368. The line runs from the black peat and reed beds of the Whittlesey fens, across the Atlantic in a period of English upheaval, and ends — for now — in the high desert of Cochise County, Arizona.

Owner of 4.5 acres off West Dakota Lane, Leslie Canyon Road, north of Douglas, Arizona — 4,500 feet above sea level, under the Chiricahua Mountains, in the dry country that the Spanish once called New Spain and the Apaches called home. The fen farmer's line has crossed an ocean, a continent, and three centuries, and it has arrived at a different kind of flatland with different kinds of water problems, but the same instinct to hold ground and work it.

Whittlesey
Cambridgeshire, England — Spring 1526 AD
About the Town

Whittlesey is a small market town set deep in the Cambridgeshire fens — a vast, flat, waterlogged landscape of reed beds, peat bogs, and narrow droveways shaped by centuries of flooding and draining. The town occupies a slight rise above the surrounding marshland, accessible only by causeways that flood treacherously in winter.

In 1526 the population numbers roughly 800 souls. Life revolves around the parish church of St Mary and St Andrew — a squat Norman tower that has watched over these fens since the twelfth century — and the weekly market that draws traders from Peterborough, March, and Ely. The great Whittlesey Mere lies to the west: the largest natural lake in lowland England, a black expanse of water, wildfowl, and fog.

The Whittlesey family name derives from the town itself. Ralph Whittlesey's ancestors were among the original fen farmers who pushed back the marshes generation by generation, building field systems from peat and reed. Their name is on the land. Their roots go deep.

England in 1526

Henry VIII reigns in the prime of his life — athletic, educated, absolute. Cardinal Thomas Wolsey dominates English government. England remains Roman Catholic; Luther's ideas are known at Oxford and Cambridge but not yet policy. The King's "Great Matter" — his desire to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon — is just beginning to surface. Anne Boleyn has lately caught the King's eye at court. The earthquake that will become the English Reformation is three years away.

England is technically at peace in 1526. Wool exports flourish. The printing press has been in England for fifty years. The world beyond the fens is changing faster than anyone in Whittlesey fully understands.
